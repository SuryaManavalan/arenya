# Chapter Twenty-Three: Dos Testigos

Nana's porch light was on in broad daylight.

The kitchen table had been cleared to bare wood. Three things sat on it in a row, squared to the edge like a place setting: the red tin, the bowl of grey lime where the dead dime slept, and a yellow No. 2 pencil, sharpened so recently that the shavings still lay beside it.

"*Siéntense,*" Nana said, and didn't sit. "I talked to your tía all night, mija. In my head. She won." She tapped the table beside the pencil without picking it up. "I haven't said yes. I want to hear what you came to ask me, in your words. And I want to know what's warm in your pocket."

Arenya set the napkin on the wood, well away from the pencil.

"A guest check from a diner. Bell's. One stroke in the server box. He was offering to be my second. I said no out loud. It's still warm, and I don't like that I wanted to keep it." She breathed. "What I came to ask isn't *will you sign*. It's smaller and worse. What does signing cost you? Aunty wrote *don't sign*. I think she meant me. I don't think she got to decide for you."

Nana poured a small white hill of salt over the napkin without touching it. "So it stops listening while we eat."

The warmth under Arenya's ribs went out like a pilot light. She was surprised how much she missed it.

The pozole was red and deep, the hominy floating in it like moons. Nana set the little clay bowl of chile de árbol beside Arenya's bowl and looked at her over her glasses. Arenya added the chile.

Nana talked with her back half turned, wiping a counter that was already clean.

"A witness isn't a signature. A signature is *I agree*. A witness is *I was there, and I'll say so again*. The book keeps the one who'll say it again. When my name goes on that line, it goes in with where I live. Who eats at my table. Who sleeps here." The rag stopped. "Then the mail starts. Never a threat. Offers. Every month, a little paper. *Sign here and we close the account. Sign here and the boy is left alone.* Your tía got eleven years of envelopes. Meridian was only the last of them."

She turned around.

"That's what Bell does with a counted hand. He writes to it."

She looked at Kino, not at Arenya. "You gave your note to the water. You're out. I felt the cold go off you like a fever breaking. If I sign, mijo, you go back in, as *who sleeps here*. That's what I sat on every Thursday while Susmithaa drank her tea and didn't push me. So tell me. Not *I'm standing next to you*. Tell me if you'll go back in."

Kino watched the hominy instead of anyone, because that was easier.

"Then let me tell you something first. So you're not deciding on a lie."

He said it in Spanish, low. Arenya caught the shape of it through the wall of the language: *semanas. La Señora. La espalda.* Weeks. The Lady. Her back.

Then in English, so she'd hear it too.

"For weeks the Lady's had her back to me. In the dreams. I climb up, I sit where I always sit, and she's turned the other way. I thought if I trained harder she'd turn around." He breathed out through his nose. "She didn't. I know where she's looking now."

He didn't nod at the chair beside him. He didn't have to. Arenya's face went hot, and she kept it still.

"So yes. I'll go back in. Not because the mountain asked. I spent my whole life waiting to be written down somewhere that mattered, and this isn't that, and I know it." His thumb found the birthmark. "*Who sleeps here.* That's true. I've slept in that back room since I was six. If the envelopes come, they come to both of us. I'll read every one out loud at this table, in daylight, and we'll burn them in the sink. Tía got eleven years of them alone. You won't." He picked up his spoon. "That's my yes. It's mine, not hers. The pencil's still yours."

Nana took off her reading glasses and folded them. Without them her eyes were wet and very plain.

"*La Señora.* Ay, mijo. Six weeks you carried that and ate my food and said nothing."

She put her hand on the back of his head for one second, the way you'd check a child for fever. Then she sat down at her own table for the first time since they'd come in, and opened the tin.

She smoothed the typed sheet flat with the side of her hand. *S. Sowri*, the loops leaning right. Under it, the empty line.

She picked up the pencil and didn't hurry. *Itzel Rosario Martínez.* The lead scratched, the only sound in the kitchen. Then, beside the signature, two small strokes side by side.

"*Dos testigos,*" she said. "*Ella y yo. Ya está.*"

The kitchen did three things at once.

Behind the dresser, the rime on the jamb ticked like a cooling engine, went clear, and ran down to the floor in one thin line.

Under the salt on the napkin, something gave with a soft *tck*, like a pencil lead snapping.

And on Arenya's palm, under the frost *12* and beside the rule, there were two short marks now, grey as graphite, side by side. Her hand was cool. For the first time in days it was just a hand.

The first thing in her throat was a joke, and it was the wrong one. She swallowed it.

Nana set the pencil down with its point away from all of them.

"Now it's cold," she said of the pozole, and her voice cracked down the middle. "Eat anyway. I'm not finished being afraid, mija. I've only stopped being alone."

* * *

When the bowls were empty, they read it aloud. Arenya said each line, Kino said it back, and Nana said it last, her vowels leaning. It was one sheet of cheap bond, typed on a machine with a sticky *e*.

*On the eighth of March I had an archana performed at the Livermore temple in the name of my niece, Arenya. I asked for it. She did not. She was not present and did not know.*

*The priest who read it was not our priest. I noticed too late. If anything was promised in that room in her name, it was promised by my mouth and not hers, and I take it with me.*

*Neither she nor any of my blood is party to any contract, policy, or account opened beneath Colma or elsewhere.*

*Any envelope addressed to the beneficiary is addressed to no one.*

Twice the pilot light flickered blue. That was all. On Arenya's palm the two strokes darkened from pencil-grey to the grey of a pencil pressed hard.

The cream note was addressed to her.

*Ari — I'm sorry I asked for you without asking you. I wanted the god to know your name before anyone else learned it. Someone else was listening. Don't go back into that room to fix my mistake unless you go with people who will say this again.*

Her voice held until the second word.

Nobody repeated that part. It wasn't for the book.

"*Not our priest,*" Nana said softly. "She never told me that." She tapped the line. "Two strokes is a no that stands. On the sixteenth day your account reads *disputada*, and a clerk who never errs doesn't pay out on a dispute. But Livermore is a different book. Whatever that man heard, he heard it in a temple. Our no doesn't reach there."

"Then we don't ask the god anything," Arenya said. "We ask the office. Did she pay by check?"

"Always. She said cash was for people who wanted to forget."

From under the tin's paper lining, Nana drew a thin stack of yellow carbons from the back of a checkbook. PG&E. *Itzel — groceries (she'd argue).* Ravi's birthday. Then:

*No. 2207. Mar 8. $101.00. Pay to: LIVERMORE TEMPLE — archana.* On the memo line, in that leaning hand: *for Ari.*

"A carbon can't tell you who cashed it," Nana said. "The bank has the back."

"An offering gets logged," Kino said. "We ask for the receipt book, not the priest. Nobody gets cagey about a receipt."

Ix lay down across the kitchen doorway with its chin on its paws, and nobody needed to say that it was staying.

"*Mijo,*" Nana said at the door. "Daylight. You come back with all of her."

* * *

At ten past four, at the bottom of the hill with the heater blowing dust, Arenya called Ava.

"Three things, and you can hang up after any of them. I used you. I needed to see what he'd do, and you were the person at the counter. I'm sorry. The man who read your name is real, and I can't promise he's done. And there's a spare bed at a woman's house in Daly City, with soup."

A faucet dripped somewhere behind Ava.

"Yeah. I figured out the first part around the time my hand went numb." A breath. "Can I tell you something weird? Around two I was wiping down the bar, and it just went warm. Like someone let go of my wrist." Quieter: "Was that you?"

*Two o'clock. Nana's pencil.*

It would have been so easy to say yes, and so cheap.

"No. It was somebody else, doing something brave in a kitchen."

"Okay. I'm keeping the salt. I'm keeping the code. I'm not taking the bed, but text me the address. Don't say it on the phone, right? You've got the voice of somebody with rules now. Because the guy, the cardigan, does he—"

At the top of the Altamont, the line clicked into silence. **Call Failed.**

Along the ridge, the white turbines turned so slowly they looked like they were thinking about it. The third from the left turned a hair behind the others, then caught up a little too quickly, the way a person turns back to look after saying they're leaving.

"It's a window, not a door," Kino said, very even. "Look at the road."

She looked at her notebook until the ink held still. "Say it. I used a barista as bait."

His thumb tapped the wheel once.

"No. You used a door, and she was standing in it. That's different, but not by much, and you know it. That's why you called. Bait that's told the truth afterward is a lookout. Don't make it bigger than it is, and don't make it smaller."

She didn't thank him, because she'd have cried, and she was not going to cry on the Altamont Pass.

On the downslope a text came through, stamped eleven minutes earlier and stuck somewhere on the ridge. **okay. salt tonight. kouign-amann if anything.**

The temple rose on its hill above the vines, white tiers of gopuram carved with a thousand small gods and catching the low sun. A sign over rows of wooden cubbies said *FOOTWEAR HERE.* A bell rang once inside, clear across the gravel.

5:14.

"Nothing's listening," Kino said, watching the temple the way he watched trails. "Not yet."

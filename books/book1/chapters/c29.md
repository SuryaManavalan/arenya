# Chapter Twenty-Nine: Can I Help You?

On Mission Street a church marquee read ALL SOULS — NOV 2 — BRING THEIR NAMES.

Arenya read it twice as they passed. Nobody in the truck mentioned it.

Near the county line, the copal on the dash began to smell. Nobody had lit it. Against her ribs the canister gave one slow tick.

"She's clearing her throat," Arenya said.

"Yeah," said Kino. "I heard."

They went up into Hillcrest, where the fog sat low on the stucco as if it had paid for the lot. Halfway up the hill her parents' house was dark, Appa asleep in his chair with his glasses pushed up, and the cottage behind it, Aunty's cottage, was darker.

Nana Itzel was waiting on her porch in a man's cardigan, a clay bowl on her hip. A candle in a jar burned by the step. A line of salt ran white along the foot of the porch, unbroken except for a gap at the far end, by an old glider made up with blankets. Ix sat at the front door facing west, and didn't turn when the trucks pulled in.

Arenya stopped at the white line with the canister in both arms and didn't step over it.

"Buenas noches, Nana. I'm on the street side. I'll stay here until you say where I stand."

Nana looked only at her grandson. "Mijo. Before you close my line, tell me where she's standing tonight. Your Señora."

Kino took the bowl with both hands. It was heavier than salt had any right to be.

"I've been dreaming her with her back to me for weeks," he said. "I thought she'd turned away. I was ashamed, so I didn't tell you." He looked west, down the hill, where Ix was looking. "But I saw it right on your floor. She's facing something. Tonight she's outside your line, Nana. Out there. Holding the door."

Nana came down off the porch and put her hand flat on top of his head, the way you would with a boy of six.

"Ashamed of her back. Ay, mijo. A woman who turns her back on the street is guarding the house." She took her hand away. "Good. Then we don't need to be brave out there. Only careful in here."

She let a pinch of salt fall at Arenya's feet, a small white star on the pavement.

"Now you're invited. Kitchen door. Carry her yourself. Mind the third step." Something not quite a smile. "She tripped on it in 2009 with a whole box of mangoes and didn't drop one."

The third step moved under Arenya's foot. She didn't fall.

Above Nana's sink a shelf ran the width of the window, and her dead were already set out for Monday: a man in a sombrero in a tin frame, a girl in a First Communion dress, a dish of salt, a glass of water. At the end, by the water, a space had been wiped. It was still damp.

"There you go, Aunty," Arenya said. She turned the canister a quarter-turn so it faced the glass. "Window seat. Nobody signs, and nobody receives. You're ours."

On the porch Nana looked Caleb over for one long breath and said, "*Pase*." He went through the gap like a man stepping onto ice. Kino poured the line closed behind his heels, and for a second something Nana had mixed into it glinted grey in the candlelight.

* * *

At the bottom of the hill, a streetlight went out.

It didn't flicker first. A moment later the next one up went out, and then the next. The lamps went dark one at a time, unhurried, coming toward her parents' house.

"Señora," Caleb said, the way he'd been told to. "My scar is warm."

Kino crouched in front of the glider. "Hands on your knees. Don't answer it, not even to say no. Count in Spanish if you have to. Uno, dos. Anything that isn't a tally."

The sixth lamp, the one in front of her parents' house, didn't go out. It dimmed to the color of weak tea and held, as if someone had rested a thumb on it. Under it, with no headlights and no engine, a grey Nissan rolled up the hill on what sounded like nothing but gravity and stopped at the curb. Four old strokes were still smeared on its passenger glass.

Then the kitchen light came on in the cottage behind the house, where Susmithaa had lived for eleven years.

It made a small yellow square with the curtain half drawn. A woman's shape moved past it. Water ran in a sink. And over the fog and the dead lamps a voice came up the hill, mild and a little tired, a voice that had answered a lot of doors.

*"Can I help you?"*

Caleb made a sound like a man who'd been hit in the stomach. "That's what she said. That's exactly how she said it."

"That's her *voice*," Kino said, one hand hard on his shoulder. "A voice is a thing you can pick up off the floor. It's bait wearing her coat. Eyes on me."

At Nana's shelf, under Arenya's palms, the canister went cold. It wasn't the cold of steel. It was the stillness of someone holding her breath behind a door.

"I hear it too, Aunty," Arenya whispered. "It's not you. You're here."

From the bottom step, Malachi listened to the tap. It never changed its note the way a real tap does as a basin fills. One second of water, looping. The shape passed the curtain, then passed it again with exactly the same sway at the shoulder.

"Salt holds what walks," Nana told him, not taking her eyes off the hill. "What talks, salt can't hold. Only your mouth holds against that."

The voice waited for an answer. Nobody gave it one.

Then the voice changed.

It got closer, without getting louder, the way a voice does when someone leans in to a phone. It went softer and warmer, rising at the end, the way you call someone in from another room.

*"Kanna?"*

* * *

It went through her like the Daly City kitchen when she was thirteen. Shoes off at the door, and Aunty's voice from the stove before she'd even said hello, rising at the end, because she already knew it was her.

Arenya's heels lifted a quarter inch off the linoleum. Her mouth opened on the *y* of *yes*.

She closed it so hard her teeth clicked.

She didn't turn around. She put both palms flat on the green steel and bent until her forehead almost touched the lid, and gave the word somewhere to go that wasn't the window. She breathed it into the shelf at a volume nothing outside that kitchen could have heard.

"You never called me from the yard, Aunty. You called from the stove. With your back to me. Because you didn't have to look."

Her eyes were wet. She didn't wipe them. Wiping was a movement, and she wasn't giving the hill a single movement.

Three seconds.

Then, under both her palms, the canister ticked. Two ticks, close together, the second a shade lower than the first, the way a word falls at its end when you say it toward a stove without needing to turn around. Warmth came back into the steel all at once.

Down the hill the cottage light went out without a click. The tap stopped in the middle of its loop.

On the Nissan's glass, something began to draw the fifth stroke, top to bottom, slowly. Halfway down, it stopped. A bead of water ran out of it and blurred it into a smear. With nobody there to answer, the tally couldn't close.

The car rolled backward down the hill. As it passed each dark lamp, the lamp came back on behind it, all the way to the bottom.

"Blue," Caleb said to nobody. "It was blue."

Ix turned from the street at last and looked down the hall at the girl at the end of the shelf. He blinked once, slowly. Then he lay down across the threshold with his back to the street, the way a cat lies down when the watch is over.

*Both voices were copies,* Abernathy said later, when she had a spoon in her hand. *The two ticks weren't. That was the warm one answerin'. It knows the difference, and it picked you.* A longer pause. *And when the fifth stroke started and stopped, something in me went quiet. Like I'd been counted, and the count got dropped. Don't ask me what that means. I'm just not pretendin' it didn't happen.*

* * *

Near midnight a phone buzzed in Malachi's coat. It was Caleb's prepaid, with its battery out and sitting in a separate fold of the lining.

"That's the Shepherd line," Caleb said through the screen. "He only texts when he's already somewhere."

Nobody read it. Nana brought a clay comal from the shed. "Clay keeps its mouth shut." Kino wrapped the pocket in a wet dishtowel, set it on the bottom step under the upturned comal, and ringed it with grey salt. The glow under the clay didn't fade. It just wasn't there anymore.

Sometime in the small hours, Caleb reached into his jacket so slowly that Kino could watch every inch of it, and set two unopened foil packs of Vesper on the porch boards between them.

"They make the scar go quiet. They made me feel like I was doing the right thing, four seconds at a time." He let go of them. "I don't want to feel right. I want to feel it."

Kino said nothing. He noticed, for the first time all night, that the ache in his sternum was gone.

* * *

At dawn, Malachi walked down the hill in his shirtsleeves.

At the curb in front of the Sowri house, everything was wet with fog except a car-sized rectangle of bone-dry asphalt with square corners. There were no tire marks and no drip. Whatever had sat there had sat on the road without putting its weight on it. Beside the passenger door, two leather-soled shoe prints faced the house. A man had stood there all night without once shifting his feet. On the wet top rail of Susmithaa's gate there was one dry right angle, the width of a clipboard's corner, and caught in the lower hinge was a thumbnail curl of pink carbon paper, printed `…CEIVED BY:` above a blank line.

Up at the house Nana lifted the comal. The phone lay face up in the lining with its battery still in its own fold, and its screen lit anyway. One message, stamped 11:14 p.m., from a number that was nothing but zeros.

> LOST PROPERTY — CUSTODY CHAIN INCOMPLETE.
> RECEIPT TO BE COUNTERSIGNED BY NEXT OF KIN.
> SIGNATORY ON FILE: R. SOWRI.
> SUN 11/1. WE WILL COME TO HIM.

It stayed lit long enough for everyone to read it twice, and then went out.

"R. Sowri was on the clipboard Monday," Caleb said. "The top sheet. I thought it was *her*." He looked through the screen at Arenya. "They'd already filed your father."

"Then your father talks today," Nana said. She pressed a twist of grey salt into Arenya's warm palm and closed her fingers over it. "For his door. Pour it yourself before you go in. And tell Raghavan that Itzel Lucero says he can stop being brave alone now. He'll know. He came to my porch in March and stood where the señor is standing, and he wouldn't come in either."

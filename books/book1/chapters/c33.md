# Chapter Thirty-Three: Opened in Error

The runner came up out of the grey as an ordinary man.

Forties. A shaving nick. A clerk's face at the end of a long shift, lanyard turned to its blank side, a clipboard held flat against his chest. Grey dust on his oxfords. He breathed in when the fog rose and out when it settled, and he never blinked in between.

Behind him came Pt. H. Dalal in a white veshti and black socks, one hand gripping a rail that wasn't there. He didn't look for street signs. He looked for the runner's footprints and put each foot inside one, like a blind man on a rope.

Arenya had spent two days wanting to hate him. It was hard to hate a man being towed.

The runner stopped one inch short of the comal's wooden handle.

"Return of personal effects. Proximal eleven. Signatory on file, R. Sowri." He looked down at the comal, and the corner of his mouth moved. "A settlement plate. Somebody in this house knows the forms. But objects don't sign. I need a struck line from the signatory or the alternate."

"Who is holding my glasses?" Dalal's voice was cracked, much older than it had been in the temple kitchen. "I can hear them. They're on wood." Then, softly, in Tamil, to no one: "*I could not see the names this morning. For the first time in thirty years.*"

Amma's hand tightened on Arenya's shoulder blade, and her other hand went toward her pocket. Arenya reached back without turning, found her mother's wrist, and pressed two fingers into it. *Not yet.*

At the curb Malachi touched the brim of his hat.

He let the silence run until the runner's next breath in, and spoke on the out. The R on the entry, he said, wasn't the signatory's hand. It was in grey before anyone in this house had held a pen. "Your own receipt says the chain is incomplete. So I'll ask it the way your office would. When an entry is opened in error by the issuing party, who voids it?"

The runner listened the way a clerk listens to a customer quoting the regulations back at him. He wasn't offended. He was interested, as if very few people had ever got this far.

"The R is in his hand. Shaky, but his. The ink is the office's."

"His hand, your ink," Malachi said. "Then the error is yours, not his. That's the whole of the category. Say what it costs before you strike it."

For the first time, the runner blinked.

"An error isn't erased. It's returned. The balance goes back up the line to whoever issued the ink. That's somebody's bad week. It isn't yours." He turned the clipboard a few degrees. "But I don't strike alone. A void needs a witness who keeps names, so nobody can say later that the clerk made it up. That's him. And he can't keep what he can't see. So the glasses go back on, and he reads. Everything facing him gets read. True names and the other kind." The mouth again. "That's the price of a clean void. Not a signature. *Being seen.* Turn your back and he reads your back, which is nothing."

Inside the blue jar the mist went white all at once.

"Nobody faces him," Arenya said, in the clipped voice she got when she was scared. "Nobody from this house."

"Not Malachi," said Malachi. He took off his hat. "I'll be what he faces. My name's mine to spend."

She opened her mouth to argue and found she had nothing. It was the trade she'd have made herself, if she'd been braver.

But she could ask one thing. "Before you strike. The nine-twelve bus. Who's it booked for?"

"For me," said the runner. "Me and what I carry back up the line. The balance rides on my lap, not on any seat of yours."

Arenya set the jar on the comal's wooden handle, lifted the lid with Nana's spoon, and let the mist pour out low across the salt.

Then she turned her back.

So did Amma. So did Appa and Nana in the doorframe, and Caleb on the glider with his face to the siding, and Kino at the bottom of the hill. Abernathy turned so fast he flickered, and when that didn't feel like enough he went into the pantry and put his face an inch from a shelf of Maseca. At the Daly City BART bay, Ava, who had been told to stay at the bottom, filled her head with oat milk on purpose.

Only Malachi stayed facing the street.

* * *

Socks on wet sidewalk. A hand finding wood. Glass touching glass.

The old man's eyes didn't stop on Malachi's face. They went through it like a finger running down a ledger, years at a time, line by line, until they stopped.

"Forty seconds," Dalal said, very quietly. "A market. Heat. You were the one he was looking at." He blinked behind the lenses. "Someone else wore glasses that day, sir. Not mine. You have been on a page since then, a page that is not ours. Now you are on mine as well." A swallow. "I am sorry. It is a true name. I will keep it properly."

Malachi held still and let himself be read. He had decided on the walk to the curb that he wouldn't flinch, and he didn't.

Dalal's gaze lifted past him to the house, and read it from its windows and its number. *Lucero*, he mouthed, the way you memorize an address. Then his eyes went to the kitchen window and stayed there a beat too long, with a small frown, the frown of a man who finds a page in his own book that is blank and shouldn't be.

"Read," said Dalal.

The runner took a pencil from behind his ear and drew one long, unhurried stroke, the length of a line on a form.

"Proximal eleven. Opened in error. Issuer's ink. Returned up the line. Closed." A second, shorter stroke. "Alternate struck with it."

* * *

Under the pestle on Nana's counter, the family's copy of the form went *tk*.

Amma's hand came out of her pocket empty. From the couch Appa made a small sound, and Arenya turned her head without meaning to and saw the grey going out of his thumb like a tide going out, until there was only a crescent left under the nail.

"Cool," said Caleb, to the siding. "Scar went cool."

Then the fog stopped breathing.

Arenya didn't see it. She had her back to the street. What she felt was the whole hill going quiet, the way a room does when a refrigerator you hadn't noticed shuts off.

She read the rest afterward in Ava's small block capitals on a legal pad. *8:48. Fog stopped mid-rise, held one count. Whole bank lifted, Westlake to ocean, higher than fog goes. Something under it turned over. Like a sleeper turning from a light. Then lay down again farther out.*

Ava had underlined *turned over* twice.

"Good morning," said the runner, as though it had been one all along, and went downhill toward the nine-twelve. Dalal followed in his steps with his glasses on. Passing Kino's turned back, he said to nobody, "The box was the other man's. I only keep the names."

Then the fog took them. Church bells started somewhere toward Colma. Arenya didn't turn around until they'd rung four times.

* * *

She was on her knees by the couch with Appa's thumb pressed to her forehead when Malachi came in with his hat in his hand.

"He has your name now," she said. "I'm sorry."

"It was spent on purpose. Don't apologize for the price." He stopped in front of Appa. "Mr. Sowri. In March my side's paper put its mark on you. I'm sorry for it, and I'm saying so to your face."

Appa didn't say it was all right.

Caleb came in last, in his socks, and stopped on the mat. "The runner struck an R," he said, steady, and that cost him more than cracking would have. "I helped kill the woman that R was written for. I know it doesn't close anything. I just want it said once, here, in front of you."

Appa looked at the crescent under his nail. Then at the man on the mat, holding the plate Amma had given him anyway.

"Not on the floor," Appa said. He used the voice he kept for bad report cards and the second mortgage, and he pushed out the chair beside him with his foot. "She would not have let you eat on the floor. Sit at the table."

It wasn't forgiveness. It was a place to sit, and in her family that had always come first.

Caleb sat.

At 9:20 Ava reached the top of the hill with salt in her shoes and a tray of coffees from the BART cart. Nana took the tray, looked her over from shoes to face, and said, "Siéntate, doctora."

Somewhere a bus was climbing a hill that wasn't theirs, with a clerk on it and a balance in his lap, riding up the line to the man who had issued the ink. *Somebody's bad week.*

Arenya thought: good.

* * *

Later, when the windows had steamed over, there was a circle on the inside of the kitchen glass, wiped clean, a palm wide. It was at the eye height of someone sitting down.

Kino pulled a chair square to it, laid salt along the sill from corner to corner, and put one small hill on the linoleum by the pantry door, the kind of thing you leave on a step so someone knows you saw them. Arenya sat three feet behind him and watched the window in the dark oven door, where everything showed reversed.

At 10:21 the clock over the stove stopped. Eleven seconds, then the tick came back.

Out past the rooftops, the fog bank lifted and settled, lifted and settled. She counted the gap without meaning to. Eleven seconds. On the third breath it didn't come all the way down, and a long low line stayed above the water a moment too long, the way a back stays above the surface when something rolls.

In the oven door a smudge appeared at the center of the circle, on the *outside* of the glass, about the height of a forehead, as if someone had leaned in to look. She could see the step the whole time. There was no one on it.

A fingertip drew on the glass from outside, slowly, through the beads. One flat stroke for a waterline. Then a low hump rising out of it.

Then it stopped, unfinished, like the fifth stroke on the Nissan's window. The beads ran and the drawing ran with them.

When the hour was up and Kino turned his chair, the salt on the sill was unbroken. The little hill by the pantry was gone. It hadn't been scattered. It had been picked up, the way you pick up something left on a step for you.

"It's not hunting us," Kino said. "It's showing us something, and it can't finish."

Arenya opened the notebook. *10:21. Clock stopped 11 sec. Waterline. Hump. Unfinished.*

*That drawing, guv,* said Abernathy at her knee, very quietly. *The waterline and the hump. I've seen that stroke before, and I couldn't tell you where. It's not a thought. It's a flinch.*

She wrote that down too.

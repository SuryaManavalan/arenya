# Chapter Twenty-Eight: Blue, Chipped at the Rim

With the lights off, Tartine looked like a different animal. Chairs stood upside down on the tables, legs in the air. Ava let them in through the alley door, which was propped with a sack of flour, and didn't say hello.

The back room had a mop sink, four milk crates, and a camera pointed at the walk-in. Malachi leaned on the walk-in door. Ava sat on a crate with her left hand palm up on her knee, as if it might report something.

Caleb Strand had the crate farthest from the door, his back to the wall and his jacket zipped to the throat. A duffel stood between his boots, and through the canvas something small and dry was ticking.

When Arenya stepped over the threshold, the ticking skipped once. Then it went on.

"You're the daughter," Caleb said.

"Arenya. Not *the daughter*." She took the crate nearest the door. Kino took the one beside it, with a jar of salt between his boots and the lid loose. "Nobody signs, nobody swears, nobody says a name that isn't theirs. If my palm does something, I'll say so out loud and you stop. Start with Monday. Where she was and what she was holding."

He pressed his shaking hands flat on his knees.

"Daly City, a little before three in the morning. She couldn't sleep. She was at the sink with her back to the window, holding a mug. Blue, chipped at the rim."

On *blue, chipped at the rim*, her palm gave one slow pulse. It wasn't a warning. It felt like something leaning closer to listen.

"There was a sticker on the bottom," he said. "Half peeled. A cartoon bird. I saw it when it fell."

Arenya wrote *cartoon bird, half-peeled*, and her handwriting was worse than she'd have liked.

"That sticker was mine," she said. "From college. She borrowed that mug every visit." She breathed. "Go on. Don't skip what happened to her."

His voice went flat, the old way. He let it, because it was the only way he was going to get through.

"Dorsey and Pruitt held the door. I went to the kitchen. She turned around and saw me and said, *Can I help you?* Like I'd come to the wrong house." His jaw worked. "You don't touch anyone for a lift. You reach for the astral, it comes loose like a coat off a hook, and the Crown does the pulling. She knew, then. She looked past me, out the window toward the city, and said something soft, the way you'd call someone in from another room. Two sounds, maybe. The body went down. The mug dropped. Dorsey said *clean*. The Crown said *yield confirmed*."

He stopped.

"And I felt good. Fourteen months of nothing, and for four seconds I felt like a man who'd done his job. I'll never get that part out." He pushed his hands under his thighs to stop them shaking. "Then I folded her tether back on itself, so nothing could follow it. In training they call that a knot. It ran to you, and nobody ordered me to fold it. It's the only thing I chose."

On *tether*, her palm went hot for one breath, sharp as a stove grate, and settled.

"Okay," Arenya said. It came out small, and she let it. "I'm not forgiving you. I want that on the page next to the bird. But you said *felt good* when you didn't have to." She lifted her eyes. "The name. Exactly as she said it. If you don't have it, say so."

He opened his mouth and there was nothing there.

"I don't have it. I'd be making it up." He looked at his hand as if it might know. "But the Crown heard it. Right after *yield confirmed* there was a tag. *Proximal reference logged.*" His voice cracked too loud for the small room. "I knotted the line, and the machine wrote down where it went anyway."

Arenya underlined *logged* until the nib nearly went through the paper.

"Aunty used to call me *kanna*," she said. "That's two sounds. Or it's Appa, or Amma. I'm not deciding that in a bakery storeroom."

She turned the page and used the voice she kept for evidence. "Shepherd. Drop Two. And why a man who was told to close a file carried the file out in a duffel."

Shepherd, Caleb said, was a voice through the Crown, level and polite with no accent. He said *lost property* when something went missing. Drop Two was an industrial lot on the Peninsula with a roll-up door, where a white van took the canisters and a man whose lanyard was always turned backward signed for them. There'd been four men on the team, all callsigns: Caleb was One. Dorsey had a lazy left eye he hid under a cap. Pruitt chewed the inside of his cheek. "And Four was Hale. Not the Hale at your notary. That's a name he rents. The real one's neck turns wrong, all at once, like a camera on a mount."

"The ash was supposed to go in the van," he said. "In the car it was ticking in my lap, and I could feel her in it. Not a ghost. What she'd left behind. I couldn't hand her to a man with a clipboard, so I ran." He looked at Arenya. "It's hers. It was always hers. I'm only the one who carried it."

* * *

On *it's hers*, her palm didn't pulse or burn. It went still, warm and attentive, the way a room goes quiet when someone you love walks into it. Under Caleb's hand the duffel ticked, and ticked again a full beat later. For the first time that night it matched her exactly.

"Mine's stopped," Ava said, staring at her own hand. "It always ticks with that thing. It's not following me now."

On the concrete, the grains along one side of Kino's open jar had crept toward the duffel in a thin crescent, like iron filings near a magnet. He put the lid on slowly.

"Salt leans like that toward something that's *owed*," he said quietly. "That's not hunger. Your call. My hand's on the jar."

Caleb took his hand off the bag and stopped holding on.

Arenya knelt on the cold, bleach-smelling concrete. She didn't unzip anything. She laid her left hand flat on the canvas where the ticking lived.

"Hi, Aunty." She gave one stupid wet laugh, because the alternative was the other thing. "You hated my driving. So you got a guy with two tours and a duffel bag. That's an upgrade, I guess." Her thumb moved on the canvas. "I'm not taking you as a delivery. Nobody's signing for you. I'm just here."

The tick slowed, and her pulse slowed with it, although she hadn't asked it to. For the space of one breath the room smelled of filter coffee boiled too long and the cold cream Aunty kept by her sink.

Then it was bleach again. The ticking didn't stop so much as settle, like a kettle lifted off the flame. Her palm felt exactly as if a hand were resting on the back of hers, the way you'd stop a child fidgeting in temple.

*Love,* said Abernathy, with the accent worn thin as a coin. *Don't move your hand. There's somebody sat on the bench with us. Warm. You know 'er. I don't think she's clocked me, and I'd as soon keep it that way. Don't ask me why.*

"It's gone," Ava said. She opened and closed her left hand under the tube light as if she weren't sure she was allowed to have it back. "The cold. All of it."

Caleb had put his hands over his face.

"You carried her," Arenya told him from her knees. "That goes in the notebook too. It doesn't cancel the kitchen, and it doesn't have to." She didn't look away from him. "Malachi. Whatever your jaw's been thinking since *roll-up door*, say it. I'm done with people knowing things in rooms I'm standing in."

Malachi crouched across the duffel so their eyes were level.

"Eleven years ago, in a port city I won't name, a customs clerk sat across from a man in a café and stopped breathing forty seconds later. Eyes open. Three days after, we traced the man's car to an industrial lot. Roll-up door. A white van at night, and a man with a clipboard signing for something you could carry in one hand. I wrote it up. I was told the clerk had an aneurysm and the file no longer existed, and I did what I was told." He held her eyes. "And I'm not with the Department of Energy. That's a card in my wallet. The people who put a voice in his head may sign my paychecks too. I'll give you the name of who I work for once saying it won't get anyone in this room killed. I'm telling you where the line is so you never catch me standing behind it."

"Where the line is," Arenya said. "I'm writing it down as that. Not as a lie."

* * *

Where the ash would sleep took them four minutes. Not her apartment, not her parents'. Kino called Nana and asked in Spanish, without writing anything down, and came back with her answer. *Yes* to Aunty. Caleb on the porch, outside the salt. *Come by Mission Street, not the freeway.* The cat had been sitting at her door for an hour, looking west.

Arenya lifted the duffel by both handles, the way you'd lift a sleeping kid.

"Nobody signs. Nobody receives. I'm moving her. That's the whole sentence."

Outside, under the sodium light, her palm went cool by a quarter-degree.

At the 18th Street end of the alley a grey Nissan sat at the curb with the engine off and nobody in the driver's seat. Its passenger window was fogged evenly from the inside, edge to edge. Malachi walked past it at an ordinary pace and read the plate without slowing: 7KBQ4R2. From the mirror hung a lanyard, badge turned backward, showing only the white plastic back of the card.

"Wrong," Caleb said, very low. It was the one word they'd given him. "That's not a team car. And my hand just went for the dose. I'm putting it in my jacket and leaving it there."

Then the fog on the glass moved.

From inside, slowly, a fingertip drew a short vertical line through the white. Then a second. A third. A fourth, evenly spaced, like a ruled ledger.

There were four strokes, and four of them were in the alley.

The fingertip paused over the glass, as if considering a fifth.

"Don't count them," Arenya said. "Don't answer it." She turned her back on the car. "Through the kitchen. Now."

They went out the front onto Guerrero at a fast, ordinary pace, and nobody looked toward 18th. In Kino's truck she buckled the belt under the duffel, not over it.

*A fifth?* she asked into the bond.

*Four's the tally of who it's got,* said Abernathy. The cockney had gone out of him entirely. *The fifth's the one it hasn't pinned. It's wondering if that's you or me. Don't give it the number.*

They took Mission south, slowly, past taquerias pulling down their shutters. The canister lay quiet against her chest, warm as a sleeping child.

The fifth stroke didn't come. Or it came where none of them was looking.

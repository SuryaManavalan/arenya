# Chapter Three: Things You Can’t Explain

Arenya was keeping a second notebook now. The real one.

It lived in her bag, dog-eared and stained with coffee, full of hand-written interviews and scraps of overheard conversations. Most of it looked like the scrawls of someone either on the edge of brilliance or burnout: looping arrows, fragments of quotes, strange timestamps, maps of the city with certain cafés and sidewalks circled over and over.

By the end of the week, the pattern had sharpened.

Every person who’d died had frozen first. Not fainted. Not stumbled. Frozen. Witnesses described it the same way, again and again:

“He just stopped moving.”

“She looked like she saw a ghost.”

“It was like… her face turned off.”

Some said the victims looked confused. Others said terrified. One had tears running silently down her face before she collapsed. All of them were frozen for at least thirty seconds, some as long as a full minute.

And then—gone.

No known connection. Different neighborhoods. Different backgrounds. But the freeze, the look, and the sudden death… that was repeating.

She was starting to dream about it. Not the deaths, exactly—just the silence before. The moment of stillness. The uncanny pause. It clung to her like a scent she couldn’t wash off.

On Sunday, she put the notebook away and changed into something her mom would call “nice but not too modern.” It was time for her cousin’s engagement dinner—another installment in the long-running family series “When Is It Your Turn, Arenya?”

The house in Daly City smelled like cardamom and nostalgia. Everyone was already there when she arrived: aunties in gold-trimmed saris, uncles crowding around a cricket match on a laptop, and cousins who looked like they’d stepped off a wedding invitation. Laughter burst out of the kitchen like steam from a pressure cooker.

She was barely in the door before her mother appeared, holding out a glass of mango juice and a question.

“So how’s the writing? Any new dog articles?”

Arenya smiled, tight-lipped. “Actually, I’m working on something new. An investigation.”

This got the attention of at least three aunties nearby.

“Oh?” her uncle Ravi asked, half-turning from the cricket match.

“It’s about a string of sudden deaths in the city,” she said, louder than necessary. “All in the past month. Random people, healthy. But they froze before they collapsed. Witnesses say it was like something stopped them in place. I think there’s a pattern. I’ve been interviewing families, building a timeline.”

The room quieted just a little.

Her cousin’s fiancée cleared her throat. “That sounds… intense.”

“It is,” Arenya replied, maybe a little too proudly. “I think it’s worth looking into.”

“Well,” her mother said carefully, “as long as it’s not too dangerous.”

“It’s not dangerous,” she lied. “It’s just research.”

Nobody pushed further after that, and for once, she wasn’t being asked about marriage or mutual funds or her five-year plan. She felt the smallest flicker of satisfaction.

Later, after dinner and after the mandatory slideshow of the engagement photos, Susmithaa Aunty found her outside on the back patio, away from the crowd. She wore her usual flowing scarf and silver rings, her hair braided with tiny charms. Most people in the family described her vaguely as “international” and “doing some kind of work in Peru last year, maybe?”

She gave Arenya a hug that lasted just long enough to feel like something more than polite.

“I heard what you said inside,” she murmured.

“Don’t worry,” Arenya said, “I’m not putting myself in danger. It’s just interviews. Witnesses. Nothing dramatic.”

Susmithaa didn’t smile.

“I know you don’t believe in the things I believe in,” she said softly, “but listen to me, kanna. There are things happening in this world that are not meant to be uncovered. Doors that are closed for a reason. You’ve always had a sharp mind. But sometimes, sharp things cut the wrong way.”

Arenya frowned. “I’m just trying to tell the truth.”

“The truth is never just,” her aunt said. “These deaths you’re chasing—they’re not just medical. Something old is stirring. Something that hasn’t touched this city in a long, long time. And if you keep following the thread, it may follow you back.”

A cold gust of wind brushed against them, and for the first time, Arenya noticed how serious her aunt’s face was. Not dramatic. Not whimsical. Grim.

“Do you know something?” Arenya asked. “About the people who died?”

“I know enough to stay away,” Susmithaa said. Then, softer: “And enough to tell you this: if you see something that doesn’t make sense—something that makes your soul tighten—turn away. Some stories don’t want to be written. Some truths only bring ruin.”

There was a long pause.

Then her aunt smiled—warm and weary—and pulled her into another hug. “But if you must go chasing shadows, go with all the light you can carry. And be careful who sees you.”

She left with a rustle of scarves and sandalwood.

Arenya stood alone for a moment, unsure if what she’d just heard was a warning, a blessing, or both.

Later that night, back in her apartment, she stared at the wall of notes and names and string. The wind howled outside again. She thought of Yuri’s frozen stare. Of the others. Of the notebook still in her bag.

She wasn’t ready to turn away.

Not yet.

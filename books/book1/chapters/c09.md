# Chapter Nine: Don’t Flinch

The Monolith campus looked exactly how she imagined it would: like a spaceship had crash-landed in Silicon Valley and decided to gentrify the entire block.

White curves. Reflective glass. Trees that were probably engineered for emotional wellness. The company’s logo—a stark black circle intersected by a single vertical line—stood like an ancient monolith (naturally) in the middle of the courtyard. Employees flowed in and out of the front atrium in sleek, controlled waves, all of them badged, focused, and glowing with too much sleep data.

Arenya adjusted her blazer for the fifth time as she approached the entrance.

She was surprised Brooke had helped. More surprised by how casually she’d done it.

“Tell them you’re not there to rewrite the overdose story,” Brooke had said. “You’re there to ask a better question.”

Then, as she turned away, she added something Arenya hadn’t expected:

“Most people think credibility gets handed to you. It doesn’t. You walk in like you already have it, and people give you room. Don’t flinch.”

Arenya had been too stunned to reply. But the words stuck.

Now, as she passed through the glass doors and approached the front desk, she kept repeating them in her head.

Don’t flinch. Don’t flinch. Don’t flinch.

The chief of security’s name was Elias Khatri. She knew that much. Brooke had set up a “conversation” with him—whatever that meant. The email had been polite, but with just enough bureaucratic vagueness to suggest this might be a courtesy more than a real opportunity.

The receptionist directed her to a waiting room just past the main lobby. Clean lines. Soft walls. A screen displayed looping footage of satellites being launched and robotic arms assembling modular rovers.

She sat with her knees pressed together, notebook in hand, heart thudding.

This was it. Her first real chance to break the wall.

She didn’t just need access—she needed trust. Enough to speak to someone close to Adonis. Enough to maybe, maybe, reach Typhus Quinn himself. If she could get words from him, her story would have real credibility.

Elias arrived five minutes later, tall and calm, with silver at his temples and the kind of quiet authority that didn’t need announcing. His suit was pressed. His eyes were tired.

“Ms. Sowri?” he said.

She stood, shook his hand. “Thank you for making the time.”

“I don’t usually meet with press,” he said, motioning toward a private conference room. “But Brooke said you weren’t writing a standard piece.”

“I’m not,” she said quickly. “I’m not interested in rehashing an overdose narrative. I don’t think that’s what happened. I’ve been following a pattern of deaths across the city—quiet, scattered, but strange. And Adonis Quinn fits that pattern. Not in his lifestyle. In the details.”

Elias didn’t blink. “You believe his death wasn’t an accident?”

“I believe it wasn’t random.”

She opened her notebook, flipped to the page with her compiled list: the names, dates, descriptions of the “freezing” incidents. She laid it out calmly, precisely—no speculation, just the evidence.

He listened without interrupting.

When she finished, there was a long pause.

Then: “This doesn’t go beyond me. Not yet.”

“I understand.”

“Whatever happened to Adonis,” he said slowly, “the family is grieving. There’s a lot of attention right now. I don’t want to feed it.”

“I’m not here to sensationalize,” she said. “I’m here because I think this matters. And because—frankly—I think there’s more going on than anyone wants to admit.”

He folded his hands. “You want to speak with Typhus.”

She nodded.

“That won’t be easy.”

“I didn’t expect it to be.”

He studied her for a moment.

Then said: “I’ll consider passing your name along. That’s all I can promise.”

Arenya nodded, heartbeat loud in her ears.

He reached into his jacket and pulled out a card—cool silver, with sharp corners and an almost imperceptible gleam. It looked more like a blade than stationery.

Titanium.

One side was etched with the Monolith insignia. The other held only a name, a single contact address, and no phone number.

E. Khatri

ek@monolith.systems

“If you hear from us,” he said, holding the card between two fingers, “it won’t be on your terms.”

She took it, the weight of the metal surprising. Cold, smooth, final.

“We’ll be in touch,” he said.

And just like that, the door opened behind him, and she was dismissed.

Arenya stepped back out into the glassy brightness of the Monolith lobby, card in hand, wondering whether she’d just been invited forward—or warned to stop.

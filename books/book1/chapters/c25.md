# Chapter Twenty-Five: Per Family

Sri Ganesh Tiffin took up half a strip-mall unit between a vape shop and a tax preparer. The smell came over the counter, browned ghee and curry leaf and mustard seed popping, and it hit Arenya like a kitchen in Daly City on a Sunday. She almost turned around to see who was behind her.

"Rule," she said, mouth half full of dosa. "Twenty minutes. No names, no numbers, nobody writes anything down. How's the rectangle? Don't say fine. One to ten."

Kino poured his coffee from tumbler to dabarah and back, long pours from high up, the way any aunty would.

"Three. Like a tooth that went numb at the dentist." He tapped two fingers under his collarbone. "Okay. Four. It's getting a vote."

The counter man refilled his sambar without being asked. "Eat, eat." Something in Kino's face went off duty.

"When I was nine," he said, "Nana had me sit on the mountain all night by myself. No flashlight. She said if I was meant for it, something would come sit with me. Nothing came. Fog. A skunk. In the morning I told her a deer had, because I wanted it to be true. I've never told anybody that." He looked straight at her. "You held your mouth shut against your mother's voice today. How are you? Not the palm. You."

She looked at the dosa, because it was easier.

"Honest version. A few weeks ago the worst thing in my life was a listicle about hamsters living their best life. I used to lie in bed and think, if something real ever happens, I'll be the one who notices." She tore a piece she hadn't meant to tear. "And something real happened, and the first thing it did was write my name in a book next to my dead aunt's. And the part I can't say to Ava or to Amma is that some small, disgusting part of me is *glad*. Finally, a real story. I've got ink on my palm that isn't mine, and part of me is taking notes."

Under her left hand, the notebook settled. It wasn't cold, just a little heavier than paper should be, the way a cat settles when it hears its name. It lasted exactly as long as she meant the words.

She didn't look down, and she didn't look at Kino either, which was how she knew he'd seen.

"Glad's not a sin," he said. "I've been glad my whole life that something might finally happen to me. That's the same thing, Ari, and I'm the one who got trained for it. Taking notes is how you stay a person in a room that wants you to be a name. Just don't let it sign anything."

She laughed once, wetly, and wiped her eye with the back of her wrist.

"Also, for the record, a deer came. You were nine, and you sat on a mountain all night in the fog, and you *stayed*. That's the deer. Nana knew it was a lie and let you keep it, because it was the true kind."

He went very still.

"A deer came," he said to the tumbler. "Okay. Don't tell Nana. She'll take credit."

* * *

The fog had beaten them to Daly City. It was a quarter to eight, and Appa's car wasn't in the driveway. Behind the house, Aunty's cottage sat dark, its curtains drawn the way she'd left them.

Amma opened the door before Arenya could knock.

"You drove to *Livermore*," she said, which was how she said hello. To Kino: "Sit. There's rice."

On the table lay a manila folder labeled **SUSI — ACCOUNTS** in Amma's capitals. One of her hands rested flat on it. With the other she wiped a counter that was already clean.

"Appa went for washers. For the tap. At twelve-thirty. Seven hours, for washers." The rag went round. "So. Eat. Then you tell me why my sister's temple called this house tonight asking about a check."

Arenya ate one bite of rice, because refusing Amma's rice was a declaration of war, and put both hands flat on the table.

"Check 2207. A hundred and one dollars, taken by a man who wasn't the temple's priest. Someone endorsed it. I'm not accusing you. I want to know whose name is on the back." Quieter: "And you said *my sister's temple*. Not ours. Why?"

At *2207*, the wiping hand stopped mid-circle. Then Amma folded the rag into a neat square, which was what she did when she'd decided something.

"Because she chose it. Ours is four streets away, and everybody's aunty is at ours. She drove one hour so nobody from Daly City would watch her ask God for something." Her eyes were dry and furious. "She was my sister. You don't get to ask me why with that face."

She opened the folder.

The March statement had the check images printed small at the bottom. The front of 2207 was in Aunty's careful hand: *for Ari.* Then the back.

**R. Sowri.** Ruler-straight strokes, every letter built from right angles, the last *i* closing on a small bracket-shaped hook. Beneath it, in the same hand: *Pay to the order of H. Dalal.*

Arenya's notebook held half of that hook. She'd have known it anywhere.

"That is not your Appa's signature," Amma said. "Your Appa signs like an engineer. Small, leaning left, lifts his pen like it costs money. So I asked him, the night after the funeral, and your father, who cannot lie to the gas man about the meter, said, *Leave it, Lakshmi. It's handled.*" Her voice caught, and she made it uncatch. "Thirty-four years. First time I didn't believe him. So I hid it. From him. Not from you."

Kino wasn't looking at the folder. He was looking past the kitchen at the garage door, where a pair of work shoes stood toes-out on the mat.

"Those weren't there when we came in."

"Washers was true. He left in his good shoes. *Those* were on the rack in the garage." Amma's mouth flattened. "I was home all afternoon. I didn't hear him come back."

Kino's fingers went to his wrist. "Something's ticking. Too even for a clock."

"The gas meter," Amma said. "By the garage door. Your Appa reads it every Sunday and writes it in a little book. It shouldn't turn. The stove is off."

At the word *meter*, Kino's breath went out of him. "It stopped. In the wall, and in here." He touched his sternum. "It was ticking in here too."

In the puja alcove, the brass lamp leaned, very slightly, toward the hall.

Arenya laid a short line of Nana's salt across the kitchen doorway. Kino laid his behind it, and his grains lay a little crooked. Amma sat with her hands folded over the rag, her lips moving in a language that wasn't English. Between verses she said, "Three keys. Mine. His. And your aunty's, on the red string. After the funeral I looked in her purse. It wasn't there."

Arenya went to the window over the sink.

Fog stood in the side yard like milk. The grey gas meter on its pipe by the garage door had its glass face frosted white, from the *inside*. A hairline of the same white ran from its base across the concrete to the garage door handle, straight as a ruler.

The same ruler that had been laid to Nana's side door.

Wedged between the meter and its pipe was Appa's meter book, a small green ledger, blown open. Her father wrote small and very neat, leaning left. Today's date. *3:12 p.m.* A reading. And under it, three words pressed so hard they'd torn the paper.

*Ari does not sign.*

"He wasn't writing about a meter," she said. Her voice only wobbled on the last word.

The frost cleared from the top down, one dial at a time, as if someone on the other side were wiping it.

"Two. Two. Zero. Seven."

Appa's Sunday reading in the green book said *4471*. The dials didn't say that now. They said the number of Aunty's check.

The smallest needle gave one tiny tick, like a single pencil stroke. Against her hip, in the bag, the notebook ticked back and went heavier. Just slightly. Just enough.

Kino's breath caught. "Another quarter behind. Four now. Still mine." He swallowed. "Somebody's reading us the receipt."

She took her hand off the bag like it was a burner and folded her arms to keep them out of trouble.

"A receipt needs somebody to take it. Nobody takes it."

They waited it out, Amma's verses against the hum of the rice cooker. The needle didn't tick again. One at a time, without a sound, the dials slid backward to *4471*. On the concrete, the ruled line broke in the middle like a snapped thread, and the fog took both halves.

Nobody had accepted the receipt, so the meter took it back.

The notebook didn't give its weight back. It stayed one stroke heavier.

Then the front door. A key, the rattle of the chain Amma never used, a plastic bag.

"Lakshmi? The washers were the wrong size, so I got both—"

Appa stopped in the kitchen doorway, on the far side of two lines of salt. A slight grey man in a cardigan who had always looked faintly apologetic. He looked at the salt, at Kino, at the open folder.

Something left his shoulders, and it wasn't relief.

Wound twice around the first finger of his right hand hung a brass key on a faded red string.

"So," Raghavan Sowri said quietly. "She showed you."

"Don't step over it, Appa. Not yet." Her voice went very flat, so it wouldn't do anything else. "That's Aunty's key. There's a page in your meter book, in your writing, from three-twelve this afternoon, when you were supposedly at the hardware store. Who's H. Dalal? And please don't say *it's handled*."

He set the bag down on his side of the salt, carefully, as if it held something fragile. It held washers.

"In order," he said.

"One. I don't know who H. Dalal is. In March a man telephoned. He said he was from the Livermore temple. Very polite. American. He said *Sowri-garu* correctly, which nobody does. *Your sister has offered dakshina for the visiting pandit. Does the family approve?* I was at my desk with the cricket on, and I said—" His mouth worked. "*Whatever my sister wants is fine.* That was all. Then the check went to him, *per family*. I was the family. I said yes for her without knowing what I was saying yes to.

"Two. Rodel, from the notary. Twenty years ago his mother was dying at Seton, and Susmithaa sat with her nine nights when he couldn't get off his shift. After this funeral he telephoned me himself. *Mr. Sowri, they used my number. You must hold her key. The side door is the one they'll come to.* So I took it from her purse at the hospital, and I lied to your mother.

"Three. Rodel said if they come, the meter shows it first, because it counts. Write the family's no where the counting thing reads, in your own hand, and press hard. So at three o'clock I came in the side with her key. The needles were already wrong. I wrote it. I put my shoes toes-out by the door, the way my mother did, so whatever came would walk away. Then I drove around and bought the wrong washers, because I did not know what else a man does."

He held the key out over the salt at arm's length and didn't let his fingers cross the line.

"I'm not good at this. Your aunty was. Take it, or tell me where to put it."

"Amma first," Arenya said. "It's her sister's key." Then, softer: "You didn't sign, Appa. You said yes to a phone call. That's exactly the kind of mistake it's built to collect."

Amma got up. She reached across the salt the way you'd take a hot dish from someone and took the key off his finger, string and all. She carried it to the alcove and laid it at the foot of the lamp, on the little brass plate where the ghee drips, so the red string coiled in the light.

The flame dipped once. Then it stood tall.

"There. Now the lamp has it. Not you. Not her." She turned. "Raghavan. Step over. Wash your hands. Eat."

He stepped over both lines the way he stepped over garden hoses. At the sink, with the water running, he said to Kino, "He never said his name. Perfect, but flat. Every syllable the same weight, like reading off a card. Nobody's mouth does that with our names. I noticed and did nothing."

He dried his hands on Amma's rag, which nobody was ever allowed to do. Amma let him.

"I asked Rodel how he knew about the meter. He said, *The front door of a Hindu house has a lamp. The side door has a meter. Things that count come in where things are counted.*" He hung the rag up. "Then he said something I didn't understand. *He asked me for one-twelve before it was empty. I should have known then.*"

Arenya's spoon stopped in the air.

"Hale rented Box 112 on March nineteenth. But somebody asked Rodel for it before that, and wrote it in a temple register before it was even rented." She looked up. "Hale was only the name on the form. Appa, did Rodel say who?"

"Only *he*. The way you say it about the weather."

"Then tomorrow. Daylight, before seven-forty, when the box closes. Rodel's own register on his own counter. Not alone."

"I'll drive you," said Appa. "I won't go in. I'll sit in the car and read the meter on the flower shop."

It might have been a joke. In thirty years nobody had ever been sure when Appa was joking.

"Eat," said Amma. "All of you."

They ate. Kino's heart ran a quarter-beat behind where it should, and he didn't mention it again. On Arenya's palm, under the *12*, Nana's two strokes lay cool and dark.

In the alcove the red string lay coiled at the foot of the lamp, and the flame didn't lean toward anyone.

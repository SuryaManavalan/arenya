# Chapter Thirty-Five: The Only Witness

The bulk over Colma was a city of the dead in lamplight. Every grave was a lit window, every row a street. The fog was a real sea down here, black and cold, and the heron stood knee-deep in it at the foot of the stair. Far out past the ridge, something the size of a coastline lifted its head.

Her hands were full. In the right, light: lamp-oil and dawn and her aunt's laugh, warm as a hand on her back. In the left, dark: fog and soil and patience. Neither asked permission.

Abernathy was already on the first step, waving both arms like a man flagging a bus. "Eyes front, love! Don't you look at the lady."

The white coin swung to him. The heron bowed, a short dip of the beak, the nod a clerk gives a man he has filed before, and made a sound like a turn signal. *Tick.* For one frame Abernathy was solid, in a coat that wasn't his.

She didn't think. If she thought she'd start footnoting.

Left hand first: two fingers at the long grey neck, a thumbprint of night under the white eye. Then the right. Susmithaa laughing at someone out of frame. Amma sleeping after Visakhapatnam. She threw all of it down the row, past Abernathy's ear and over the back of the spotted cat that was Kino here, into the open throat.

"You don't get her," she said, and her voice came out too loud and very much hers. "She's *borrowed*."

The heron screamed like a tide going out too fast over stones. Then it bent and drank the black water, and the stair tilted toward the sea, and a lamp no bigger than a cupped hand came loose from Ernesto's window and began to slide down the steps. A brass flame, small and bright.

* * *

She never could say afterward how long it lasted.

The undertow dragged the cat down the stair until he fetched up against stone with the lamp beside his paw. It went through Abernathy like wind through a coat on a hook, and the lamp stopped against his shin the way a dog stops against the one person at a party it knows. Gold ran along his edges: a high collar, brass buttons, a servant's livery from a house older than the country.

On the hill her body stood with its eyes on nothing, and the foam-hands went for her wrist. The jerk came like a hand on her collar. She planted her feet on the top step and leaned the other way, into the dark hand, the patient one, the one that had held this land since before anyone drew a county line on it. *I'm not leaving this hill until she's off it.*

She sent one thin thought down through the bulk to a man with coffee on his sleeve. *Wrist. Cuff. Off.*

Abernathy flicked a coin of gold across the lit city. It landed warm in her palm. "Don't waste it on a speech."

She spent it on the bird's eye. The white coin split like a dinner plate. Seawater ran down the grey face, and the heron jerked back, hurt now the way animals get hurt, honestly, with nothing filed about it.

It inhaled. The whole sea went out with it, and far past the ridge the coastline-sized thing breathed *with* it. The brass lamp lifted off the step.

She put her free hand out under it, palm up, not touching. Abernathy leaned his whole self into the nothing in front of the flame, and the nothing held. *Stay. You're with the Sowri, and the Sowri stay.*

Up on the real grass, she learned later, Nana threw a fistful of salt over her granddaughter's frozen wrist instead of into her husband's grave. *Los vivos primero.*

The leash went slack. Abernathy walked backwards up the stair saying *follow the coat*, and the lamp followed him like a duckling and settled into her open hand. It weighed nothing. It was warm as a cup of tea someone else had made for her. And it laughed, short and delighted, at something out of frame.

The heron saw it go into her hand. It looked at her, not through her, and it wasn't a clerk's look. It was a thing doing a sum and not liking the answer.

Then the cat went up the grey neck like night up a wall. *Esta no es tu agua.* The sound the bird made was a breakwater giving way.

* * *

It was nine steps from the sea and broken, lowering itself into the black water. Her right hand was already up. The grey back was half turned. That would be clean, and she'd never find out what it was.

She lowered the hand.

"You don't kill the only witness," she said, mostly to herself, and came down two steps until the cracked eye couldn't pretend she wasn't there.

"You count things. So take this to whoever does your filing. Susmithaa Sowri is an open entry. Two people stood for her tonight, her mother and me. She's borrowed, at Ernesto Lucero's feet, until the water's clean, and when it's clean I bring her down to it. Not you." Her throat hurt. "If your master wants to talk about the balance, it can come and ask. Properly. Nana leaves salt for company."

She laid a thin bright outline across its back, to watch it home.

The heron shook the way a wet bird shakes, and the light came off it in drops. But the drops lay on the black water and didn't go out, and the film they made began to move. Not west toward the open ocean. North and east, around the shoulder of the hill, toward the Bay. Oil on a current running the wrong way, toward the place where the city's lights ended and old industrial dark began.

The heron looked at her once more and bowed. *Tick.* It walked into the sea, and the sea closed without a ripple.

* * *

She came back into her body like stepping off a bus that hadn't quite stopped.

Ava's scarf was wrapped around her wrist. Caleb's palms were on her arm, and there was coffee down her sleeve. Every candle on the hill burned straight up. She crawled the three feet to Amma and laid a palm between her mother's shoulder blades.

"It's over. She's warm, and she's staying."

*The lamp's laughing,* Abernathy said. *Tell your mum.* And then, plainer: *It bowed to me. Twice. And I didn't put the coat on. It was just there, and it fit.*

She told her mother only the laugh.

"Yes," Amma said, lifting her cheek out of the marigolds. "She always laughed at the wrong time."

Appa took off his glasses and wiped them on his shirt. His left hand came out of his pocket. In the candlelight the stain on his palm had shrunk to a crescent no wider than a fingernail paring.

* * *

Low tide was at 7:31, and Kino knew it the way other people knew their own phone numbers. He stood up in the middle of a sentence, holding his left forearm away from his body like something hot. "She wants me at the water."

Nobody voted.

The lot at Candlestick had one working light out of six. Past the riprap the Bay had pulled back a quarter mile, but it wasn't going *out*. It was sliding sideways, one long sheet, northeast toward the cranes at Hunters Point, as though someone up there had a hand on the tablecloth. Gulls stood on the mud and faced that way.

Out on the flat lay a heaped grey-green thing the size of a parked bus, both arms braced in the mud. Every eleven seconds it lost an inch.

"It's not pulling," Arenya said. Her wrist under the scarf had gone the waxy white of a candle. "It's holding. I'm not fighting anything tonight. I'm going to say hello with manners."

On the other side the mud was lit from underneath, the blue of a phone screen in a dark room, and the cranes were ledger-pens, tall and black, nibs down. The woman on the flat was the idea of one, from before the shipyards: oyster shell, eelgrass hair, the soft grey clay that used to be the whole south shore. Dozens of lines ran out of her. Not rope. *Straight* water, ruled flat like lines on a page, hooked into her and drawn tight toward the pens.

She opened one eye, a tidepool with a crab in it.

*"Land's child. Good boy. And the one who sent my brother home with manners. He came back bowing. I haven't seen a bow in a hundred years."* The eye found Abernathy and stayed a beat too long. *"And a filed man, without his ledger."*

"Ma'am. Arenya Sowri." She stopped a polite distance up the stair and held up the candle-white wrist so nothing was hidden. "What's pulling you? Names, a place, what they want. If you can't say it, give me a corner."

Kino didn't wait. He went down onto the mud and set his back against the nearest line like he was bracing a door. *Aquí, abuela.* For half a second it bowed. Then it *read* him. It turned smooth as a page and laid itself across his forearm, and a thin straight stroke appeared on the lit skin.

*"Off, child. They like a living hand."* And then, because Arenya had asked properly and nobody had in a century: *"Not a name. They don't give names, they take them. But a place. Under the dry dock where the ships used to lie down, there's a stone house that grinds. A mill. My rivers go in at the top and come out at the bottom as nothing. The pens are stakes. Iron, in the real mud, on your side. Men put them in at low water. Pull a stake and a line comes loose. Pull enough and I can stand up."*

The eye went to Abernathy, gentler. *"That hand on the lines is older than the yard, filed man. Somebody tore your page on purpose."*

His hat came down over his face.

A second stroke began on Kino's arm. "Oi! Page-turner!" Abernathy slapped a thumbnail of lamplight across it, and the line *paused*, the way a clerk's pen hovers over a name he knows. The nib hung toward Abernathy, considering. Arenya grabbed Kino's good wrist and hauled.

* * *

In the car Ava laid a ruler beside Kino's forearm. One stroke, straight and clean, not bleeding. Four centimeters exactly.

Two hundred yards up Harney, a box truck sat dark with its engine running. At 7:31 two men got out in hi-vis vests with no logo and grey steel-toe boots, and walked down the riprap like people who'd done it in the dark before. One drove a survey rod into the mud left of the second piling. Every phone in the car flickered. Kino's arm gave one hard tick, like a pen pressing through to the next page.

Ava got the plate. *8ZK-41.* Under it, stenciled small: **D2-07**.

On the way back one of them came over and stopped a polite six feet from Ava's window. Forty, clean-shaven, a Safeway-manager face.

"Evening, folks. This lot's port property after dusk." His eyes went once to Kino's sleeve. "And keep that arm warm, son. Tide's cold tonight."

He tapped the roof twice, like a stamp, and walked away.

"He knew about the arm before he saw it," Arenya said. Her voice shook, and she let it. "He's not security. He's a clerk."

"D2 is Drop Two," Caleb said to the windshield. "It's a fleet number." A pause. "Tomorrow I tell Malachi everything I know. Not the guess. What I saw."

In the mirror the truck idled with its lights off. It didn't need to watch them go. It had already written the car down.

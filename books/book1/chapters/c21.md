# Chapter Twenty-One: On My Account

Arenya was home by six, in her own kitchen, with Amma making rasam and pretending not to watch her.

She noticed it while washing her hands.

Under the frost *12* and its rule, the pencil dot wasn't a dot anymore. It was a short faint line, a stroke someone had started and then lifted the pen from.

She photographed it the way she'd log any evidence, and her hands weren't quite steady doing it. Under *Wednesday. Thirteen days*, she wrote: *It's not waiting for me anymore. Somebody started.*

*Look, don't touch,* Abernathy said from somewhere near the ceiling, not performing for once. *That's someone leaning in and stopping. An unfinished stroke wants a second hand to agree with it. Don't offer yours. Not even to be polite.*

Kino knocked at a quarter to nine, twice and then once. He leaned over her palm with his hands clasped behind his back, like a man in a museum, and didn't breathe on it.

"It sits *on* the line. Like someone went over it once, lightly, checking." His birthmark ached; she could see it in his face. "Somebody read it, Ari. Not wrote. Read."

"So we're being proofread."

Her phone buzzed face down on the counter. One call, unanswered. Then a voicemail.

Amma turned from the rasam with the spoon still in her hand. "Which one first, kanna?"

"Nobody listens alone," Arenya said. "Speaker. Low. And nobody answers it."

Car-interior hiss. An idling engine. A man's voice, clipped and federal.

*"It's me. Ten o'clock. Somewhere with—"*

The hiss thinned. Very close to the microphone, closer than anyone in a car could be, a pen clicked shut.

Then a second voice, dry as a page being turned, in no hurry at all:

*"…doce. Leído."*

The first voice came back mid-word, as if nothing had cut it. *"—people. Don't bring the hand into a room you didn't pick."*

Nineteen seconds. Then it stopped.

A drop of rasam fell from Amma's spoon. In the hall, Ix had drawn its lips back from its teeth.

"*Leído,*" Kino said, very low. "Read. Not us. *It.*"

Amma didn't ask what that meant. She took down the salt, the cylinder with the blue girl on it, and poured a line along the sill over the sink. Too thick. She did everything too thick when she was frightened.

* * *

At the top of the hill, Malachi's call log said *0:19*. He'd said six words. He checked again anyway, and didn't like how that sounded.

In the rearview mirror, a line of frost had been drawn across his back window from edge to edge, thin and perfectly straight. While he watched, it sweated off the glass. Where it had been, a dry stroke remained.

A tally. Someone had put a pen to his car while he sat in it.

He pulled the SIM and drove to a pay phone in Colma, across El Camino from a monument shop, and from a second burner sent a text with no name in it. *Don't come to ten. The line is read. Pick a public street, say nothing aloud, send it to this number by 9:30.*

Waiting at the curb, he opened the photo he'd taken of the window. The stroke photographed well. But the glass had caught the street behind him too, and at the far left edge, where he'd seen nothing but a blown-over recycling bin, there was a sleeve. Grey, with knit cuffs. It rested on the roof of a parked car as casually as a man waiting for a light.

At 2:10 a.m., from a motel landline, he read forty pixels of a plate to a woman at CHP who owed him.

*"No record, Shaw. That plate's never been issued."*

His name, his badge, and a plate that didn't exist were now in a CHP log with a timestamp. He'd spent a week watching other people leave paper. Tonight he'd left some.

* * *

The text reached the Sowri kitchen from an unsaved 650 number.

"Whoever that is, he's afraid of the same people we are," Kino said. "Doesn't make him a friend."

Arenya wrote the name of the bakery in her notebook, then crossed it out hard enough to tear the paper. Names were how she'd ended up on row twelve. What she sent was barely a sentence. *Where I got the footage. Opening. Outside, by the bench.*

A clerk would read a riddle with no address in it. A man who'd been watching her would read a corner.

* * *

Thursday, 6:58 a.m. Guerrero and 18th. The fog lay in the street like it had been poured.

The line had already started: two strollers, a man in cycling shoes, a woman on a call in Cantonese. Inside, at the corner table facing the door, a man in a windbreaker two sizes too big had been nursing one black coffee since five-thirty.

At seven the lock turned. Ava propped the door with her hip, a tray of morning buns on her other hand. Behind her, the oven light had gone the color of a lamp flame. It was leaning, very slightly, toward the street.

"I'm not here to ask for anything," Arenya said. "I needed a crowd and a door that opens at seven, and you're the only person I know with both. If the light does something strange, you don't have to be brave about it. I won't think you're crazy. I'm the last person in this city who gets to."

Ava looked at the oven just long enough to be sure.

"Okay. Clinical summary. It's seven a.m., I slept two hours, and my oven is doing something that isn't in the manual. It's leaning at *you*." She set the tray down. "Also, I dreamed you. A woman made of lamps, going out one at a time. A hand with a ring, pinching the wick. Come in. All of you. The wolf too. And that guy's been here since five-thirty. Is he with you?"

Malachi didn't get up. When the line had swallowed Ava's attention, he paid, passed the bench on his way out, and set a folded napkin on its dry end without looking at her.

"Good corner. The line on your hand is read. Let someone else hold your pen today."

He walked up 18th toward BART. In cramped pencil, the napkin said: *SERRA— 7K?4R.* And under that: *don't call me, I'll find you.*

Kino saw Bell first, because he'd been watching for a cuff.

Three people back in line: a grey cardigan with knit cuffs, hands folded, a single white chrysanthemum in its florist's sleeve.

The door chime rang for the cyclist ahead of him. When Bell crossed the threshold, stepping politely over Ix, it made no sound at all.

Behind the counter, the oven light guttered and, for the first time, leaned *away*.

"Good morning," Bell said to Ava, in a voice like a page being turned. "A plain croissant, please. For here." He laid the chrysanthemum beside the tip jar, stem toward her. "And whatever the young lady at the door is having. On my account."

"Ava." Arenya kept it level and small. "Don't take the flower. Don't take his money. Don't write his name on anything." She put a crumpled ten on the nearest table and pushed it over with a knuckle. "That's for mine. I'm not on anyone's account."

Kino stepped in at Ava's elbow, so Bell had to look past him. "Cash, señor. Four-fifty. Buy it or don't."

* * *

Ava had the Sharpie and a cup in her hands before she knew it. Four years of habit. Her mouth was already shaping *name for the order?*

She caught it and set the Sharpie down on its cap.

"We don't do accounts. Sorry. And you can take your flower when you go. No gifts from customers. Health code."

It wasn't true. She couldn't think of a truer way to say *no*.

Then she did what she was good at. She looked. The door stood open to fifty-degree fog. The cyclist had left a faint mist of breath going out. Bell left none. Steam off the espresso wand drifted toward the window and toward Kino, and bent a hand's width around Bell, the way smoke avoids a closed jar.

*Case note, 7:04. Subject polite. Chime silent. Light averse.*

She set the croissant on the counter, not in his hand.

He paid exact change, a coin at a time. One of the dimes was worn nearly smooth, and its date was still legible. *1912.*

He glanced at the tag on her apron with the courtesy of a man reading a name off a lapel.

"Thank you, Ava."

Her left palm went cold under the oven's heat, as if a thumb had come to rest on it.

"It isn't a gift," he said of the chrysanthemum, to no one in particular. "It's a reservation. For the sixteenth day. I understand there will be pozole."

He sat at the corner table Malachi had left. He didn't eat.

Kino lifted the dime off the till tray with a napkin, by its edge, and pressed a pinch of Nana's salt into Ava's apron pocket. "Not for luck. So there's something in there that isn't his."

"Left palm. Localized, about two centimeters." She pressed it flat to the steel counter. "I'd like it noted that I hate that I can measure it. I'm not leaving. If I leave, he gets my kitchen." Lower: "Go out the front. Text me one word a block away. Not a name. I'll text back a pastry."

"Sir," she called, at ordinary customer volume, "your croissant's getting cold."

"It keeps," Bell said fondly. "Most things do, if someone looks after them."

* * *

The door swung shut behind them without a sound.

Under the laundromat awning, Kino took the folded coffee sleeve out of his pocket and held it away from his body. A ring of damp had soaked through the cardboard. It was perfectly round, the exact size of a dime, and the morning wasn't cold enough for that.

*It ain't leakin', love,* said Abernathy. *It's* answerin'. *Somethin' that's been kept sweats when it's looked at. That's him, usin' the dime like a window.* For half a second the voice was low and old, with no cockney in it. *Daly City. I don't love it, and I can't say why, which I also don't love.*

She texted *fog.* Ava texted back *kouign-amann*, and then: **he's still here. not eating. am i ok to stay**

*Stay if you can stand it. Palm on something old.*

"Out loud," Kino said. He'd been waiting the way he waited for deer. "You said you'd tell me what you're deciding."

"Two ways for row twelve." She held up a finger. "A second witness. But nobody volunteers. I'm not putting a name on that row because somebody loved me too fast." A second finger. "Or I un-say the archana in that temple, with Hale's other listeners. That's the one that scares me."

"Which way are you leaning?"

"Livermore." It came out smaller than she meant.

"Then you don't go as the only one who can hear. Nana comes, or I do, or both. A room full of their listeners can be full of ours."

"Both of you, then. Nobody sits in that room alone with my name in it again."

On BART, past Glen Park, where the tracks climbed out into flat white daylight, the stroke on her palm went cool all at once, like a hand lifted off a table.

At Balboa Park her phone buzzed.

**he left**

**didn't eat. took nothing**

**left a tip**

Of course he had. Mr. Bell was exactly the kind of person who tipped.

# Chapter Twenty-Seven: His Name, My Hand

She texted Teddy with steady thumbs. **Not a big deal, but I need to tell you in person. Don't answer the DPH email. Don't forward it. Please stay.**

On the 5:00 BART she opened the notebook on her knee and wrote the sentence she owed him, before she could talk herself out of it.

*I cloned your keycard. I made up the project. The footage request is mine. His name, my hand.*

The pen dragged on the last words, as if the paper had gone thicker. The line came out darker than the ink should have allowed, pressed through from the other side of the page.

Then her left palm, cool and quiet all day, turned warm.

It didn't hurt. It felt like a coin that had been held in someone's fist. One slow pulse, and then it stayed.

Whatever had stopped counting her had started again. This time it was counting a number she'd put there herself.

*Oi,* said the bond. In the seat beside her a shape in a shabby coat flickered like a bad bulb. *Wrote it first. Proud of you, love. Don't tell anyone I said so. When you get to "Teddy" and then "I", don't dress it up. Angry's allowed. Anger's just a man being honest at you.* The voice slid, for half a word, toward something older. *And eat something.*

She didn't eat. She practiced the first word, which was *Teddy*, and the second, which was *I*.

* * *

The floor was mostly dark. Teddy sat at his desk with his coat on, his corgi mug full of cold tea and the DPH email open on the screen.

He'd stayed.

"You said *not a big deal* and *please stay* in the same text," he said. "Scariest text anyone's ever sent me." He pushed a chair toward her with his foot. "Okay. I'm here."

She put the notebook on the desk, open to the page.

"Teddy. I did it." And then all of it, before she could turn it into a listicle. "There's no Urban Mortality Patterns. I made it up. In September Saiesh and I cloned your keycard, I logged into the vendor portal as you, and I requested the Tartine footage under your credentials. Your name, my hand." She made herself look at him and not at the mug. "I did it because people are dying, nine since August, the same way, and nobody was looking, and I wanted to be the one who looked. That's the reason. It isn't an excuse. If this gets referred, it lands on you first. It shouldn't."

She put her hands in her lap.

"Also, I'm sorry. That goes last, because it's the cheapest part."

Teddy read the line twice, his lips moving on *his name, my hand*, then squared the notebook with the edge of the desk the way he did with contracts.

"You used my *badge*." He said it quietly, which was worse. "I defended that email. I told legal it had to be clerical, because I'm the guy who reads the terms of service." He held out his hand, palm up. "Your keycard. Until Monday. And you don't write DPH a letter tonight, alone, at midnight. That's how you got here. We write it together, with legal. My name's on it too, as the guy who didn't know. That's the truth, so it goes in."

She put her keycard in his palm. "Monday. Together. I don't deserve *together*, but I'll take it."

He turned his monitor toward her. Under the DPH email sat a photographed pink while-you-were-out slip from reception, 4:12.

*Caller for Theodore re: footage request. Asked if he had "signed for it yet." Said he'd "wait for the signature." No name.*

The callback number began with 650. She knew it. It was Rodel's shop line, borrowed again.

"That isn't an auditor," she said. "He wants you to sign something saying the request was authorized. Your name, his hand. It's the same trick, aimed at you this time. Don't sign anything. Not for me, not for him, not for legal until legal's read it twice."

"Who's waiting, Arenya?"

She got as far as *My aunt Susmithaa died on Monday.*

The desk phone lit.

It hadn't rung yet. The line-one light went amber, and the little screen filled in by itself, **650**, then Rodel's number one digit at a time, like someone dialing slowly. Then it rang, the old office double ring.

On the second ring her warm palm pulsed once, in time with the bell.

"My hand just ticked," she said, out loud. "Teddy, that's the number. It dialed itself. Don't pick it up. Don't say your name to it, and don't say *speaking*."

She wrote while it rang. *6:58. Rodel's number dialed itself on Teddy's line. Nobody here is signing.* The ink came up dark.

Teddy held his pen in his fist like a kid holding a crayon. The phone rang eleven times and stopped on the twelfth. The amber light went red.

*1 new message.* **0:41.**

It was the same length as the call on the flip phone in the temple hall.

Teddy leaned down and pulled the cord out of the wall jack, neatly, the way you'd unplug a kettle.

"Phones don't dial themselves," he said. His voice wobbled once and then steadied. "I'm an analyst. I know that. So keep talking. I'm deciding."

She gave it to him in order, like a timeline for an editor. A cottage, a car, *aneurysm*. Nine deaths, each one a freeze, a stare across a room, a fall. A paid prayer in Livermore in her own name, which had put her family in a book. A man who wrote in other people's handwriting and borrowed their numbers, who never threatened anyone and only ever asked them to *confirm*.

When she'd finished, Teddy opened his drawer, put his pen in it and locked it.

"I believe you're scared. I believe that phone. And I believe you'd have let me take the fall for a week if you thought it would keep me out of this, which makes me angrier than the badge." He rubbed his eyes. "We listen to the voicemail Monday, with legal. And Arenya, I'm not signing anything. For anyone. Put that in your notebook too."

She wrote it where he could watch. *Teddy: not signing anything. For anyone.*

The ink came up dark.

* * *

On Nana's floor in the Hillcrest house, with Ix warm against his back, Kino didn't try to sleep. He did what he'd done on the mountain since he was twelve. He slowed his breath, let the ceiling go, and this time asked a different question. Not *why did you leave me*.

*What are you looking at, Lady?*

The path in the hour before light. The Lady with her back to him. He didn't look at her shoulders this time. He looked past them.

Down the path, behind his own footprints, ran a thin grey line, ruled straight, following every step he'd taken. There was a tiny tick at each print. It was counting, and it stopped a few paces short of her heels, because she was standing on it.

She had never turned away from him. She'd been standing between him and the line.

He didn't step over it and he didn't scuff it, because scuffing it would only have been writing on it. He laid his open palm in the dirt beside her foot, the way you put a hand down for a dog to smell.

She didn't turn around. But her right hand came behind her back and opened, palm down, a handspan above his, the way a mother holds a hand back without looking while she watches the road. Down the path the ticks stopped multiplying. The line wasn't erased. It paused, like a pen lifted by someone who's noticed he's being watched.

He woke at 7:04 in the evening. Nana was in the doorway with the salt jar already in her hand.

"You looked," she said.

* * *

Arenya played his voicemail on the sidewalk outside Montgomery, nineteen seconds of it. *The Lady never turned from me. She's standing between me and a line that counts my steps. Something at the far end felt me look. Don't sign anything tonight. I'm coming to the city.*

* * *

Across the city, at Tartine, Ava stood in the café door behind a crooked line of Kino's salt. Her left palm was pressed flat to her thigh.

Malachi sat on the bench outside with a coffee he hadn't touched. In the laundromat doorway opposite, a young man in a lost-and-found ball cap stood with his hands out of his pockets, shaking where anyone could see them.

When he stepped into the light, the cold spot in Ava's palm, two centimetres and unchanged for days, went to *aching* in about four seconds, as if something across the street had called it by name.

"It's not ticking at you," she said to Malachi, in her case-report voice. "It's ticking at me. Whatever he's carrying is reading my hand like a name tag."

The man stopped at the curb and didn't step off it.

"Caleb." It came out too loud, like a word he hadn't used for a while. "That's my name. I'm carrying something that belongs to a dead woman, and it's counting at you, ma'am. I'm sorry." His eyes slid, without his permission, to the corner table inside the glass, the one Ava wiped twice a shift and nobody ever sat at. "I was the grey jacket. And on Monday I was on the team that took Susmithaa Sowri. I want to say that to her niece with my own mouth, before anyone else does."

* * *

On the escalator at Montgomery, Arenya's phone buzzed. It was an unknown number, a cheap prepaid.

*Tartine back room, 9:00, after close. Ava's door. He'll be there. He came on his own. — M.*

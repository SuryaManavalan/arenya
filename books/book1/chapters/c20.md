# Chapter Twenty: Posted, Not Thrown

At the red light on the Daly City line, the dashboard clock rolled over to 12:00, and the phone in Arenya's pocket ticked once against her hip.

Not a ring. A tick. The way a clock ticks.

Nobody in the Corolla said anything about it.

* * *

Nana shot the deadbolt, set the chain, and knelt with a grunt to pour a line of salt under the back door. Then she put the pozole on to warm, sat down with her hands folded, and didn't touch the pot.

Arenya laid the phone face up on the oilcloth. *Missed call. Unknown. 12:00 PM.* Under that, the photo of the cream envelope in Box 112.

"Kino. It's yours. Like I asked."

He read it once with his thumb held off the glass. Then again with it on, and something went through him so quietly she'd have missed it if she hadn't been counting his breaths.

"First time, it's what you saw. Your whole name. Middle name too. I didn't know you had one. I'm not saying it in here." He turned the phone face down, like a card he didn't want to play. "Second time there's another line. It sits on top of the picture like frost. It isn't written to you. It's written to whoever reads it to you."

Nobody breathed.

"'The one who reads it to her' is a job. If I say it out loud with you listening, I think I take the job." He tipped his head toward nothing. "The clerk asked me *¿Y tú?* This is them asking again, in different handwriting."

Nana fished the grocery-list pencil out of the sugar bowl and tore the flap off a PG&E envelope. "*Escríbelo.* Writing is not keeping. Saying is how they get you."

He wrote. She read it without moving her lips and put it in the freezer, in the Ziploc with the grey man's card, between the frozen tortillas and a bag of peas.

"Two things in my freezer with their handwriting. I don't like it."

Then she slid the phone halfway back across the table and left her fingers on it. "You can look yourself, and then nobody read it to you. Or we eat. I won't say which. *Ella* would have looked." A crooked smile. "*Ella* also told the insurance man to go to hell in three languages, so."

Every instinct Arenya owned was pointing at that screen. Her whole life she'd wanted to be the girl who looked.

*Don't verify,* Aunty had written. In her own hand, in her last week.

"Looking is verifying," Arenya said. "I think looking is the first stroke. And I think whatever's on that screen knows it."

She set the salt shaker on top of the phone like a paperweight. It wasn't a plan. It was what she had.

*Good call, love,* Abernathy said, from the chair nobody else could see. *Daft, and right. It wants a reader more than it wants you.* A pause, with something older in it. *And since I owe you. That voice that said* mark it so it can be found again. *It sounded like me. Someone built a wall round it, and I don't know if it was them or me.*

She opened her fist a quarter inch, so he'd know she'd heard.

* * *

They ate. For six minutes nobody talked, and it was the best six minutes of the week.

Then came a yellow legal pad and Arenya's block capitals. Numbered, no adjectives. Adjectives are where people hide.

**MARCH 9.** MERIDIAN LETTER. AUNTY = 11. ME = 12. SHE NEVER BOUGHT A POLICY.
**MARCH 19.** AUNTY'S DECLARATION AT RODEL'S. ONE HOUR LATER, HALE RENTS BOX 112.
**TODAY.** BOX LEFT OPEN. ENVELOPE TO ME. BELL WALKED IN AFTER US.

Kino took the second pen and boxed one line beside hers.

**WHO TOLD THEM MARCH 9?**

Nana got up and took the parish calendar off its nail by the phone jack. She paged back through October, September, all the way to MARZO. Monday the ninth was printed in black. The Sunday before it was in red.

**8.**

Arenya had held the pink temple receipt herself, in Aunty's bookroom, in the dust. *Livermore. Archana. Second Sunday in March.* And under *Nakshatra*, her own birth star.

A priest reads those lines aloud. That's what an archana is: a name and a star, said into a room.

She wrote the 8 slowly so her hand wouldn't shake.

"She went to Livermore the day before the letter. She wasn't signing anything. She was praying for me. And somebody heard it." Her throat closed. "It's not a hole. It's a door she held open for me."

Nana's hand came across the table and covered hers, and stayed.

"She didn't leak it, Ari," Kino said after a while. "She gave it. To a god, for you. Somebody was just standing at the back of the room with a pencil."

* * *

At two, the side yard went quiet.

It wasn't a sound. It was the lack of one, as if the wind in the concrete strip between Nana's house and the neighbor's had stopped all at once.

Across the doorstep of the only door nobody had salted, eight inches off the ground, someone had ruled a line from the fence post to the gas meter. Thinner than fishing line, white as hoarfrost, perfectly level.

"In my house," Nana said. "He drew on my house."

"Don't cut it," Arenya said. "Cutting is a mark too." She photographed it with the meter in frame. "It's a ledger line. Somebody's counting the doors."

Nana's mother had a rule for that, of course. A door that isn't a door can't be counted. It took ten minutes and the old dresser from the garage, walked across the hall a foot at a time until its back sat flush against the side door. On top went a plaster Virgen de Guadalupe.

"There. Now it's furniture. Count furniture, *cabrón.*"

Ix came in, a long low shape that most eyes slid off. It didn't look at the dresser. It looked through the laundry-room window at the other end of the line, at the gas meter with its little dials, for a long time.

Nobody said what that meant. They didn't know.

* * *

The fog sat offshore like a wall nobody had built.

At four-thirty the Pacifica pier belonged to the regulars: crabbers with orange buckets, a woman feeding the gulls something she shouldn't, an old man with three rods and the Giants pregame on a radio. Nana sat on the bench at the foot of the pier with her purse in her lap.

"*Your* letter," she told Kino. "You only take your own to the water. Carry somebody else's and now you're the mailman. I watch. I don't carry."

At the end of the pier he held the folded page flat on his palm over the rail, so the wind couldn't have it before the tide did.

"Nana didn't fail," he told the water, in Spanish first and then in English. "They knew. That's the only thing I'm sending."

He turned his hand over. He didn't throw it. He gave it.

The page lay on the green swell for one heartbeat, as if it were being read. Then the water folded over it.

Arenya put two fingers on his sleeve, over the cold forearm, for about a second. "That's one of us out of the count. Bronze medal for the rest."

He pushed the sleeve back. The birthmark was still there, the Lady's thumbprint. But where her fingers had been, the skin was warm, plain skin-warm, and it wasn't fading.

Halfway back, she saw that Nana wasn't alone on the bench.

He wore a navy overcoat over a suit, no tie, and held a bait-shop coffee. Forty or so, clean-shaven, with a pleasant face you'd forget on your way out of the room. He sat a respectful foot of slats away, angled toward the water.

Nana lifted two fingers off her purse clasp, barely, and put them back.

"That's Hale," Kino said, barely moving his mouth. "Don't say your name. Don't take anything. Not a cup, not a card."

*Brass,* Abernathy said. *Same as the box, only fresher. And he smells of nothing, love. Sat by the sea an hour and it never touched him. Narrow questions. And don't thank him. Thanking's agreeing.*

She stopped two steps from the bench and put on her telemarketer face.

"You're sitting with somebody I love. That's the first thing I don't like."

"Daniel Hale. Meridian Mutual." No card, no hand. Both of his stayed on his knees. "You're right. I shouldn't have sat here. Mrs. Lucero was kind enough not to tell me to leave."

Nana flinched.

Arenya had never seen her do it. Not at the frost, not at the line on her door. It was the flinch of someone hearing a name out of a grave.

"A library is my room," Hale went on. "This isn't anyone's. Witnesses. Good idea." To Kino, warmly: "That was well done out there. Posted, not thrown. Your matter's closed." Then to Arenya, in the voice people use for bad news at a kitchen table: "Your aunt disputed an arrangement, Ms. Sowri. Very properly. But a disputed entry doesn't stay open forever, and a gentleman in a grey cardigan filed paperwork about you this morning. He's less patient than I am. Ask me whatever you like. I don't lie. It's bad for business."

"Narrow, then." She read it back to him like a quote. "Who entered it, and on whose authority? Did Meridian rent Box 112 an hour after her declaration, yes or no? And if it isn't your decision how this closes, whose is it?"

He gave the small nod of a man who rarely got asked good questions.

"In order. Your aunt entered it. Sunday, March eighth, Livermore. She had a priest say your name and your star aloud and ask that you be *kept*." He let the word sit. "She meant protected. In the book it's the same word. Nobody makes entries, Ms. Sowri. The clerk you met writes down what's true. I only read it.

"The box: yes. Fifty-nine minutes after.

"And it's no one's decision, which is the problem. One stroke runs out. Hers runs out on her sixteenth day, when your family finishes mourning and lets her go. Then the entry closes as written, and whoever has standing collects." His eyes slid to Kino. "The gentleman is Mr. Bell. He's filed for verification of beneficiary. What he does after that, I won't describe with a child twenty feet away. I watched him do it in March."

He touched the heavy side of his coat. "The envelope in the box is a copy. This one closes the entry in your favor. You receive, and Mr. Bell has nothing to collect." A pause. "May I ask one thing back? Do you know what your aunt was carrying?"

"Sixteenth day," Kino said, low. "Thank you for the straight answer." And then, almost nothing: "*No contestes lo de su tía. Es cebo.*"

She'd already decided. She uncapped a pen so Hale could watch her not write his name.

"I'm not answering, and I'm not trading. I don't agree. Put that on your record, since you like records. If I never take your envelope and never sign, does it still close? Is there any way it stays open that doesn't involve somebody standing for me?"

"You're welcome," Hale said pleasantly, to Kino.

On the old man's radio, the tide report skipped a word, like a needle.

"Noted. I'll carry it." He didn't write anything down. "Yes, it still closes. There's one other way, though I've never seen it done. What your aunt did, she did out loud, in a particular room, through a particular man. Something said in a room can sometimes be unsaid in the same room." He stood and buttoned his coat. "I don't go into temples. Be careful who you ask there. Rooms where names get said aloud are popular with more than one kind of listener. The box is open until Friday, seven-forty. After that I'm only a phone number."

He walked up the pier a little out of step with the surf.

"Lucero," Nana said, very quietly, when he was gone. Her knuckles were white on the clasp. "It's on the paper for the house. It's on my husband's stone. I never told him that name."

Kino crouched and took her hand. "*Él ya lo tenía, Nana.* He got it somewhere that counts things."

"He reads books," Arenya said. "He read you in one. It isn't a door you left open."

Over Nana's head, not looking up, Kino said: "I thanked him. Reflex. I felt it get written. On my arm, small, like a receipt tearing off a pad."

"It's on the table now," she said. "Don't carry it alone."

In the lot, Hale's damp footprints ran at perfect intervals, like the marks on a ruler, to an empty parking space. No oil stain. No tire marks. As if no car had ever parked there.

* * *

Back in her kitchen, before Arenya left, Nana taped a sheet of butcher paper to her fridge and wrote on it in the square hand she used for church raffles.

*Fri 30 — 7:40 — the box.*

A long blank. Then, circled twice: *Mar 10 Nov — día dieciséis.* And under it, smaller: *pozole. chile aparte.*

She set the marker down as if it weighed more than a marker.

In the side yard, the frost line had melted into a wet stripe on the concrete. It was perfectly straight.

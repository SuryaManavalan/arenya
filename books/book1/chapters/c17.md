# Chapter Seventeen: Curtain

By two o'clock the house had filled the way houses fill when someone dies.

Shoes three deep in the entry. Folding chairs from nowhere. Aunties Arenya half recognized sat on the sofa with their hands in their laps. Amma had set the cracked lamp in the puja alcove beside a drugstore-framed photo of Aunty laughing, out of focus, caught mid-turn. Appa had lit it with a kitchen match. The flame was small and steady.

Someone knocked, twice.

Arenya got there first. An old woman in a pinned shawl. Behind her, a young man in a black shirt, holding a heavy pot high against his chest.

She didn't need an introduction.

"You're Itzel," she said. "She said you'd come with pozole."

The old woman took both of Arenya's hands, pot or no pot. Her thumb passed over the right palm and stayed there half a second longer than courtesy. She didn't say what she'd found.

"Mija. Ella me habló de ti. *Mucho.*"

The young man toed off his boots and set them in the pile, toes out. "Lo siento mucho," he said, to the height of a mother's face. Then he carried the pot down the hall.

As he passed the alcove, the lamp flame lay down sideways.

It didn't flicker. Arenya had watched a lot of candles in the last day, and she knew a flicker. This was a lean, flat and patient, like a dog's head turning at a footstep, and it held the whole length of the hall. No window was open. The furnace was off.

Her right hand answered. A warm pulse ran up her wrist, once, like a second heartbeat out of step with the first.

When he came back from the kitchen, she was standing there holding somebody's sneakers.

"Her grandson," he said, eyes lowered politely to her collarbone. "Joaquín. Kino." The corner of his mouth almost moved. "I saw it. I don't know why it did that. I'm not going to guess in your mother's house."

"Neither am I," she said. "But I'd like to talk to you and your grandmother later. Somewhere with fewer aunties."

He moved half a pace sideways so they stood shoulder to shoulder, facing down the hall, the way you'd stand beside someone at a trailhead. If the flame wanted to lean, it could lean at both of them at once.

"Those go by the door, by the way," he said, nodding at the sneakers. "Toes out. In case anybody has to leave fast."

* * *

Steel bowls came out, then paper ones when the steel ran out. Two aunties from the Fremont side asked if pozole was like sambar, and Nana told them gravely that it was exactly like sambar, and completely different. An uncle asked for more chili and was told, in a tone Susmithaa would have recognized, not to be rude about the chili.

Kino counted faces out of habit. At nineteen he stopped.

There were twenty.

The twentieth stood by the bookshelf: mid-fifties, grey cardigan, an untouched paper bowl. He had the kind of face you forgot while you were still looking at it. When Appa came by with the ladle, he filled the bowls on either side of the man and skipped his, without seeming to notice.

The man was looking at Arenya's right hand. The way an appraiser looks at a ring in a pawnshop window.

"Don't look," Kino said, handing her a bowl. "The man by the bookshelf. Whose cousin is he?"

"Nobody's," she told the steam. "Everyone here has been fighting about seating since noon. I'd know."

"Appa skipped his bowl and didn't notice. That isn't rudeness. It's *made*." He picked up a bowl he didn't want. "If he comes toward you, say my name."

*Abernathy,* she thought, hard. *Look at him.*

*Not a hole like the coat at the hospital, love.* Quick and close. *Dull light, pilot-flame color. But he's wearin' somethin'. Ring of frost round his head like a hatband. His light bends round it, all smoothed over. That's why everyone's eyes skid.* A pause. *And he's pointed at your right hand. Only that. Level as a spirit-bubble.*

The man set down his bowl beside a row of Agatha Christies and crossed the room through gaps that opened just before he reached them. At the photograph he pressed his palms together and dipped his head, correctly, like someone who'd learned it long ago. Close up he smelled of wool, and faintly, impossibly, of frost on a windshield.

"I'm sorry for your loss," he said, in a flat Midwestern voice. "She was very good at keeping her hands still."

Kino was already at her shoulder. He looked straight at the forgettable face and held it, and let himself be seen holding it.

"She's eating," he said quietly. "Let her."

Arenya swallowed a spoonful and looked at the knot of the man's tie. "Did you know her? Or did you just read the spines?"

"We never met. I read about her for a long time." He considered the shelf. "*Curtain* is upside down. I don't think that was an accident." His head turned a degree toward Kino. "Bell. People call me Mr. Bell. It's on nothing, so don't bother looking. Your dog's very well trained, son."

He drew a plain white sympathy card from his cardigan, already sealed, and laid it on the sofa arm by her knee.

"For the family. Open it when the aunties go home."

He left by the back door, unhurried. Kino didn't follow. Something low and heavy in the fog outside did.

Ten minutes later Kino bent to collect her empty bowl and spoke to it.

"Beige Camry, barcode on the rear window. Plate starts 7K, maybe an R. In the car he took something off his head with both hands, like a tight hat, and his nose bled. Then he called someone and sounded sorry about it." He straightened. "Write the plate on your hand, not your phone."

She turned her left wrist over and wrote it there in small block letters, under the cold coin. *7K_R? BEIGE CAMRY. NOSEBLEED. SORRY.*

The sympathy card was cold through her palm. She'd never had a sympathy card feel like a dead battery.

* * *

By six the aunties were gone and the windows were black mirrors. Appa slept upright in his chair with the ladle still in his hand. Amma sat on the floor by the alcove. Nana had the good chair. Kino sat on the floor beside Amma, the lowest person in the room, sleeve pushed back. A pale birthmark showed on his forearm. He didn't hide it and he didn't point to it.

The card hadn't warmed by a single degree in four hours.

*There's a thread off the corner,* Abernathy said. *Fine as spider silk, runnin' toward the back door. A tag, like a ring on a pigeon's leg. While it's in your hand, they know where you are.*

"I think this is tagged," Arenya said, and heard how it sounded, and didn't stop. "I'm not going to explain tonight how I know."

Nana didn't touch it. "Joaquín. The towel from the pot."

She draped the damp towel over the envelope, folded the corners in like a tamal, and carried the bundle into the kitchen. She put it at the back of the freezer, behind a brick of frozen parathas and a bag of peas gone white with frost.

"Cold hides in cold," she said, wiping her hands. "Now it smells of my kitchen. Whoever holds the string will feel a house, and soup, and a girl who stayed home." She sat down heavily. "We don't cut it. Cutting is a word, and they would hear it. We just bore them."

Arenya opened her right hand, palm up, in the lamplight.

"Amma. I lied to you at the hospital. It wasn't the steering wheel."

Amma got up on her knees and took the hand the way she used to check for splinters. Her thumb stopped on the heel of the palm.

"Your aunt's hands were warm like this. Always. December, January. I used to say it was the ghee." Her grip tightened, and her eyes came up wet and furious and steady. "You don't lie to me again, kanna. Not about this. I don't care how strange."

"Everything," Nana said gently. "But first." She lifted her chin toward the bookshelf, toward the one spine standing on its head. "That man saw it in one look. I have been sitting in this chair four hours trying not to."

Behind Kino, the lamp flame stretched a full inch taller, toward the shelf.

Poirot's last case. Of course she'd picked that one.

Arenya slid it out and turned it right side up. It fell open where something had been pressed flat for a long time: a thin pink carbon receipt from the Shiva-Vishnu Temple in Livermore. *Archana, 1.* The second Sunday in March. On the name line, *S. Sowri.* Under *Nakshatra*, someone had written Arenya's birth star. Not Susmithaa's.

She had paid for a prayer in Arenya's name.

On the back, in fountain-pen ink gone slightly brown:

> *Ari —*
> *The lamp knows who. Itzel knows the rest.*
> *Don't let them count you.*
> *Keep your hands still until you can't. Then don't.*
> *— S.*
> *(Not too much sugar. I know.)*

When her right hand closed on the receipt, the heat went out of her palm all at once. A heartbeat later it came back stronger.

Behind Kino, the lamp's single flame split in two. Side by side, like two people at a trailhead.

For one second.

Then it was one again.

In the good chair, Nana had a hand over her mouth.

*Ari.* Nobody had called her that since she was nine. She'd forgotten Aunty ever had.

She knelt and put the receipt in Amma's hands. Amma read it three times. On the third, her lips moved on *Ari*. She held it against her chest and didn't cry, which seemed to cost her more than crying would have.

"*Don't let them count you*," Arenya said to Nana. "That's the net. I saw it last night. Who are *they*?"

"My grandmother called them *los que cuentan*. The ones who count." Nana turned a small silver jaguar brooch over in her fingers. "In her time they came with priests' ledgers. In my mother's time, with government clipboards. Now, cardigans and rental cars. They write down people who carry something. Then the people go quiet." She looked at the lamp. "Your aunt carried a light, mija. Not a metaphor. Down her mother's side, never to the one you'd expect. In March she felt it start looking for somewhere to go. It was looking at you." Her mouth went thin. "I told her to tell you. She said you'd laugh, and then write an article about it."

"She was right," Arenya said. "I'd have been wrong. But I'd have written it."

Then she told it, the part that sounded worst first. Nine people frozen mid-sentence over eight weeks. A dream with a net and four men. A man in a cardigan pricing her hand like a ring.

"And Kino," she said at the end. "Whatever's on your arm, you don't have to show me. But I'd like to know if it's cold."

He crossed the carpet and held out the forearm. A pale rosette, like a thumbprint pressed into brown skin.

"Since the mountain turned," he said. "For weeks I've dreamed it. She rolls over, and I slide off her shoulder. I thought it meant she'd turned away from me." He didn't look at Nana. "It hurt. It still does. But it goes cold when you're near, and when the lamp leans. Not *at* you. *Toward* something." His mouth moved, tired. "The training says I'm supposed to be the one who walks through the door. I'm trying to learn the other job. Standing beside it."

She pushed her own sleeve back and held her wrist under the lamp. The coin sat on the pulse point, the grey of an old nickel, a plate number written underneath.

"Same cold. Since the minute she died." And to Kino, quieter: "Beside, not through. I can do beside."

Amma got up off the floor the way mothers do, all at once, with no help.

"Then nobody goes home tonight. Itzel-amma, you take Susmithaa's room. She changed the sheets Sunday." Her voice caught on *Sunday*, and she walked straight through it. "Joaquín, the sofa. If your dog wants the porch, he can have the porch. And you, kanna, start from the beginning. I'll make tea."

She went into the kitchen. The freezer hummed.

From Arenya's bag on the floor came a short, polite double buzz. Through the thin mesh pocket, Susmithaa's phone lit up blue.

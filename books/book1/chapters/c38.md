# Chapter Thirty-Eight: Collateral

Colma after dark was a town of the dead with a few streetlights for the living.

Malachi sat at the far end of the bench in the 110 shelter, coat collar up, and looked at the cemetery wall. A mile of brick and cypress and wrought iron. His phone said 7:03. His watch said 7:00. The Serra Bowl sign down the road said 7:05, and while he watched it said 7:06. He had stopped being angry about the drift. He noted the gap and let it be a number.

Across El Camino a SamTrans bus idled at the curb. Doors open. Cabin lights on. Route sign dark. Nobody had got off, and no one sat at the wheel.

A bus that wasn't going anywhere was a message, or a cage.

Beside him on the bench someone had left an orange, its navel turned toward the road. He didn't touch it.

"Caleb," he said to the wire at his collar, without turning his head. "The bus. Tell me if anything in it moves."

*Staying in the car,* said the wire, flat and low. *Nothing's moved.*

"You wrote that you'd be early, H," Malachi said to the empty shelter. "I'm on time. I'm alone, as asked. If you want to be vouched for, you'll have to be seen."

Then he let the silence work.

The bench creaked under a second weight.

The man had not been there, and now he was. Fifties. A Carhartt jacket gone grey at the seams, work boots with the steel showing at one toe. A weathered, ordinary face, the face of a man who drove a van for a living. His hands lay folded and very still. He smelled of turned earth and diesel.

His boots were dry. No wet grass. He could only have come through the gate.

"Early," Hale said. "Like I said."

In the dark car forty feet back, Caleb knew the voice before his brain caught up. The van. *Keep your head down, son.* And under it, a smaller thought, the one he'd say later: *He's older than I left him.*

"Don't look at the bus, Mr. Shawani," Hale said. "It's not for you. It's for me, if this goes wrong. The Counter sends transport when a balance comes due. Doors open, no driver. It's very patient. That one's been waiting nine days." He nodded at the orange without touching it. "That's not mine either. Rodel's people leave them for the dead. Somebody thinks one of us is."

Across the road the bus doors gave a soft pneumatic sigh, like something breathing out. They did not close.

"I can give you the Thursday site. Where the lattice goes up. Which door Team Four uses. Where the boy's people will be standing when it blows. What I need is your word to El Contador that I'm worth more off that bus than on it. Your *word*. Not your name." A pause. "I know what you did with your name."

"You came through a chained gate," Malachi said. "I'll assume you know what that tells me. I won't give you my name again, Mr. Hale. You've already sold it. I'll give you something smaller and harder to take back."

He counted it out. One: he'd carry what Hale said exactly as said, no edits for or against. Two: he'd say Hale came unarmed and spoke first. Three: if the Thursday site was true, he'd say so. If it was false, he'd say that too, and the vouch would die with it.

"I won't promise you're worth more off the bus. I'll promise you were worth hearing."

Hale breathed out through his nose. It might have been a laugh, from a man out of practice. "That's more than fair."

Across the road the bus door sighed again and stopped halfway, as if it were listening too.

"Now the price of mine," Malachi said. "Who bought my name?"

"I didn't sell it to a face. I sold it to a lease. Bayline put it on their books as an asset, paid for in water rights. Somebody wanted a man who could talk to things that live under the Bay. The guarantor on the paper is Applied Field Research, signed with one letter. *M.* I never met M. I met a woman at the Embarcadero with a pen that wrote in salt. I got eleven seconds of her and a check that cleared."

Then he gave the door, plainly, like a delivery address.

"Thursday. Hunters Point. The old drydock pump house off Crisp. Team Four goes in through the south roll-up at 9:30. The lattice goes live at 9:41, and it doesn't run off a coil. It runs off the *pilings*. That's what the stakes were for. They're tuning the water. Strand's old crew stands the apron. Dorsey. Pruitt. The new kid. When the water answers, the apron's the first thing it takes back."

The bus door closed. It eased shut with a polite hiss, and the cabin lights went out one row at a time, back to front. The bus pulled from the curb with no one at the wheel. It did not signal.

Hale watched it go. His folded hands were shaking now. "That's provisional. It comes back if your third part goes the wrong way."

"The woman with the salt pen," Malachi said. "Tell me one thing about her I could find again."

Behind them a car window came down with a grinding sound.

"Hale." Too loud. Caleb pulled it back down. He didn't get out. "Fourteen months on your team, and I don't remember most of it. That's what the Crown is for. The new kid. How long's he been on Vesper? Does he know what's in it, or did they tell him it's focus? You said the apron's the first thing the water takes. Then you knew when you wrote the card."

Hale answered Malachi first, without hesitating. "Sea-glass ring. Green, on her thumb. Lanyard on backwards, so I wouldn't see the card. Pool chlorine. When she left she didn't go to a car. She walked to the Ferry Building and took the Alameda boat."

Then he turned to the open window, and something in the ordinary face gave.

"Ollie," he said. "Ollie Baptiste. Twenty-two. Six weeks on it. They told him focus. They told all of you focus. Yeah, Strand. I knew when I wrote the card. That's why I wrote it."

"I'm not asking you to forgive me," Caleb said. "I'm asking you to pull the kid off the apron."

"I can't walk him off. If I pull him, they know I talked, and the bus is back before morning. It has to be someone he's never met who knows the words." Hale looked at the hands shaking on Caleb's knees. "You know the words. You used to say them to me."

He went back the way he'd come, through the iron gate. On the far side he looped the chain closed, though it couldn't be locked from there. The cypresses took him in eleven steps.

The orange stayed on the bench, navel to the road.

* * *

The T stopped between stations and the doors stayed shut.

Arenya felt it before she saw it. Every rider turned toward the bay windows at once, with the same face: someone who had forgotten something terrible and was about to remember.

Eleven seconds. Then twelve. Then it let go, and they went back to their phones, sheepish, the way people are after a sneeze in a quiet room. Except the woman at the rear doors, who kept her palm flat on the glass and cried without making a sound.

Arenya didn't look at her for long. If she did, she'd stay.

Low water had left the shore a wide shine of mud. Two Bayline trucks sat nosed onto the gravel with their headlights off. Between them stood a man holding a light, and the light was the wrong color: grey and cold, an office at three in the morning. She couldn't see his face. Only the page he read from, in a low flat voice. Up on Hudson, under a dead streetlight, a dark sedan idled. MILL OPERATIONS on the door. A woman at the wheel, lanyard turned backwards over her shoulder.

And out past the survey rod, something ankle-deep and patient was facing her.

*The clerk's lamp turns when he reaches a name he doesn't like,* Abernathy said, too cheerful, the way he got when he was frightened. *I'll say "turn." You don't step in it, and you don't look at the page. Pages look back.*

At the waterline Kino crouched behind the riprap with his left hand open over the shine and the jaguar mark turned up. The mud leaned toward his palm, and a voice came out of it, made of the sound a boot makes pulling free. *Three nails. Pull one and I am loose. Loose, I go to the one who reads her.* Then, toward Arenya's tailgate: *Choose for me. I am tired of men choosing.*

On the mud a laborer set the first stake to its line. The clerk lifted his page into his own light. "One for the slack. One for the ebb. One for—"

He stopped. The light slid off the paper and began to sweep, slow as a lighthouse.

*Turn,* said Abernathy.

Arenya folded herself smaller, one hand over the bag so the Lamp couldn't glow, eyes shut. She did everything right. Her shoe scraped anyway.

Somewhere to her left, Ava said, "Hey!" She had walked off the platform with her phone blazing white like a flare. "Is this the right place for the sunset kayak thing? My phone says the tide's *wrong*." It was the stupidest thing Arenya had ever seen, and the bravest thing Ava had ever done. Up Hudson, a car alarm began to scream.

Then Malachi stepped past her.

He walked into the line between the light and the tailgate as if he were crossing a lobby. Coat open. Hands empty. "Evening. Federal. Nobody's in trouble yet. I'd like to see the permit for those stakes."

The beam found him. It crossed his chest and *stuttered*, the way a scanner chirps at a barcode it has already rung up.

"Permit's on file," the clerk said. "Under your name, sir. You're the collateral."

The edge of the beam slid past Malachi's hip and came to rest on Arenya's shoe. It was cold as low tide. It sat there, patient, a finger on a sleeve. The clerk didn't look down. He wrote one word in his margin, and she could hear the pen.

*He knows someone's here. He doesn't know who.*

"Collateral," Malachi said, mild as a man confirming a reservation. "Then I'm entitled to see the instrument. A lease with a blank signature line isn't a lease."

Up on Hudson the sedan's window came down two inches. The woman with the green ring held a clipboard to the glass. One signature line, circled in red. Blank. Then the sedan pulled away, unhurried, and Arenya had the impression, absurd and awful, that the woman was sorry for him.

At the water, the slack had curled around Kino's wrist. "No nails," he was saying. "I didn't pull three out of you so they could put three back. But you don't go to her, *abuela*. She's got a lamp, a dead woman's canister, and a light coming at her right now. She can't carry the tide too." He turned his arm over, the Lady's thumbprint and the clerk's ruled stroke side by side. "Come to me. Loose, you come up the creek to my door. Salt on the sill. Water in the bowl. I'll walk you to the sea myself. That's an offer, not an order. Name your price. If it's no, I'll pull the nail anyway."

Nobody ordering anything. Arenya hadn't known he could do that. She wasn't sure he had either.

*"You asked,"* the water said. *"Nobody asks."* And a price: his own nail, the one he'd pulled out of her, driven into his own door-sill with his own hand. Held by a house, not a ledger. *"But their nail goes in at the turn. Spoil it first."*

"My nail, my sill," Kino said. "Done."

He didn't throw like a pitcher. He threw the way you salt a doorway, low and flat from the hip, a pinch fanned off the side of his hand, timed to the tide and not to the man. The salt rode the still water to the foot of the stake. *Sal para la casa. No para el libro.*

The maul came down. The sound was wrong: iron on iron, flat and dead, a bell struck underwater. The stake stood in the mud holding nothing. The slack slid away from Arenya's tailgate, out along the flat toward the culvert and the hills.

*Held,* said the mud, almost shy. *By a house.*

The clerk looked at the line he could no longer finish and gave Malachi a small, tired, collegial nod. "The instrument is executed Thursday, sir. You'll be asked to sign the blank." He folded the page into his breast pocket, face in. "Everyone is."

The work lamps clicked out. Out past the piling the foghorn sounded, and Ava counted it without meaning to.

Twelve.

Later, under the dome light of a parked car, she took out the kelp letter and counted the knots twice. There were five. At dinner there had been four.

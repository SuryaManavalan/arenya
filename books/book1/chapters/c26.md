# Chapter Twenty-Six: Not Today

Friday. Seven twenty-five.

The fog over Mission Road had come apart into strips, lit gold from underneath. Two doors down from Colma Notary & Mail, the florist had her buckets out already, chrysanthemums and marigolds.

Appa parked the Corolla nose-out and left the engine running.

"Seven-forty, I come in," he told the windshield. "Before, if you wave." His hands stayed at ten and two.

Rodel let them in by the side door off the alley and held it with his foot. Inside, the wall of brass post-office boxes gave off a faint warmth, like a stove someone had just switched off. Every little door was filmed with dust and fingerprints except one, low by the floor.

Box 112 had been wiped clean.

*Brass again, love,* said Abernathy near her ear. *Warm. Like somebody's just took their hand off it.*

"Ask me," Rodel said. He stood with his palm flat on the counter. "I was waiting for her niece to ask me. Ask me right and I'll tell you right."

"Who is *he*?" Arenya said. "You told my father he asked for one-twelve before it was empty. Who, when, and how?"

Rodel bent under the counter and came up with a fat blue ledger, BOX RENTALS 2014– on masking tape, the spine mended twice.

"The journal belongs to the State. This one's mine."

Row 112, in his tired right-leaning ballpoint: **S. SOWRI — opened 04/2015 — cash, yearly.** Eleven renewals. **CLOSED 03/06/26, key returned.**

Above it, squeezed into the margin in pencil, was one more line.

*Hold 112 for Mr. Dalal on vacancy.*

Every other word on the page leaned. These letters sat on an invisible ruler, and the last *y* ended in a small hook, like a bracket.

Arenya knew that hook. She'd been staring at it for two days, on the back of a check.

"That's my pencil," Rodel said. "I wrote it while he stood where you're standing. End of February. He said he was from the temple in Fremont. He knew her star. I can't give you his face. I've tried, at night. I get a nice shirt, and hands cold like Mr. Bell's. He didn't sign. He asked me to write it, and I thought writing it so neat was *my* idea." He tapped the counter twice, hard. "Then he asked could the family use my number for the paperwork. I said yes. That's how my number got on your line."

His eyes went to the wall.

"A week later she emptied her box and gave me the key. She was angry. Not at me. She said, *Rodel, they're moving in next door to me.* Thirteen days after that she came back with your father to notarize her no. An hour after them, the polite one rented one-twelve with new twenties. He asked for it by number."

Arenya held her phone over the page, slowly, so he'd see it coming. "A photograph. Pictures aren't signatures."

"No," Rodel agreed. "They're not."

On the screen the page looked the same as on the counter, with one difference. Under the pencil line ran a faint grey rule that wasn't on the paper, the stroke a bookkeeper draws under a column before he writes the total.

She photographed the closing card too. *S. Sowri, box surrendered.* Her aunt's signature, firm and slanted. And in the photo, only in the photo, something after it: a small round sun with short rays, drawn the way a child draws one.

Her throat did something she hadn't authorized.

*That sun smells like her,* Abernathy said softly. *Ghee and pencil shavings. She signed that card twice, love. Once for him to see. Once for you.*

At the alley door Kino had been reading the street through the slats. "Grey Nissan. By the florist. Nobody in it," he said. "Windows dry. On a cold morning."

"Since before six," said Rodel. "Nobody got out." He wiped his palm on his guayabera. "Mr. Hale called last night, nine-twelve, on the shop line. *If Ms. Sowri comes before I do, tell her the box is open to her until seven-forty. She needn't sign anything. Only show identification.* He said your whole name, anak. Every part, the way it's written on things. Flat. Polite like a recording of polite. Forty-one seconds." He paused. "And I should offer her coffee."

Behind him a fresh pot steamed on the warmer. He looked at it as if he'd never seen it before.

"I don't drink coffee," he said. "I have tea."

"Don't pour it," said Arenya. "Not for me, not for you. Pouring is how it gets accepted." She took one full stride back from the brass. "Here's what I'd like. Not the box. Showing ID is still walking in. Write one line in your own book, in your own slant. *Ms. Sowri came. Declined. Nothing accepted.* At seven-forty, open like always."

Rodel tipped the whole carafe into the bucket under the counter. It didn't steam going down. Then, standing, leaning right, he wrote:

*Fri 10/30 7:25 — Ms. Sowri came. Declined. Nothing accepted. — R.P.*

As the pen lifted, the shimmer over 112's keyhole died, and behind the brass door something small and stiff slid down. An envelope tipping over.

"Thirty-one years," Rodel said, "and every line in this book has two hands on it. This one only has mine." He laid the pen across the page and didn't offer it.

"Let it stand alone," Arenya said. "A no doesn't need a second hand. It needs to be true."

Through the front glass the Nissan's passenger window had fogged. From the inside, one slow breath's worth.

"It's listening," Kino said. "Rodel, if anything asks for a second hand on that line, you say *Not today*. Out loud, and then you go and make tea. We go out the side. Nobody looks back at the car."

At the door Arenya stopped. "When a man writes through your hand, can you tell?"

Rodel thought about it the way he'd think about a fee.

"It feels like being helpful," he said. "On Wednesday I felt *useful*. I haven't felt useful since my mother." He went to fill the kettle. Under his breath, practicing: "Not today."

* * *

From the back seat of the Corolla, through a gap between the florist's van and a light pole, they could see a narrow strip of Mission Road.

At 7:40 by the dashboard clock, the Nissan's driver's door opened, and Daniel Hale stepped out in his navy overcoat as if he'd been sitting there all along.

Nobody had been sitting there.

He went into the shop. Ninety seconds later he came out holding a cream envelope by one corner. On the sidewalk he stopped, turned his pleasant face to the gap between the van and the pole, to the back seat of a car he shouldn't have been able to see, and gave them a nod, the courteous kind you give across a lobby.

Then he drove toward Colma. The fog on the passenger window didn't clear.

Appa's phone buzzed. Rodel, in capitals like a man who'd learned to text late: **HE ASKED FOR TEA. I SAID NOT TODAY. BOX 112 CLOSED. NEW LOCK ORDERED.** And then: **PEN STILL THERE. NOBODY TOUCHED IT.**

"In order," Appa said to nobody, and put the car in gear.

* * *

At the Westlake Chevron he pulled in although the needle sat above half.

"Five minutes," he said, and went inside, and stood in front of the coffee machines without touching any of them. He was buying them time, badly, which was the only way he knew how to do it.

Kino sat in the back with his forearms on his knees. He turned his left wrist up so the four brown spots showed.

"Two things I've been carrying since Livermore. One. Your notebook gets heavier when you say something true. I watched it at the meter, and again this morning at *nothing accepted*. It isn't answering to the Lady or to Nana. It's answering to *you*." He looked at the fog in the road cut. "Two. For weeks, every time I dream on the mountain, the Lady's there with her back to me. Not angry. Turned. Looking at something I can't see. I haven't told anybody. Twenty years of night-walks, and I can't get her to look over her shoulder."

Arenya turned the dented thermos over in her hands.

"I don't know anything about mountains," she said. "But I've written about a lot of dogs. A dog that turns its back on you in a doorway isn't ignoring you. It's standing between you and whatever's coming up the walk." She shrugged, embarrassed. "That's a listicle, not theology."

Kino didn't answer for a while. Something in his shoulders let go.

"Facing the walk," he said. "I'll take that to the mountain."

Appa got back in smelling of burnt coffee, checked the mirrors left, center, right, and took them up onto 280 into the fog. After a mile he spoke to the windshield.

"The thermos is rasam. Your amma's. I warmed it at five. I didn't give it before because you don't eat before an exam." Another mile. "Did it go okay? You don't have to tell me what it was."

"It went okay, Appa." She poured a capful. "I said no three times. Nobody signed anything. Rodel's getting a new lock."

She held the cap out across the gearshift, the way she had at nine. He took it with his right hand and drove with his left, which he wasn't supposed to do and always did.

"Too much pepper," he said. "She always made it too much pepper."

His voice skipped on *always*. A mile later he reached over without looking and turned the heater up one notch on her side.

* * *

At 4:12 that afternoon, at home in Daly City, with Appa asleep in his chair in front of a muted cricket replay, her work email received a message she hadn't been copied on. Teddy forwarded it with no comment except his corgi-gif signature.

*SF Dept. of Public Health, Records Compliance. Re: "Urban Mortality Patterns – SF 2025." Our annual audit flagged a footage request under this project. Please confirm the requesting party's authorization by Monday, Nov 2, 5:00 p.m. Unconfirmed requests are referred.*

Then a text. **renny do u know what this is?? not a big deal prob 👉👉**

She read it standing in the hallway, between Amma's chappals and the cricket.

Nobody had written anything yet.

*Yet* was doing a lot of work.

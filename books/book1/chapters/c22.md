# Chapter Twenty-Two: Seven Signs Your Free Coffee Is Haunted

Nana's kitchen smelled of dried chiles blistering on a comal, smoky and sweet and so stubbornly *alive* that Arenya stopped on the top step with her hand on the rail.

Nana opened the screen, looked at Kino's jacket instead of their faces, and the welcome drained out of her.

"*¿Qué traes ahí?*"

She turned off the comal. From under the sink she brought a chipped enamel bowl and a paper sack of slaked lime, the stuff she soaked the corn in, and added water from the kettle. It hissed and clouded.

"Salt is for keeping things out. *Cal* is for taking the skin off. Corn. Walls. Ink." She tipped the coin onto a saucer with the tongs. A Barber dime, Liberty worn almost smooth, *1912* still sharp, a tiny **S** under the wreath. The damp on it didn't dry. "He didn't give it. He *paid* it. A paid coin isn't his anymore. So he's looking through a door he already shut, and that's cheating, mijo. A cheater's window can be painted over."

Arenya copied *1912 / S / Liberty* into her notebook before it was gone.

Kino laid it in face down. The lime hissed like a match dropped in sand, and a ring of dead grey spread out from the coin, and pressed into the grey, faint as breath on glass, was the print of knit ribbing. A cuff. As if someone on the far side had leaned an elbow on the window for a better look, right up to the last second.

Then the lime settled, and the print was gone.

"*Bueno.* He's blind."

The kitchen went quiet the way a room does when a fridge you hadn't noticed stops humming.

And Arenya's palm woke.

It didn't burn. It was warm, like a cup somebody had just set down. When she tilted her wrist toward the lime, nothing happened. When she tilted it toward the hall, it leaned.

She followed it like a compass down eight feet of runner rug to the dresser against the side door. The warmth didn't care about the rime still clinging to the jamb behind it. It cared about the top drawer.

"That drawer is just my things," Nana said.

Too fast. It was the first thing she'd said too fast all morning.

*Not Bell, love,* Abernathy said. *This ain't got an edge. Camphor. Old paper. And under that I'd swear it's your aunt's hand lotion, the jasmine one.* A pause. *Quick ain't guilty. Quick is scared of what you'll do with it. Let her open it herself.*

"Nana." Arenya kept it gentle. "It's warm. Not Bell-cold. Warm like something somebody already wrote on. I'm not going to open your drawer. But if there's something in there that's hers, I'd rather you hand it to me than have me stand in your hall guessing."

Nana stirred the chiles once. Twice.

Then she turned off the burner, and that was how they knew.

"*Ella me dijo* you would do this. 'She'll stand in your hall and be polite at you, Itzel, and you'll feel like a thief in your own house.'" She set the Guadalupe on the floor facing the door, like a guard, and opened the drawer.

Camphor, old paper, and under that, faint as memory, jasmine and sweet almond. Arenya's body knew it from every hug of her life before her brain caught up.

It was a dented red Brooke Bond tin from an Indian grocery.

"Thursdays. Eleven years she brings her own tea, because she says mine is *agua de calcetín*." Sock water. Nana almost laughed, and pried off the lid.

On a bed of dusty leaves lay a sheet of cream paper folded around a typed page. Through the cream, three short paragraphs in Susmithaa's hand. Through the typed page, two lines for signing at the bottom. One held a looping *S. Sowri*. The other was blank, and typed beneath it:

**WITNESS — ITZEL R. MARTÍNEZ.**

The warmth in her palm stopped pointing at the drawer. It pointed at the empty line.

"She asked me in March," Nana said, very steady, the way people sound when steady costs them. "She said one stroke would hold a little while, but a no needs two. And I said, *déjame pensarlo.* Because I know what that book does to a name. Every Thursday I thought. And then there were no more Thursdays. That's the part I left out."

Arenya took the pages in both hands, the way you'd take a bird.

"It has your name on it, and I'm not going to ask you to fill it. You thought about it for months because you knew what it costs. That isn't cowardice. It's the reason I'd trust you with anything." She slid the pages between two clean ones in her notebook. "She told me to feed Itzel's boy. I think this is the other half of that sentence. Somebody has to feed *you* back."

Nana patted her cheek twice, hard enough to count. "She said you'd be polite. She didn't say you'd be kind."

She put the Guadalupe back on the dresser, facing the side door this time.

"There. You watch *that.*"

* * *

SERRAMONTE AUTO was a narrow lot between a tire shop and a shuttered Sizzler, thirty-odd cars under limp pennant string, prices in yellow grease pen. A heavyset man in a Warriors windbreaker was hosing down a Civic.

"Benny! You folks looking, or you folks *looking*?"

"Looking," Arenya said. "My cousin Joe needs a Camry."

Through the office window, the back wall was a corkboard of Polaroids: smiling buyers holding up keys, each dated in Sharpie. Low on the right, half under a newer photo, was a beige roof and a man's arm holding keys. The face was hidden by the overlap. The sleeve wasn't. Grey knit, the cuff turned once.

While Kino made Benny turn his back to the wall for the service records, she lifted the top Polaroid with a fingernail.

**WALT — 3/19 — 4:51 PM — CASH ✓**

March nineteenth, the afternoon Hale had rented Box 112. Under the Sharpie the margin was frosted, a rime as straight as a ruled line, with a space heater ticking four feet away. Her palm went hot and *pushed*.

*Window's lit,* Abernathy said, very fast. *Put the top one back and it shuts like an eyelid.*

She let it fall flat.

"Walt," Benny said, when Kino asked. "Oh, you don't forget Walt. Cardigan, in March. Manners like a librarian. Paid in old soft bills, each one folded down the middle, counted them out on the desk in *Spanish*. *Diez, quince, veinte.* Doesn't speak a word otherwise, I tried. Title's Walter Bell. Address is a box at Colma Notary. Number twelve."

Arenya's pen went through the paper.

"Funny thing. He called me last week. Wanted to know had anybody come asking about his car. 'If they do, Mr. Benasaraf, take their names. I'd like to send a thank-you.'" Benny pulled a sticky pad toward him and uncapped the Sharpie, all good cheer. "So. Joe. And the lady is…?"

"Joe Ramos," Kino said, and slid the pad back with two fingers. Not rude, just final. "You don't need a name for a thank-you, Benny."

Out in the sun her whole body let go, and she kept walking fast.

"Box twelve is Bell's. Hale's is 112. Two boxes, same shop."

"Either Bell's following the same paper," Kino said, "or he laid it."

* * *

They walked down into Colma, where the living were outnumbered a thousand to one and the sidewalk knew it.

She didn't see the Camry. It had been parked so long under the cypress that pollen had turned its roof the color of the curb. She only saw it in the window of COLMA NOTARY & MAIL, reversed in the glass: a beige sedan with a dealer frame, and in the driver's seat a grey sleeve and two hands folded on the wheel the way a man folds his hands on a pew. Where the face should have been, the glass gave back a soft wash of light, like Benny's Polaroid.

She took the plate off the reflection in one half-second and put it in her head like a stitch. **7 K B Q 4 R.** One more character hid behind a taped-up USPS RATES sign.

As she took the plate, the reflection took something back. The washed-out head turned toward where she'd been standing in the glass, a beat late, the way a sleeper turns toward a sound in the next room.

Inside, Rodel looked up from his ledger and very slightly shook his head. Then he came out with a push broom and swept, slowly and squarely, his back to them, between his door and the curb.

At the far corner they turned and looked straight back down Mission, with no glass in the way.

The Camry was there, pollen on the roof. The driver's seat was empty. No door had opened.

In the florist's front bucket, one white chrysanthemum had turned on its stem to face them.

* * *

LUPE'S, said the door. Inside were six booths, a counter, and one lemon meringue left in the pie case. The waitress didn't ask their names. She turned two mugs over and poured, then frowned as if she'd miscounted and set a third mug at the empty place beside Kino, handle out, and filled it.

"For your friend. He said he'd be right in."

The steam off the third cup didn't rise. It leaned toward the window, like a candle flame in a draft.

The bell over the door rang, and Malachi came down the aisle in a Raiders windbreaker zipped to the throat, a fresh scrape along his jaw. He stopped a step short of the booth, looking at the mug.

"I didn't order that. I didn't know I was coming in until I saw your ponytail through the glass. Who's sitting there?"

"That one's for your friend, hon," Lupe called from the grill. "He already paid. Nice old man. Counted it out in Spanish."

Kino put himself between the empty seat and Arenya and waved Malachi in on the wall side. "Don't pick it up. Don't drink it."

"Seven-K-B-Q-four-R," Arenya said. "One character hidden. I'd like to trade."

"Q fits. That's a real string." He kept his voice under the grill noise. "Nobody hit me, by the way. That's the lie I could afford. I ran a plate under my own name last night, and the log says *never issued*, and anyone with access can follow it back to my badge. I walked through Colma in a stranger's jacket to see who'd come close enough to read it."

"Not DOE," she said.

"Not in a room where the coffee has an opinion."

The steam had bent around Kino's hip the way water goes around a post. It crossed the table a finger's width above the Formica and stopped over her open notebook. It didn't touch the page. It read it.

"Close that book," Malachi said quietly.

She lowered the cover like a lid on a pot. "Closed. Now it's reading the cover. Dog breeds, mostly."

Lupe laid a green guest check face down by Arenya's hands. "He said give this to the girl. No charge. Said he'd be back when it's cold. Don't clear it."

Through the thin paper, the pencil showed in reverse. **3 COF.** And in the little box marked SERVER, a single upright stroke in soft grey pencil.

The heat in Arenya's palm *pulled* toward it. One stroke beside one stroke. *Dos testigos.* An old man in a cardigan was offering to be her second.

She was so tired. That was the thing. She was so tired, and it was free, and it was already paid for.

"Don't put your thumb on it," Kino said. He'd crouched at the edge of the booth, level with her. "He's not offering a witness. He's offering his hand in your entry. The clerk never asked who the second would be. It would count Bell." His eyes went from the check to her face. "That's what a trap looks like when it's polite. It pulls the way I'd want to be pulled if I were tired. If you want a second stroke, it's Nana, with her own pencil. It isn't him."

"I know." She turned her palm down on the Formica. "No," she said softly, to the check, the cup, whoever was leaning on the other end. "I'm not taking an offer from a man who counts me in without asking."

In the third mug, the steam went straight up, thin and ordinary.

"Huh," said Lupe. "Guess he's not coming back."

Arenya slid the check into a napkin with her pen cap so her skin never touched it. Evidence isn't agreement. She'd have liked to see the ledger argue.

"You owe me a real name," she told Malachi.

"Later. Nana's freezer is the only post office I trust."

On Mission the Camry was gone. Where it had stood, the pollen had settled into a clean rectangle the exact size of a car.

"A polite trap," Arenya said. "The worst kind. I'd write a listicle about it."

"*Seven Signs Your Free Coffee Is Haunted*," said Kino.

"Don't. I'll use it."

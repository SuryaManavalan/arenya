# Chapter Sixteen: Don't Be Rude About the Chili

At the same hour, on a cold rock above the East Peak fire road, Kino Balam felt the mountain turn over.

Nothing moved. Not a pebble. But he felt it through his palms and up his spine, slow and enormous, a sleeper turning in a bed she'd lain in for ten thousand years. She turned her face toward the city, toward something lit down there under the fog.

Her back came around to him.

The mark on his left forearm went cold.

Nana picked up before the first ring finished. "Mijo. I felt it. Like the bed tipped." Behind her the kettle began to scream, and she ignored it. "Listen to me. She didn't turn her *back*. You're thinking like a boy whose mother looked at the baby. She turned her *face*. Something new came into the house. A big fire. Old." A pause. "Is she worried or is she curious? That's your work. Not *why not me*. I can hear you thinking it from here."

He'd smelled something on the wind just before. Burnt butter, and something sharp and medicinal, like a church that wasn't his.

He didn't know yet that it was ghee and camphor. Or that the woman it belonged to had drunk tea at his grandmother's table every Thursday for eleven years, in the chair across from his.

He started down the mountain in the dark.

* * *

At 5:48, in the Inner Sunset, Ava Lindqvist woke with her face wet and both hands cupped in front of her, as if she were holding something that would go out if she breathed on it.

There was nothing in them.

She'd dreamed of a woman made of lamps, and four men with a net. Her palms were warm, the way they get after a mug, and she hadn't held a mug.

In the kitchen the oven clock read **2:41** and wasn't flashing. Neither was the microwave. They'd lost power and decided to stop instead of reset.

The oven door was black glass. In it, her reflection held a single gold flame in its cupped hands.

She looked down. Her own hands hung at her sides, empty.

She looked back. The flame was still there.

"Okay," she whispered. "Hypotheses." She filmed it at two percent battery, narrating like a protocol, because her voice held steady when it had a job. She stepped left. She crouched. A hallucination would drift.

It didn't drift. It was perfectly, insultingly consistent.

Past her reflected shoulder, the window wasn't her window. A brass lamp on a low shelf. Browning marigolds. An old woman's hand, a ring on its smallest finger, reaching for the wick to pinch it out.

The hand stopped.

It did not pinch.

The oven clock jumped to **5:52**. The glass showed only a tired woman in a sleep shirt.

But her palms stayed warm. Not on the skin. Under it.

"Don't go out," Ava said to nobody. "I've got you."

* * *

Seton's emergency doors opened half a second too slowly, as if they were thinking it over.

The family room was beige chairs and a dying ficus. A muted TV ran an infomercial for a knife that could cut through a shoe. Amma sat in her nightdress and Appa's PG&E fleece, plait half undone, holding a paper cup of water she hadn't touched. Appa stood at the window with his back to the room. Arenya could tell from his shoulders that he wasn't going to sit down.

On the chair beside Amma, wrapped in a hospital blanket like a child, was Susmithaa Aunty's brass lamp. A crack fine as a hair ran down the glass chimney.

"They let me keep it," Amma said into Arenya's shoulder. "I wouldn't give it to them. Is that stupid?"

"It's not stupid." Arenya's throat closed on the second word, and she waited it out. "Nobody's taking it."

Through the open door she could see the nurses' station. A man in a dark wool coat leaned on the counter, tall and very still, much too awake for four in the morning. He held a lanyard face-down under two fingers.

Arenya knew the coat.

"Kanna. Why are you holding your wrist?"

She hadn't known she was. She'd held it the whole drive.

"Steering wheel," she said, too fast. "Pinched a nerve."

She made herself let go. The cold stayed anyway, a coin pressed into the skin. Her right hand stayed shut, so she hugged her mother with forearms and chin, like someone carrying groceries.

*Don't look, love.* Abernathy stood at her shoulder, flickering at the edges. *That bloke in the coat, he's got no light on him at all. Not hidden. Not shuttered. Nothing. Like a hole in a fence you could put your hand through.* A pause, and something older crept under the next words. *I've never seen a livin' man like that. And I don't like how it itches, that I might have.*

The nurse said something short and final. The man tucked the lanyard away, straightened, and looked down the hall. At the lamp in its blanket. At her.

He stopped two paces inside the doorway, as far as courtesy allowed, and kept his hands where the family could see them.

"Mrs. Sowri. Mr. Sowri. I'm sorry for your loss, and I'm sorry about the hour. My name is Shawani. Department of Energy." He showed the card for exactly as long as it took to read. Then his eyes came to Arenya. Too clear. "Ms. Sowri. I won't ask you anything in front of your mother. I'll be by the vending machines."

"You were in my apartment on Friday," she said, low and flat, the voice she kept for strangers who called her *hon*. "You told me to stop. Now you're at my aunt's deathbed with a lanyard." She tightened her arm around Amma. "Ten minutes with my mother. Then you can buy me a terrible coffee and give me names. I take notes. And don't come any closer to that lamp."

"Ten minutes." He didn't argue. "The coffee here is worse than you're imagining."

He inclined his head toward Appa's back, the small bow of a man leaving a house, and went.

Arenya sat Amma down, crouched in front of her, and took the paper cup out of her hands before it crushed.

"Drink some, Amma, or I'll make you eat the ficus." Amma almost laughed, which was the point. "Tell me what Aunty said last. Anything. Don't edit."

Amma drank like a child taking medicine.

"Dinner was Sunday. She made too much rasam, as always. She was silly-happy. Her Thursday friend was teaching her tamales, the grandmother in Daly City. Itzel." She sounded the name out carefully, the way Aunty must have. "She said, *If I die first, Itzel will come with pozole, and you must eat it and not be rude about the chili.* I told her not to say such things at the table." Her mouth worked. "That was the last thing. *Don't be rude about the chili.*"

Behind them, a chair scraped.

Appa had turned from the window. His face was grey and dry. He sat two chairs away, big hands loose between his knees, and spoke to the floor.

"In March she called me at work. She never called me at work. She said, *Ravi, if an American ever comes asking about my niece, you tell him there is no niece.* I said, which niece? She has only one." He lifted his eyes. "She said, *Exactly.* And she hung up." A breath. "Tonight I told him there is no niece. I am a very bad liar, kanna."

Arenya didn't write it down, because Amma would see. She said it back to herself instead, twice, the way she memorized quotes. *March. No niece. Exactly. Itzel. Pozole.*

She moved to the chair beside him and took his hand in her left.

"You're a bad liar because you love somebody, Appa. It's the only kind of bad I'd want."

Amma patted her purse, where a plastic hospital bag had been stuffed. "Her phone is in here. The nurse said it kept buzzing at three o'clock. I couldn't look. You look, later." Then, with the certainty of scripture: "But she said Itzel will come. So she will come. We will have to clean the house."

Arenya's knee had come to rest against the lamp. The brass under the blanket was warm. Not body-warm. Warm the way a chair is when someone has been sitting in it a long time and just stood up.

For a second, less, she would have sworn the warmth moved toward her closed right fist.

* * *

She took the nearer coffee without thanking him. She didn't drink it.

"Names. Start with who sent you to my aunt."

"Nobody sent me." Malachi kept his back to the wall and his eyes on the family-room door. "Her name was on a list I keep. People who travel to odd places and keep odd company. I put it there in September. I never knocked on her door." A beat. "That's mine to carry."

"And the name you promised?"

"Quinn. Adonis Quinn, who died at Tartine. He worked for Monolith, and Monolith's founder is his cousin. I don't know that they're the ones doing this. I know your wall had a man in a grey jacket on it, and my people have three more like him. And I know somebody reached this hospital before your family did." He paused. "It wasn't me. I got here second."

His eyes dropped to her wrist.

"One for one, Ms. Sowri. Don't say the steering wheel. I watched you drive in."

"Wow. You watched me drive in. That's either surveillance or a crush, and I'm not sure which is worse."

She turned the wrist over. On the inside, a pale circle the size of a coin, a little greyer than the skin around it. No bruise.

"I woke up with it at 3:19. It's been cold for an hour." She didn't hold it out. "Who are *your people*?"

He looked at the mark just long enough to memorize it. "I haven't seen that before. I'd tell you if I had. Don't let a doctor photograph it, or anyone with a clipboard." Then: "My people are three analysts and a phone. A cell, not a department. Nobody upstairs would sign for it if I fell off the earth tonight."

"So you're me with a lanyard. Great. Bronze medal."

She tore a corner off a clean page and wrote her burner number. She held it out and didn't let go when he reached for it.

"One more. You said nine. Is Susmithaa Sowri the first of them to die, or the first you *noticed*? I'll know the difference by your hands."

His hands stayed open and still. He let her read them.

"I mixed two numbers. That was careless. Nine are the dead. Freeze deaths. None of them were on any list of mine beforehand, and I think that's the whole problem. The other list is people my analysts call *unusual*. Your aunt is the first name on it to die." His eyes went to the door and back. "And she didn't freeze. No fixed stare, no seizure. Sitting by a tree in her cardigan. Whoever did this either changed method or did something I haven't seen. I don't like either answer."

She let go of the paper.

"I'll call once, tomorrow. I'll say *it's me* and a time. If you don't answer, I won't call again." A pause. "I'm sorry about your aunt. I mean that more than I'd like to."

She'd watched his hands the whole time. They hadn't moved. She hated that they were the first thing about him she believed.

"She didn't freeze," she said. "Write that down, Mr. Shawani. If I find out that's why you stopped looking, I'm going to be very unpleasant."

When she glanced back from the family-room door, the corridor held two coffees on a vending machine and nobody else.

She hadn't heard him go.

Amma had fallen asleep sitting up, her head on Appa's shoulder. Arenya sat on the other side of the lamp and laid her left palm on the blanket.

Still warm.

At 5:51 the window went the color of weak tea. In her bag, Aunty's phone buzzed once, short, like a knock, and went quiet.

She didn't take it out. Not yet.

But her right fist, for the first time since the garden, uncurled a quarter-inch on its own. It loosened the way a hand does when it has decided it's safe enough to sleep.

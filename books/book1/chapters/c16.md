# Chapter Sixteen: Per Family

Nana drove slow and boring, like she'd been asked to.

Mission Street. A bus. A man selling mangoes off a tailgate. Three cemeteries, and then, because this was Colma's idea of a joke, four.

At the red light on the Daly City line, the dashboard clock rolled over to 12:00.

The phone in Arenya's pocket buzzed once against her hip. It didn't ring. It ticked, the way a clock ticks.

Nobody said anything about it.

* * *

The kitchen smelled the way it had yesterday: hominy, oregano, the chili Aunty had said not to be rude about. Nana shot the deadbolt and set the chain. Then, without explaining, she knelt with a grunt and poured a line of salt under the back door from the blue Morton can.

She put the pot on to warm. She pulled out a chair for each of them. She sat down herself, hands folded, and didn't touch the pot.

Arenya laid the phone face up on the oilcloth, between the salt shaker and a jar of Tajín. *Missed call. Unknown. 12:00 PM.* Under that was the last thing she'd opened: the photo of the cream envelope in Box 112.

"Kino," she said. "It's yours. Like I asked."

He pulled it toward him with one finger.

She watched his hands, because his face never told her anything. He read once with his thumb held off the glass. Then he read again with it on, and something went through him so quietly she'd have missed it if she hadn't been counting his breaths. His left sleeve sat very still over his forearm.

"First time, it's what you said." His voice was low and even, the voice for somebody standing too close to a drop. "Cream envelope. Typewriter caps. Your name, all of it, middle name too. I didn't know you had one. I'm not going to say it in here. Neither should you. *To the beneficiary. Collected in person. ID required.*"

He turned the phone face down, like a card he didn't want to play.

"Second time, there's another line. It wasn't there before. It's not blurry like the rest. It sits on top of the picture like frost." He looked at Nana, who would know what it meant, and then at Arenya, because it was hers. "It isn't written to you. It's written to me. To whoever reads it to you."

Nobody breathed.

"I'm not going to say it. I'll write it down for Nana, or you can look yourself. But 'the one who reads it to her' is a job. If I say it out loud, in a room, with you listening, I think I take the job." He tipped his head toward nothing. "The clerk asked me *¿Y tú?* and I didn't answer. This is them asking again, in different handwriting."

*Abuela*, he said. *Tell me I'm wrong.*

Nana didn't tell him he was wrong.

She reached into the sugar bowl for the pencil stub she kept there for grocery lists, tore the back flap off a PG&E envelope, and slid both across.

"*Escríbelo.* Your Susmithaa said it right. Writing is not keeping. *Saying* is how they get you." She saw Arenya's face and softened, but didn't take a word back. "In Colma the old people know. When the mail comes for the dead, somebody has to sign for it, and whoever signs gets the next one too. That is what *c/o* means to them. Care of. Care is a job."

Kino wrote. Nana read it without moving her lips, folded it twice, and put it in the freezer, in the same Ziploc as the grey man's card, between the frozen tortillas and a bag of peas.

"Two things in my freezer with their handwriting," she said. "I don't like it."

Then she turned the phone face up again and slid it halfway to Arenya, and stopped with her fingers still on it.

"Kino won't take the job. Good. Smart. But it's still your mail. You can look yourself, and then nobody read it to you. Or you leave it, and we eat. I won't say which." A small crooked smile. "*Ella* would have looked. *Ella* also told the insurance man to go to hell in three languages, so."

The pozole ticked on the stove.

Arenya put two fingers on the phone, beside Nana's. She didn't pull it closer.

Every instinct she owned was pointing at it. Her whole life she'd wanted to be the girl who looked, the one who found the cracks in the world where the truth leaked out. Here was one, lying on an oilcloth with a rooster print.

*Don't verify,* Aunty had written. In her own hand, in her last week.

"Looking is verifying," Arenya said. It came out clipped, so she made it slower. "I think looking is the first stroke. And I think whatever's on that screen knows it."

She slid the phone back to the middle, face down, and set the salt shaker on top of it like a paperweight. It wasn't a plan. It was what she had.

"Not at this table. Not with the pozole on, and not with Kino's arm that cold. But I'm not ignoring it either. Mail that gets ignored just keeps coming." To Kino: "Thank you for not reading it out. I know what that cost." To Nana: "We eat. Then we write down everything we *can* say out loud, in order, and see what shape the hole is. I'm good at holes."

Under the table, the cold *12* sat in her right palm. She closed her fist on it.

*Good call, love,* Abernathy said, close in her ear, from the chair nobody else could see. *Salt shaker on it. Daft, and right. When Kino turned that phone over I got brass again. Cooling, like a pan off the ring, and wet ink under it, same as the notary's box. Whoever wrote that line wrote it to be* read. *It wants a reader more than it wants you.*

A pause, with something older in it.

*And since I owe you. That voice that said* mark it so it can be found again*. It sounded like me. I can't tell you more. Someone built a wall round it, and I don't know if it was them or me. It's not a nice thing, not knowing.*

She opened her fist a quarter inch, so he'd know she'd heard, and closed it again.

* * *

They ate. For six minutes nobody talked, and it was the best six minutes of the week.

Then Nana brought out a yellow legal pad from the drawer with the batteries and the rubber bands, and two pens, because she didn't trust one.

At 12:41 the phone under the shaker buzzed once. The screen didn't light. But the spilled grains around the shaker had gone dull and grey, like salt on a winter windshield, in a perfect circle the size of the phone.

Nana put a fresh pinch on top of the grey. "Write, mija. In order."

Arenya wrote the way she always did: numbered, block capitals, no adjectives. Adjectives are where people hide.

**1. MARCH 9.** MERIDIAN LETTER. AUNTY = 11 (INSURED). ME = 12 (BENEFICIARY). SHE NEVER BOUGHT A POLICY.
**2. MARCH 19, MORNING.** AUNTY'S DECLARATION AT RODEL'S. NO CONTRACT, NO AGREEMENT, NO UNDERSTANDING. APPA WITNESSED.
**3. MARCH 19, ONE HOUR LATER.** HALE RENTS BOX 112. CASH.
**4. TODAY.** BOX LEFT OPEN. ENVELOPE INSIDE, TO ME. BELL FILED FOR THE 3/19 JOURNAL AND WALKED IN AFTER US.
**5. YARD.** ROW 11, AUNTY'S NO IN THE MARGIN. ROW 12, ONE STROKE, WAITING. *FALTA UNA.*

She drew an empty box under line 5.

"Here's the hole. On March ninth, Aunty's already in the book. Nobody counts a person that early unless they plan to be the cause, or plan to be right."

Kino turned the pad a quarter toward him and took the second pen. Under line 3, in a plain trail-log hand: **HALE = same dry count as the voice on the voicemails. Older than Hale. He's the mouthpiece.** Under line 4: **BELL watches. Box left open = bait.** Then, boxed beside hers:

**WHO TOLD THEM MARCH 9?**

"That's the hole," he said. "Not what they want from you. Who gave them the date." His eyes went to the freezer once, and away. "And I'd want to know if cold paper counts as a post office."

Nana was out of her chair before he finished.

The freezer light was yellow and ordinary. The Ziploc wasn't. Its inside had grown a fur of frost so thick you couldn't read the card through it, and when she lifted it out it was heavier than paper had any right to be. She set it in a saucer and poured salt over it until it was a small white hill.

"I put it in there because cold keeps things. My mother did that with letters she didn't want to answer." She shook her head once. "*Pendeja.*"

Then she went, not to the freezer, but to the wall by the phone jack, where a free parish calendar from Our Lady of Mercy hung on a nail. She lifted October. September. Back and back until MARZO, and held the pages flat against the wall with her palm.

Monday the 9th was in black. The Sunday before it was in red.

**8.**

Arenya had read the pink temple receipt herself, in the bookroom, with the dust. *Livermore. Archana. Second Sunday in March.* And under *Nakshatra*, her own birth star.

A priest reads those lines aloud. That's what an archana is. A name and a star, said into a room.

She wrote *8* in the empty box, slowly, so her hand wouldn't shake.

"March eighth. Aunty went to Livermore the day before the letter. She had them say my name and my star out loud, for her." She put the pen down. "She wasn't signing anything. She was *praying* for me. And somebody heard it." Her throat tightened. "Her kindness was the leak. It's not a hole. It's a door she held open for me."

Nobody said *I'm sorry.* Nana's hand came across the table and covered her closed one, and stayed.

"You're not reaching about the door," Kino said, after a while. "But she didn't leak it, Ari. She *gave* it. To a god, for you. Somebody was just standing at the back of the room with a pencil."

*Ari.* Nobody called her that but her aunts. She let it stand.

* * *

What to do with the white hill on the saucer took longer.

Kino wanted running water and open air: the Pacifica pier, tide going out, daylight, Ix. "*Agua que corre,*" Nana agreed, and then laid down her mother's rule. You only carry *your own* letter to the water. Carry somebody else's and now you're the mailman. Kino's note was his. The grey man's card was not theirs to drown.

"You write *REHUSADO* on it," Nana said, eyes hard and bright, the look of a woman who had fought utility companies for forty years. "Refused. You mail it back to his box. Then it's a no he has to *receive.*" The brightness dimmed. She was honest. "But then he knows we know his box."

"It's a tracker," Arenya said. "I'm not putting it in a mailbox with our handwriting on it. That's handing him a receipt." She thought. "Malachi said he'd call. Once. He knows what Bell is, or says he does. Let him carry the card back. It's his kind of problem."

Out on the street a car went slowly *down* the hill. Kino, at the edge of the window, saw it this time.

"Beige sedan. Doesn't stop."

The brake lights tapped once at the corner. The way you'd tap a pen.

He didn't let anybody run for the back. He took out Nana's trash instead, a grey bag heavy with pozole bones, at the pace of a man who hated Wednesdays. He dropped the lid harder than he needed to. He looked downhill at nothing, at the corner, at the sky. His eyes crossed a beige bumper and kept moving.

"Seven K," he said, when the chain was back on. "A letter, B or an 8, the glare ate it. A gap. A four. And the R." He tapped her left wrist, where she'd written *7K_R* in ballpoint two nights ago. "Same R. Chrome dealer frame. SERRAMONTE AUTO."

"Half a plate and a dealership," Arenya said, "is a sentence I can finish."

Then the side yard went quiet.

It wasn't a sound. It was the lack of one: the wind dropping off, all at once, in the concrete strip between Nana's house and the neighbor's. In the laundry room, where Nana had gone with the salt, the pouring stopped.

"*Ay, no.* Mija. Come see. From the door. Don't step."

Across the side path, eight inches off the ground, a line had been ruled from the fence post to the gas meter. Thinner than fishing line, white as hoarfrost, perfectly level. It ran straight across the doorstep of the one door nobody had salted.

Outside, the beige car pulled away from the curb without hurry, signaled left like a man taking a driving test, and was gone.

"In my house," Nana said. "He drew on my house."

Arenya crouched a full step back from the sill and sighted along the line without letting her breath near it. Level. Not a hair off. She hated how much she respected that.

"Don't step. Don't cut it. Don't pour on it. Cutting is a mark too." She photographed it, no flash, with the meter in frame so the position was on record. "A ruled line is a ledger line. Somebody is counting the doors in this house."

"It's the one you didn't think of, Nana," Kino said, a hand on her shoulder. "That's what they were counting on. You didn't miss it. They knew."

Nana's mouth worked. She patted his hand twice, hard, like she was tenderizing it.

"My mother said a door that isn't a door can't be counted. You don't argue with the line. You make it a wall."

It took ten minutes, and Kino's objections about her back, and the old dresser from the garage, forty years of Christmas lights in its stuck drawer, walked across the hall a foot at a time until its back sat flush against the side door. On top, Nana set a plaster Virgen de Guadalupe.

"There. Now it's furniture. Count furniture, *cabrón.*"

Ix came in through the front, a long low shape most people's eyes slid off of. It didn't go to the dresser. It went to the window over the dryer and looked at the frost for a long time with its ears flat. Then it turned its head, very deliberately, toward the *other* end of the line. The gas meter. The grey box with its little dials.

Arenya saw Kino see it. Neither of them said what it meant. They didn't know.

At 2:15 the salt on the saucer went grey to the top and stopped, as if it were full.

At 3:30 the four of them went out the front of a house with a dresser against one of its doors, and Arenya kept her right hand shut the whole way down the walk.

* * *

Highway 1 ran south with the fog sitting offshore like a wall nobody had built.

The Pacifica pier went a quarter mile straight out into the grey, concrete and railings, bait and diesel and kelp. At four-thirty on a Wednesday it belonged to the regulars: crabbers with their rings and orange buckets, a woman in a puffy coat feeding the gulls something she shouldn't, an old Filipino man with three rods and a radio playing the Giants pregame.

Nana sat down on a bench at the foot of the pier with her purse in her lap.

"*Your* letter," she told Kino. "I watch. I don't carry."

He went out with Ix padding along the railing, and the crabbers stepped around it without knowing why. Arenya followed half a step behind. Crabbers. Puffy coat. A kid with a bucket. No grey cardigans. She counted them anyway, and hated that she did.

At the end, where the swell ran hardest out, Kino took the page from inside his jacket. He'd fished it out of the salt with Nana's tongs; it had gone soft and damp again. He didn't unfold it. That was the whole point.

"Nana didn't fail," he said to the water, Spanish first, then English. "They knew. That's the only thing I'm sending."

He held it flat on his palm over the rail so the wind couldn't have it before the tide did. Then he turned his hand over.

Not thrown. Given.

The page lay on the green water for one heartbeat, as if it were being read. Then the swell folded over it and took it under the pilings.

Arenya put two fingers on his sleeve, over the cold forearm, for about a second.

"Okay," she said. "That's one of us out of the count. Bronze medal for the rest."

He pushed the sleeve back. The birthmark was still there, the Lady's thumbprint, still cool. But where her fingers had been, the skin was warm. Plain skin-warm. And it wasn't fading.

"It's just a birthmark," he said, like he didn't trust it. "Nothing's pulling."

On the old man's radio a man read the tide table in a flat, pleasant voice. *Low water four-fifty, minus point three, Sharp Park.* It was the most normal sentence she'd heard all week.

Halfway back, she saw that Nana wasn't alone on the bench.

A man sat at the far end, a respectful foot of slats between them. Navy overcoat over a suit, no tie. A paper cup from the bait shop. Fortyish, clean-shaven, a pleasant face you'd forget on the way out of the room. He was angled toward the water, like a stranger making small talk about the weather.

Nana had both hands on the clasp of her purse. She was looking straight down the pier at them. She lifted two fingers off the clasp. Barely. Then put them back.

Arenya's stomach dropped through the planks.

The man turned, found her face, and smiled. An apologetic smile. *Sorry I missed you at noon.* He set his coffee down on the slats and waited, like someone who'd budgeted all the time in the world.

"That's Hale," Kino said, barely moving his mouth. "Nana's two fingers. Don't say your name. Don't take anything. Not a cup, not a card, not a pen."

*Guv.* Close and slightly too loud against the inside of her ear. *Brass, same as Box 112, only fresher, like a stamp still wet. That's the voicemails. And he smells of nothing, love. Sat by the sea an hour and it never touched him. His watch is running a few seconds slow on purpose. Man who's never late because he's never* on *the time the rest of us are on. Don't let him set the pace. Narrow questions. Whole sentences. And don't thank him. Thanking's agreeing.*

They stopped two steps short of the bench. Close enough for Nana to touch, far enough to stay outside his foot of slats. Arenya gave him the flat, polite face she used on telemarketers.

"You're sitting with my grandmother's friend. That's the first thing I don't like. Who are you, and why are you on this pier?"

He nodded, a little rueful, as if she'd made a fair point in a meeting.

"Daniel Hale. Meridian Mutual." No card. No hand. Both of his stayed on his knees, where Kino could see them. "And you're right, I shouldn't have sat here. Mrs. Lucero was kind enough not to tell me to leave."

Nana flinched.

It was small, and Arenya had never seen her do it. Not at the frost, not at the beige car. It was the flinch of someone hearing a name out of a grave.

"I'm here because you didn't come at noon, and I understood why. A library is my room. This isn't anyone's." He tipped his head at the buckets, the kid. "Witnesses. A good idea." To Kino, warmly: "And congratulations. That was well done, out at the end. Posted, not thrown. Your matter's closed, as far as anyone I work with is concerned."

Then to Arenya, in the register of someone delivering bad news at a kitchen table.

"Your aunt had an arrangement she disputed, Ms. Sowri. She did it very properly, in writing. But a disputed entry doesn't stay open forever, and how it closes isn't my decision. There's a gentleman in a grey cardigan who filed paperwork about you this morning. He's less patient than I am, and he doesn't sit on benches." The coat hung a little heavy on one side, over the flat rectangle of an envelope. "I'm not going to hand you anything. I'm not allowed to. Ask me whatever you like. I don't lie. It's bad for business."

"You know the word *posted*," Kino said, flat. "You know my grandmother's name, and nobody gave it to you. Polite isn't honest."

Arenya waited one swell. Then she said it the way she'd read a quote back to a source.

"Narrow, then. You said 'a disputed entry.' Who entered it, and by whose authority? Second: did Meridian Mutual rent Box 112 one hour after my aunt's declaration was notarized, yes or no? Third: if it isn't your decision how it closes, whose is it?" She looked at his watch, then his face. "And I'm not taking anything, I'm not agreeing to anything, and nothing you say gets thanked. I'm listening. So are they."

He gave a small nod of real professional approval, the look of a man who rarely got asked good ones.

"In order. Who entered it: your aunt did. Sunday, March eighth, the temple in Livermore. She had a priest say your name and your birth star aloud and ask that you be *kept*." He let the word sit. "She meant protected. In the book it's the same word.

"Nobody *makes* entries, Ms. Sowri. The clerk you met in Colma writes down what's true. I only read it. I'm a reader with a briefcase.

"The box: yes. Fifty-nine minutes after her declaration. I wanted to be close to the record. I'm not ashamed of that.

"Whose decision: no one's, which is the problem. Two strokes keep a dispute open. One stroke runs out. Hers runs out on her sixteenth day, when your family finishes mourning her and lets her go. Then the entry closes as written, and whoever has standing collects." His eyes slid to Kino. "The gentleman is Mr. Bell. He filed for *verification of beneficiary*. If the notary gives him that journal, he doesn't need you to say your name. He can verify you himself. What he does after that I won't describe with a child twenty feet away. I watched him do it in March."

He touched the heavy side of his coat once. "The envelope in the box is a copy, so you'd know I was serious. This one closes the entry *in your favor*. You receive. Mr. Bell has nothing to collect." A pause. "May I ask one thing back? Do you know what your aunt was carrying? I don't think you do."

"Sixteenth day," Kino said, low. "Thank you for the straight answer."

Arenya's whole body went still.

"*No contestes lo de su tía,*" he added, almost nothing. "*Es cebo.*" *Don't answer about your aunt. It's bait.*

She'd already decided. She took out the coffee-stained notebook and uncapped a pen, so he could watch her not write his name.

"No. I'm not answering that, and I'm not trading. Here's what I heard, in front of witnesses. Sixteen days. A book that writes down what's true. A man who reads it, a man who collects from it, and a closed entry that 'receives' me whether I agree or not. I don't agree. Put that on your record, since you're fond of records." She clicked the pen. "Narrow. If I never take your envelope and never sign, and Bell never gets that journal, does it still close on day sixteen? And is there any way it stays open that doesn't involve somebody standing for me?"

"You're welcome," Hale said pleasantly, to Kino. As if it were nothing.

On the radio the tide voice skipped one word, like a needle.

"Noted. You don't agree. I'll carry that." He didn't write it down. "Yes, it still closes. Not taking the envelope only keeps it from closing in your favor. And there's one other way, though I've never seen it done. What your aunt did, she did out loud, in a particular room, through a particular man. Something said in a room can sometimes be *unsaid* in the same room. I wouldn't know how. I don't go into temples." His eyes found hers. "Be careful who you ask there. Rooms where names get said aloud are popular with more than one kind of listener."

He stood and buttoned the coat over the heavy pocket. "Mrs. Lucero. Ms. Sowri. Sir. The box is open until Friday, seven-forty. After that I'm only a phone number."

He walked up the pier a little out of step with the surf.

Nana let out a breath she'd been holding since before they got there.

"Lucero," she said, very quietly. "Mija. Nobody living calls me that. It's on the paper for the house. It's on my husband's stone." Her knuckles were white on the clasp. "I never told him my name."

Kino crouched in front of her and took her purse hand. "*No le diste tu nombre, Nana. Él ya lo tenía.*" *You didn't give it. He already had it.* "He got it somewhere that counts things."

"He reads books," Arenya said, taking the other hand. "He read you in one. It isn't a door you left open."

Over Nana's head, Kino said, without looking up: "I thanked him. Reflex. I felt it get written. Right on my arm, small, like a receipt coming off a pad. I'm putting it on the table. Next time I say nothing at all."

"It's on the table," she said. "Don't carry it alone."

In the lot, Hale's footprints were there on the damp asphalt, faint and perfect, each exactly the same distance from the last, like marks on a ruler. They stopped at an empty parking space. No oil stain. No tire marks. As if no car had ever parked there.

Arenya opened the notebook and wrote one line under the wind. *Livermore. Said aloud in a room → unsaid in the same room? Who else listens there?*

"Sixteen days," Nana said, to herself, as they lifted her into the Corolla. "I'll make the pozole for the sixteenth. She told me to."

Behind them, past the end of the pier, the tide bottomed out at four-fifty and turned.

* * *

Nana Itzel taped a sheet of butcher paper to her fridge that night and wrote on it in the square hand she used for church raffles.

*Fri 30 — 7:40 — the box.*

Then a long blank.

Then, circled twice, *Mar* for *martes*: *Mar 10 Nov — día dieciséis.* And under it, smaller: *pozole. chile aparte.*

Thirteen days. She set the marker down like it weighed more than a marker.

Out in the side yard, the frost line had melted into a wet stripe on the concrete. It was perfectly straight.

* * *

Arenya was home by six, in her own kitchen, with Amma making rasam and pretending not to watch her.

She noticed it washing her hands. Under the frost *12* and its rule, the pencil dot had changed. It wasn't a dot anymore. It was a short faint line, like a stroke someone had started and then lifted the pen from.

She photographed it the way she'd log any evidence, and her hands weren't quite steady doing it. In the notebook, under *Wednesday. Thirteen days*, she wrote: *It's not waiting for me anymore. Somebody started.*

*Look, don't touch,* Abernathy said, somewhere near the ceiling, and for once he didn't sound like he was performing. *That's not the clerk, love. He's tidy as a bank. This is someone leaning in and stopping. Like they heard something, or got told to wait.* The cockney thinned for half a word. *Part of me knows what an unfinished stroke wants. A second hand to agree with it. Don't offer yours. Not even to be polite.*

She texted Kino. *Come over. Bring Ix. The line on my hand moved.*

* * *

Malachi read the forwarded tip twice in his car at the top of the Sowris' hill.

*Elderly resident reports a man in a grey cardigan "drawing on her garage." No damage. Responding officer notes a "very straight water stain."*

He didn't call the officer. A second query left a second trail, and his own people read trails. He watched the street instead. A man with a takeout bag. A recycling bin blown over. Then a young man in a hood parked two doors down from the Sowris', looked over the hedges for a long time, and knocked. Twice, then once.

Malachi wrote the pattern down.

At 8:41 he took out the burner and made the call he'd promised her. Six words and a time.

"It's me. Ten o'clock. Somewhere with people—"

He hung up. He was sure of it.

He didn't notice his own rear window. A line of frost had been drawn across the glass from edge to edge, very thin and perfectly straight. It had started forming while he read the tip. Under the streetlight it was already beginning to sweat.

* * *

"It's Kino," said the voice through the wood. "Two and one."

She checked the peephole, and the hedges behind him, and pulled him in by the sleeve. Ix flowed past her ankles into the hall like a shadow that had somewhere to be.

Kino leaned over her palm with his hands clasped behind his back, like a man at a museum. He didn't breathe on it.

"That's not graphite. It sits *on* the line, like it was gone over once, lightly, by someone checking." His birthmark ached; she could see it in his face. "Somebody read it, Ari. Not wrote. Read."

"So we're being proofread."

Her phone buzzed face down on the counter. One call, unanswered. Then a voicemail.

Amma turned from the stove with the spoon in her hand. "Which one first, kanna?"

"Nobody listens alone," Arenya said. "Speaker. Low. And nobody answers it. If it counts, we let it count to itself."

Car-interior hiss. An engine idling. A man's voice, clipped and federal.

*"It's me. Ten o'clock. Somewhere with—"*

The hiss thinned. Very close to the microphone, closer than anyone in a car could be, a pen clicked shut.

A second voice, dry as a page being turned, in no hurry at all:

*"…doce. Leído."*

Then the first voice came back mid-word, as if nothing had cut it. *"—people. Don't bring the hand into a room you didn't pick."*

Nineteen seconds. It stopped.

A drop of rasam fell off Amma's spoon without a sound. In the hall, Ix had drawn its lips back from its teeth.

"That second voice said *leído*," Kino said, very low. "*Read.* Not us. *It.*"

Amma didn't ask why. She took down the salt, the cylinder with the blue girl on it, and poured a line along the sill over the sink. Too thick. She did everything too thick when she was frightened.

* * *

At the top of the hill, Malachi's call log said *0:19.* He had said six words. He checked again anyway, and didn't like how that sounded.

In the mirror, the frost line had finished sweating. Where it had been, a thin dry stroke marked the glass. A tally.

Someone had put a pen to his car while he sat in it.

He pulled the SIM and drove to a pay phone in Colma, across El Camino from a monument shop whose window said GRANITE · BRONZE · LETTERING in flaking gold leaf. Rodel Panganiban came out onto the sidewalk in his shirtsleeves with a push broom and began sweeping cypress needles off his stretch of concrete. Slowly. Squarely. His back to them, between his door and the curb.

He didn't call after them. He just swept.

At the far curb past the monument yard, Kino's hand jerked in hers. The pull hadn't faded. It had been cut, a line snipped clean.

They turned and looked straight back down Mission, with no glass in the way.

The Camry was there. Pollen on the roof.

The driver's seat was empty. No door had opened. Nobody on the sidewalk but Rodel and his broom.

In the front bucket of white chrysanthemums, one bloom had turned on its stem to face the corner where they stood.

"He doesn't need the car," Kino said. "It's a post. Somewhere to sit and be counted. That scares me more than him sitting in it."

"Next open door," she said. "Anything with a light on."

* * *

LUPE'S, said the door, and nothing on the menu said otherwise.

Six booths, a counter, a pie case with one lemon meringue left. The waitress was sixty, in orthopedic shoes, a pencil behind her ear. She didn't ask their names. She turned two mugs over and poured.

Then she frowned at the table as if she'd miscounted, took a third mug off the tray, set it at the empty place beside Kino with the handle turned out, the way you would for a regular, and filled it.

"For your friend. He said he'd be right in."

The steam off the third cup didn't rise. It leaned, toward the front window, like a candle flame in a draft, though the door was shut and the air in Lupe's was still.

The bell over the door rang.

Malachi came down the aisle in a Raiders windbreaker two sizes too big, zipped to the throat. There was a fresh scrape along his jaw. He stopped a step short of the booth, because he was looking at the mug.

"I didn't order that. I didn't know I was coming in until I saw your ponytail through the glass." His eyes followed the steam. "Who's sitting there?"

"That one's for your friend, hon," Lupe called from the grill. "He already paid." And, half to herself: "Nice old man. Counted it out in Spanish."

"Sit," Arenya said. "Not there. Kino, scoot."

Kino put himself between the empty seat and her and nodded Malachi into the wall side. "Don't pick it up. Don't drink it."

"Seven-K-B-Q-four-R," she told Malachi. "One character hidden. I'd like to trade."

He read the line in her notebook once. "Q fits. That's a real string. It goes to a clerk or a lawyer, not a cop. My name's on one query already."

The steam bent around Kino's hip the way water goes around a post, crossed the table a finger's width above the Formica, and stopped over her open notebook.

It didn't touch the page. It read it.

The stroke on her palm went from warm to hot.

"Close that book," Malachi said, very quietly. "Then I'll tell you who hit me."

She didn't snap it. Snapping is a reaction. She lowered the cover like a lid on a pot and looped the bag strap around her wrist. "Closed. Now it's reading the cover. Nice boring cover. Dog breeds, mostly."

The steam drew back toward its own mug, like a man sitting back in his chair.

"Nobody hit me," Malachi said, and she watched him decide. "That was the lie I could afford. I ran a plate under my own name last night. The log says *never issued*, and anyone with access can follow it back to a badge. I walked through Colma in a stranger's jacket to see who was close enough to read it."

"Not DOE," Arenya said.

"I'm not DOE. I'm not going to say more in a room where the coffee has an opinion." Lower: "Counting in fives, out loud, onto a desk. In my work that's a man who wants the sum witnessed. Whoever hears it is a party to it. Benny. Rodel. Her." A glance at the grill. "Entered without agreeing to anything."

Lupe came down the aisle with a green guest check from the spindle and laid it face down by Arenya's hands. "He said give this to the girl. No charge. Said he'd be back when it's cold. Don't clear it."

Through the thin paper the pencil showed in reverse. **3 COF.** And in the little box marked SERVER, where Lupe would have put her number, a single upright stroke in soft grey pencil.

The heat in Arenya's palm stopped burning. It *pulled.* Toward that stroke. One stroke beside one stroke. *Dos testigos.* An old man in a cardigan was offering to be her second.

She was so tired. That was the thing. She was so tired, and it was free, and it was already paid.

"Don't put your thumb on it," Kino said. He'd crouched at the booth's edge, level with her. "He's not offering you a witness. He's offering you his hand in your entry. The clerk never asked who the second would be. It would count Bell." His eyes went from the check to her face. "That's what a trap looks like when it's polite. It pulls the way I'd want to be pulled if I were tired. It's your row. But if you want a second stroke, it's Nana, with her own pencil. Or it's Livermore. It isn't him."

"I know." She turned her palm down on the Formica. "No," she said, very softly. To the check, the cup, whoever was leaning on the other end. "I'm not taking an offer from a man who counts me in without asking."

In the third mug, the steam went straight up, thin and ordinary.

"Huh," said Lupe, from the register. "Guess he's not coming back."

They left one at a time. Kino first, a ten under the sugar caddy. "Not for the coffee. For Lupe. She shouldn't be the one who owes." Arenya slid the green check into a napkin with her pen cap so her skin never met it and zipped it into her jacket pocket against her ribs. Evidence isn't agreement. Tell that to the ledger. She left her own ten under her mug.

"You owe me a real name," she told Malachi, barely moving her lips. "Later."

"Freezer," he said. "Nana's freezer is the only post office I trust."

Out on Mission, the Camry was gone. Where it had stood, the pollen on the curb had settled into a clean rectangle the exact size of a car. Every chrysanthemum faced the road again, all but one. That one faced Lupe's door.

Through the diner glass, a man in a Raiders jacket went out the kitchen side, left a twenty on the counter on his way, and headed uphill toward the cemetery road. He didn't look back.

"A polite trap," Arenya said. "That's the worst kind. I'd write a listicle about it."

"*Seven Signs Your Free Coffee Is Haunted,*" said Kino.

"Don't. I'll use it."

* * *

Nana's porch light was on, in daylight.

The kitchen table had been cleared to bare wood. Three things sat on it in a row, squared to the edge like a place setting: the red tin, the bowl of grey cal where the dead dime slept, and a yellow No. 2 pencil, sharpened so recently that the shavings still lay beside it. The pozole was already going.

"*Siéntense,*" Nana said, and didn't sit. "I talked to your tía all night, mija. In my head. She won." She tapped the pencil without picking it up. "I haven't said yes. I want to hear what you came to ask me, in your words. And I want to know what's warm in your pocket."

Arenya set the napkin on the wood, well away from the pencil. "A guest check from Lupe's. Bell's. One stroke in the SERVER box. He was offering to be my second. I said no out loud. It's still warm, and I don't like that I wanted to keep it."

She breathed.

"What I came to ask isn't *will you sign*. It's smaller and worse. What does signing cost *you*? If you say no, I'll eat your pozole and we'll go to Livermore and try to un-say it. If you say yes, I want you to say it knowing what Bell does with a hand that's been counted." She looked at Nana's glasses instead of the pencil. "Aunty wrote *Don't sign.* I think she meant me. I don't think she got to decide for you."

"It's hers to ask," Kino said. "I'm standing next to you, Nana. Same as I said this morning."

Nana poured a small white hill of salt onto the folded napkin without touching it. "So it stops listening while we eat."

The warmth under Arenya's ribs went out like a pilot light. She was surprised how much she missed it.

The pozole was red and deep, the hominy floating in it like moons. Nana set the little clay bowl of chile de árbol beside Arenya's bowl and looked at her over her glasses. Arenya added the chile.

Nana talked with her back half turned, wiping a counter that was already clean.

"A witness isn't a signature, mija. A signature is *I agree.* A witness is *I was there, and I'll say so again.* The book doesn't keep the one who signs. It keeps the one who'll say it again. When my name goes on that line, it goes in with where I live. Not this address. *Where I live.* Who eats at my table. Who sleeps here." The rag stopped. "Then the mail starts. Never a threat. Offers. Every month, a little paper. *Sign here and we close the account. Sign here and the boy is left alone.* Your tía got eleven years of envelopes. Meridian was only the last ones. That's what Bell does with a counted hand."

She turned around.

"He writes to it."

She looked at Kino, not at Arenya. "You gave your note to the water yesterday. You're out. I felt the cold go off you like a fever breaking. If I sign, mijo, you go back in. As *who sleeps here.* That's what I sat on every Thursday while Susmithaa drank her tea and didn't push me." The rag went down by the pencil. "So tell me. Not *I'm standing next to you.* Tell me if you'll go back in."

"Eleven years," Arenya said. "Nobody told me that. I'd have asked differently." She made herself look up. "If it's no, it's still an answer. It doesn't make you less."

Kino watched the hominy instead of anyone, because that was easier.

"Then let me tell you something first. So you're not deciding on a lie."

He said it in Spanish, low. She caught the shape of it through the wall of the language: *semanas. La Señora. La espalda.* Weeks. The Lady. Her back.

Then in English, so she'd hear it too.

"For weeks the Lady's had her back to me. In the dreams. I climb up, I sit where I always sit, and she's turned the other way. I thought if I trained harder she'd turn around." He breathed out through his nose. "She didn't. I know where she's looking now."

He didn't nod at the chair beside him. He didn't have to. Arenya's face went hot, and she kept it still.

"So yes. I'll go back in. Not because the mountain asked. Not to be counted for something. I spent my whole life waiting to be written down somewhere that mattered, and this isn't that, and I know it." His thumb found the birthmark and stayed there. "*Who sleeps here.* That's true. I've slept in that back room since I was six. If the envelopes come, they come to both of us. I'll read every one out loud at this table, in daylight, and we'll burn them in the sink. Tía got eleven years of them alone. You won't." He picked up his spoon. "That's my yes. It's mine. It isn't hers. The pencil's still yours."

Nana took off her reading glasses and folded them. Without them her eyes were wet and very plain.

"*La Señora.* Ay, mijo. Six weeks you carried that and ate my food and said nothing."

She put her hand on the back of his head for one second, the way you'd check a child for fever. Then she sat down at her own table for the first time since they'd come in, and opened the tin.

Camphor and jasmine came up out of it like a door opening in another house. She smoothed the typed sheet flat with the side of her hand. *S. Sowri*, the loops leaning right. Under it, the empty line.

She picked up the pencil and didn't hurry. *Itzel Rosario Martínez.* The lead scratched, the only sound in the kitchen. Then, beside the signature, two small strokes, side by side, the way she'd seen them in the salt in March.

"*Dos testigos,*" she said. "*Ella y yo. Ya está.*"

The kitchen did three things at once.

Behind the dresser, the rime on the jamb ticked like a cooling engine, went from white to clear, and ran down to the floor in one thin line.

Under the salt on the napkin, something gave. A soft *tck*. A pencil lead, snapping.

And on Arenya's palm, under the frost *12*, beside the rule, there were two short marks now. Grey as graphite. Side by side. Her hand was cool. For the first time in days it was just a hand.

The first thing in her throat was a joke, and it was the wrong one. She swallowed it.

Nana set the pencil down, point away from all of them.

"Now it's cold," she said of the pozole, and her voice cracked right down the middle. "Eat anyway. Then somebody tell me what we do about Livermore, because I'm not finished being afraid, mija. I've only stopped being alone."

* * *

"I want to read every word out loud," Arenya said, when the bowls were empty. "Once. In daylight. Anyone who stands for me should be able to say it again."

"Slow," said Kino. "I'll say it back. Nana too. If the room goes cold, nobody gets up. We finish the line."

One sheet of cheap bond, typed on a machine with a sticky *e*, corrected in blue ballpoint where the *e* hadn't struck. Arenya held one finger an inch above the paper and read at the pace of someone crossing a rope.

*I, Susmithaa Sowri, of 41 Hollister Court, South San Francisco, being of sound mind, declare the following before a witness.*

*One. On the eighth of March I had an archana performed at the Livermore temple in the name of my niece, Arenya, with her star and her gotra. I asked for it. She did not. She was not present and did not know.*

*Two. The priest who read it was not our priest. I noticed too late. If anything was promised in that room in her name, it was promised by my mouth and not hers, and I take it with me.*

*Three. Neither she nor any of my blood is party to any contract, policy, or account opened beneath Colma or elsewhere in my name or hers.*

*Four. Any envelope addressed to the beneficiary is addressed to no one.*

Arenya said each line. Kino said it back. Nana said it last, the vowels leaning. Twice the pilot light on the stove flickered blue. That was all.

The tin's lid settled with a small *pank*, like a can in a cooling car. On her palm, the two grey strokes darkened from pencil-grey to the grey of a pencil pressed hard.

The last part was addressed to her.

*Ari — I'm sorry I asked for you without asking you. I wanted the god to know your name before anyone else learned it. Someone else was listening. Don't go back into that room to fix my mistake unless you go with people who will say this again.*

She read it alone. Her voice held until the second word.

*Ari.*

Nobody repeated that part. It wasn't for the book.

"*Not our priest,*" Nana said, very softly. "She never told me that."

Arenya wrote, because writing was how she stayed upright: *Mar 8. Livermore. Priest not hers. Who?* She underlined *Who?* twice, hard enough to dent the page.

"What do two strokes do?"

"What Susmithaa told me. One stroke is a no that can be argued. Two is a no that stands. On the sixteenth day your account reads *disputada, dos testigos*, and a clerk who never errs doesn't pay out on a dispute. Bell can't say you're his. He can wait. They're good at waiting." Nana tapped the declaration under *not our priest.* "But Livermore is a different book. Whatever that man heard, he heard it in a temple, not in Colma. Our no doesn't reach there. That's why she wrote *don't go alone.*"

"Then we don't ask the god anything," Arenya said. "We ask the office. Did she pay by check?"

"Always. She said cash was for people who wanted to forget."

Nana lifted the paper lining of the tin and drew out a thin stack of yellow carbons from the back of a checkbook. PG&E. *Itzel — groceries (she'd argue).* Ravi's birthday. Then:

*No. 2207. Mar 8. $101.00. Pay to: LIVERMORE TEMPLE — archana.* On the memo line, in that leaning hand: *for Ari.*

"A carbon can't tell you who cashed it," Nana said. "The bank has the back."

"A hundred and one is a temple number," Kino said. "An offering. The office logs it. We ask for the receipt book, not the priest. Nobody gets cagey about a receipt."

They left the tin open on the bare table, so the house could hear the declaration sit there. The check stayed under its salt. Kino twisted a pinch of salt into a scrap of paper towel and put it in Arenya's pocket without asking. Ix lay down across the doorway to Nana's kitchen and put its chin on its paws, and nobody needed to say that it was staying.

"*Mijo,*" Nana said at the door. "Daylight. You come back with all of her."

* * *

Kino's car sat at the bottom of Nana's hill with the heater blowing dust. It was ten past four.

"Ava first," Arenya said, thumb over the name. "If I do it after Livermore I'll have a better excuse to be a coward."

Four rings. Then Ava, and behind her a kitchen faucet dripping, and nothing else. "Hey. I'm home."

"I'm going to say three things," Arenya said, "and you can hang up after any of them. I used you, Ava. I needed to see what he'd do, and you were the person at the counter. I'm sorry. That's on me." She kept the pen capped on her knee. "The man who read your name is real, and I can't promise he's done. And there's a spare bed at a woman's house in Daly City, with soup, for as long as you want it."

The faucet dripped.

"Yeah," Ava said. "I figured out the first part around the time my hand went numb." A breath. "Can I tell you something weird? Around two, I was wiping down the bar, and it just went warm. Like someone let go of my wrist." Quieter: "Was that you?"

*Two o'clock. Nana's pencil.*

Arenya almost said yes. It would have been so easy, and so cheap.

"No. I wish I could take that one. It was somebody else, doing something brave in a kitchen."

"Okay. I'm keeping the salt. I'm keeping the code. I'm not taking the bed, but text me the address. Don't say it on the phone, right? That's a rule. You've got the voice of somebody with rules now."

"That's one."

"Good. Because the guy, the cardigan, does he—"

It was an hour east. Over the San Mateo Bridge with the long grey flat of the bay at their elbows, 92 to 880, then 580, where the land lifted its skirts and went to gold. Along the Altamont ridgelines, rows of white turbines turned so slowly they looked like they were thinking about it.

At the summit the line clicked into silence. **Call Failed.**

"Dead zone," Kino said. He didn't sound sure that was all it was.

Arenya looked out his window at the ridge. "Third from the left. It's out of step."

The blades went round together, a slow white chorus. The third turned a hair behind, lagged, then caught up a little too quick, the way a person turns back to look after they've already said they're leaving.

"It's not the light," Kino said, very even. "But it's not close. It's a window, not a door. Bell likes windows. Look at the road. Whatever's in that blade gets nothing from being stared at."

She looked at her notebook until the ink held still.

"Say it," she said. "I used a barista as bait."

His thumb tapped the wheel once.

"No. I won't say it. You didn't use her. You used a door, and she was standing in it." He eased into the slow lane. "That's different, but not by much, and you know it. That's why you called. Bait that's told the truth afterward is a lookout. Don't make it bigger than it is, and don't make it smaller."

She didn't say thank you, because she'd have cried, and she wasn't going to cry on the Altamont Pass.

On the downslope, Ava's text came through, stamped eleven minutes ago and stuck somewhere on the ridge. **okay. salt tonight. kouign-amann if anything.** Arenya sent the address back, and under it, *salt on the sill.*

They stopped for gas at the bottom of the grade. Kino came back from the restroom with his hair wet at the front.

"Better?"

"No. But cleaner."

Then the valley opened, vineyards ruled in green lines, a windsock hanging limp at the little airport. Kino took the Vasco exit into oak and dry grass, and the temple came up on its rise above the vines: white gopuram tiers carved with a thousand small gods, catching the low sun. A sign over rows of wooden cubbies said *FOOTWEAR HERE.* From inside, a bell rang once, someone's evening puja, clean across the gravel.

The dashboard clock said 5:14.

"Nothing's listening," Kino said, watching the temple the way he watched trails. "Not yet."

To the left of the main steps, a door stood propped open with a brick. **OFFICE — ARCHANA / DONATIONS / RECEIPTS. 9–12 · 4–7.**

"I'll talk," Arenya said. "You watch the room."

"Ask for the receipt book. Priests lie out of habit. Ledgers just add."

* * *

The shoes went in the cubbies, her sneakers and his boots side by side like a couple at a very small, very strange wedding. Then cool tile under her socks, and a smell she knew from every Sunday of her childhood: camphor, ghee, floor cleaner. Somewhere deeper in, a nadaswaram played on a loop, turned low.

The office was narrow. One desk, one ceiling fan, a calendar from a Fremont jeweler. A laminated price list: *Name + Nakshatra + Gotra: $21. Special (with Sahasranama): $51. Family Sankalpam, full: $101.* Beneath it, taped: **RECEIPTS ISSUED ONLY FROM OFFICE BOOK.** Behind the desk, binders by year, and a fat one marked **2026 — JAN–JUN**.

Kino took up a spot half a step behind her shoulder without saying he was taking it. "*Aquí nadie cuenta todavía,*" he said, to nobody. *Nobody's counting here yet.*

*Yet.*

The woman behind the desk looked up over half-moon glasses. Seventy, maybe. Gold nose stud, a pen on a beaded chain, a blue cardigan. The nameplate said **Mrs. K. Ramaswamy, Volunteer Office Secretary.**

"Good evening, ma. Archana? Next one is six."

"Namaste, Aunty. Sorry to bother you." Arenya made her voice somebody's daughter's. "I'm looking for a receipt. An archana from March eighth, in the name of Ari. My aunt paid, and she passed away, and the family's sorting her papers." She set the carbon on the desk, one finger on its corner. "Number 2207. I don't need to take anything. I want to know who wrote it, and who read it."

Mrs. Ramaswamy rolled her chair to the shelf without standing, a move she'd made ten thousand times. Pink stubs flipped under her thumb.

"Sorry about your aunty, ma. *Om shanti.*"

Stub No. 2207. *S. Sowri* on the donor line, in the hand from the tea tin. On *Archana for*, Susmithaa had written the whole name, every syllable, with gotra and nakshatra beneath. Everything a priest needed to say a person aloud to a god. *Cash, $101.00.* In the corner, another hand had initialed: *K.R. 3/19.*

"That's me. Funny, now I remember. A gentleman came for this same one. Nice-spoken, grey suit. Insurance. He said the policy needed proof of the family's religious *expenses*, can you imagine. I photocopied it for him. He paid ten cents, in an old coin. I put it in the hundi." The aunty-gaze came back, kind and immovable. "But this I can't just show, ma. You are the one on here? Show any ID, I'll make you a copy also."

A copy. Another place her name would sit.

"Aunty, I'm not going to lie to you. I'd rather not put my ID on your copier. Somebody has been collecting my family's papers. Did the insurance man sign anything?"

"Don't look at the clock," Kino said, low.

She'd noticed it anyway. A plain wall clock above the binders. On every tick, the second hand landed a hair short of the mark and then corrected, as if something had to count it before it was allowed to move.

"Aunty," Kino said, easy as a man at a trailhead, "has that clock always run like that? Or only since March?"

Mrs. Ramaswamy let her glasses fall on their chain. For a moment she wasn't a volunteer at all. She was somebody's grandmother, hearing something she'd half known.

"Your aunty said the same. '*Cash, Aunty, and no copies.*' I thought she was being careful about the government." From the drawer she took a spiral register: **COPY REQUESTS.** Three entries all year. The March 19 line read *Stub 2207 — 1 copy — $0.10*, and beside it, in upright, perfectly even fountain pen: ***D. Hale.*** A cream card was clipped to the page. *Meridian Mutual · Beneficiary Services.* No phone number. "He asked *who reads them*, and how often. I told him. The full sankalpam is read every Friday at the abhishekam, for one year from payment."

Every Friday until next March, Arenya's whole name went up to a god in a room somebody had a photocopy of.

"Sastri garu keeps saying that clock is slow. We changed the battery twice. Since end of March."

On the next tick the second hand stopped a full breath short, as though whatever was counting had to look twice. On Arenya's palm, the two strokes pressed down, light and exact.

"Aunty. I have her original carbon, in her hand. I'd like the sankalpam stopped before tomorrow. Not read, not changed. Stopped. Could you strike it with your own pen and write that it was cancelled by family? I won't sign anything." A beat. "And may I keep Mr. Hale's card? It's the only thing in this room I'd be glad to take."

Kino opened his fist and let a few grains of salt fall along the baseboard under the clock. "Say it out loud while you write, ma'am. So it's you writing. And if the clock slows, don't stop. Finish the line."

Mrs. Ramaswamy slid Hale's card across without a word, the way you'd hand a child something hot. Then she uncapped the pen on her chain and said, clearly, "Cancelling. By family." One firm line through every syllable. Beneath it: ***CANCELLED — BY FAMILY, ORIG. CARBON SHOWN. K.R.*** and the date.

The second hand came up to the mark and stopped, and didn't correct. The nadaswaram's loop caught and started again. Arenya's palm pressed once, hard, like a nib set down. Then cool skin. Nothing.

"There. Now the office won't read it." Mrs. Ramaswamy capped the pen, pleased and a little shaken. "But ma, properly: a sankalpam is a promise made *to Him*, not to me. My pen stops the list. It doesn't close the promise. For that Sastri garu does a small visarjana. He says the name one last time and tells Him it's finished."

The name. Out loud. In a room Hale had a photocopy of.

Kino crouched by the baseboard. The salt had been counted into a row, evenly spaced. "It's waiting for the name," he said. "Can the closing use the receipt? The number, and *family*?"

"Who told you it needs the name?"

The man in the doorway was small and old, bare-chested under a folded angavastram, his feet wet, a steel tumbler of water in his hand. He looked at the struck stub, at the salt, and for a long moment at the clock.

"The name is for the people in the hall, so they know whose function it is. *He* already knows who she is. Star, gotra, her own hand on the plate. That is enough. Inside, door pulled, nobody at the rail. Now, before arati. After arati the hall is full." He set the tumbler down. "Sastri. I was in Fremont on March eighth with my knee. The visiting man signed my register. Very neat hand." He didn't like saying it. "One thing, child. If I don't say it, *you* don't say it either. Not to Him, not to yourself, not even in your head. You stand there as the one the stub was for, and you keep your mouth shut until I pour the water. Can you do that?"

Every reporter's question in her rose up. She swallowed them. After.

"Yes, Sastri garu. It's the thing I'm worst at. But I can do it."

"Hold the door," she told Kino. "If anything counts, cough."

* * *

The sanctum was small and warm and smelled of old ghee. Sastri pulled the wooden door to, leaving a hand's width, set a brass plate under her fingers, and began, low and fast, the way men chant who have done it for fifty years.

Her star was in it. Her gotra. Where the name should go he said *ayam*, *this one*, and tapped the plate once.

The lamp flame lay flat, as if a door had opened somewhere it shouldn't.

Out in the hall, something buzzed on the shoe bench.

Through the gap, Arenya saw Kino turn. A cheap flip phone sat on the bench that hadn't been there when they came in, face up, already connected, a call timer running. Under the clock, one grain of salt rolled out of the counted row and sat alone, like a tally carried over.

A voice came out of it, small and tinny and unmistakable.

*"Ari? Kanna, are you there? Say something, just say it's you—"*

Amma.

Everything in her lunged toward it. Her mouth opened.

She bit the inside of her cheek, hard. Copper. She kept her fingers flat on the brass.

*Poodle. Curly. Stupid. Groomed into a ridiculous pom-pom.*

Sastri didn't stop chanting. His eyes flicked to her once. The water wasn't poured yet.

*Amma doesn't call. Amma texts. Amma says it in the other order.*

She held that like a rail. Her lips went white. She made no sound.

In the hall, Kino said nothing at all. Not her name, not *Amma*, nothing the phone could keep. Two steps and he lifted it by its edges, the way he'd lift a dead bird, folded it shut over the voice, and pressed it flat against his chest under his palm. He planted himself in the doorway with his back to the sanctum, so the sound would have to go through him to reach her.

Then he coughed once, hard and clear. Not a warning. A hand held out through a wall. *I'm here. That's not your mother.*

Sastri lifted the kalasam and poured. Water ran over the brass and over her fingers.

"*Samāptam.*" Flat as a man closing a shop. *It is finished.*

In the office the second hand ticked past the mark, an ordinary tick. The salt on the baseboard slumped into a loose heap, the way salt is supposed to lie.

Against Kino's chest the phone went dead. It didn't hang up; the battery was simply flat, cold as a creek stone. When he took it away the cold stayed: a rectangle of numb skin over his sternum the exact size of the phone. Something meant for her had been said into him, and his body had kept it.

On the cheap screen, burned faint: *CALL ENDED 0:41.* And a number she knew from Rodel's card. *650-755-*. Colma Notary & Mail. She didn't say it aloud.

Sastri gave her a flower and a pinch of kumkum and finally let her talk. "Done. Whatever was promised in your name here is closed." Then, gruff, past her shoulder: "Your friend took something for you. Feed him."

She held two fingers over the numb patch, not touching, the way you'd check a stove. "Does it hurt? Can you feel your heart under it?"

"Cold, not pain. I can feel it." He rubbed two knuckles over his shirt. "Slow. Same cold as that freezer."

"You took that for me." Her voice came out rough. "We're eating right after this. Priest's orders."

* * *

Mrs. Ramaswamy already had the temple register open to March 8, her finger under the visiting priest's line.

The page was mostly her own round hand. Halfway down, the handwriting changed, and nobody would have missed it. **No. 2207 · $101 · Archana · "Ari."** Under PRIEST: *Visiting — Pt. H. Dalal, Fremont.* The signature was a single straight line with a small hook at each end, like a bracket lying on its side. Under CONTACT, Rodel's number. And an address: **c/o Colma Notary & Mail, Box 112.**

Hale had rented that box on March 19. Eleven days *after* this page was written.

Arenya started to copy the signature into her notebook. The pen dragged, as if the paper had a grain. The line wanted to come out perfect. Her palm prickled once. One hook in, the long stroke beginning to run on, smooth and eager.

She lifted the pen.

"No. I'm not drawing it." She photographed the page instead, three frames, with the clock's face in the corner of one so the time was on record. "The check. Did it get deposited? I need the back of 2207."

Mrs. Ramaswamy ran a finger down the deposit book and frowned. "Not here. Ah. Yes. He asked for it himself. As his *dakshina*. He said the family wanted it so." The line read *2207 — given to priest as dakshina, per family. —K.R.* She looked embarrassed. "So the back, the signing, is in the family's bank. Her statement will have the picture." Her eyes went to the floor by the sanctum. "That man, I never saw a car. I came from the kitchen and he was standing in the hall. Very polite. Very nice teeth. And he never took his shoes off. All afternoon he walked in shoes. Why did nobody tell him?"

"You did nothing wrong," Arenya said gently. "Nobody told him because he made you forget to. That's what he does." She wasn't sure it comforted. It was true.

"No shoes off in a temple," Kino said, low. "That's not bad manners. He doesn't think he's a guest. So none of the house rules bind him."

He wrote his station line and cell on a corner of his ranger notepad and slid it under the brass paperweight. "Salt on the sill, ma'am. If he comes back, don't hand him anything. Call this."

"Nice shoes, I will not see," said Mrs. Ramaswamy, firmer than her hands, and laid a folded dhoti over the March page like a sheet over a sleeper.

On the way out, by the sanctum door, Kino stopped. Down the hall, in every third grout seam, ran a faint grey hairline, the same dead grey as the ring in Nana's cal, each the length of one even stride. They stopped at the sanctum threshold.

He'd come this far, and no farther.

In Arenya's bag, the half-drawn hook sat alone on its page. It didn't finish itself. But the notebook felt a fraction heavier than it should, like a book with a flower pressed in it.

Sastri stood in the temple doorway with his arms folded until their car was out of the lot.

* * *

Sri Ganesh Tiffin took up half a strip-mall unit off First Street, between a vape shop and a tax preparer. Six tables. A TV in the corner playing a Tamil serial on mute, in which a woman was crying in a doorway. At a quarter past six the room belonged to them and one uncle reading the *India West*.

Then the smell came over the counter. Browned ghee. Curry leaf. Mustard seed popping. It hit Arenya like a kitchen in Daly City on a Sunday, and she almost turned around to see who was behind her.

The dosa arrived longer than the plate. Steam came off the sambar in a straight line. She watched it for three full seconds, because she'd started watching steam. Nothing about it was wrong.

"Rule," she said, mouth half full. "Twenty minutes. No names, no numbers, nobody writes anything down. How's the rectangle? Don't say fine. One to ten."

Kino poured his coffee from tumbler to dabarah and back, long pours from high up, the way any aunty would. Something to do with his hands while he listened to his own heart lag.

"Three. Like a tooth that went numb at the dentist." He tapped two fingers under his collarbone. A small crooked smile. "Okay. Four. It's getting a vote."

"Palm's a two. Brain's an eleven." She turned her tumbler. "And thank you. For the phone. I held my mouth shut and you held the phone, and I'm not pretending those weigh the same."

"You held the harder thing."

He lifted his tumbler. "To paper dosa." Steel clinked on steel. It was the least magical sound she'd heard all week, and she could have wept.

For a while nothing in the room was counting anything. The counter man refilled Kino's sambar without being asked and set a second bowl beside it. "Eat, eat." Something in Kino's face went off duty.

"When I was nine," he said, "Nana had me sit on the mountain all night. By myself. No flashlight. She said if I was meant for it, something would come sit with me." He turned his tumbler a quarter turn. "Nothing came. Fog. A skunk. In the morning I told her a deer had, because I wanted it to be true. I've never told anybody that." He looked straight at her. "You held your mouth shut against your mother's voice today. How are you? Not the palm. You."

She looked at the dosa, because it was easier.

"Honest version. No bit. A few weeks ago the worst thing in my life was a listicle called *Eleven Hamsters Who Are Living Their Best Life*. I used to lie in bed and think, if something real ever happens, I'll be the one who notices." She set down a piece she hadn't meant to tear. "And something real happened, and the first thing it did was write my name in a book next to my dead aunt's. Today it called me in my mother's voice. And the part I can't say to Ava or to Amma is that some small, disgusting part of me is *glad*. Finally. Finally it's a real story." Her left hand pressed flat on the notebook. "I'm sitting here with ink on my palm that isn't mine, and part of me is taking notes."

Under her hand, the notebook settled.

Not cold. It was only a little heavier than paper had any right to be, the way a cat settles when it hears its name. It lasted exactly as long as she meant the words. Then it was a notebook again.

She didn't look down. She didn't look at Kino either, which was how she knew he'd seen.

"Glad's not a sin," he said. "I've been glad my whole life that something might finally happen to me. Waiting to be needed. That's the same thing, Ari, and I'm the one who got trained for it." He dipped a piece of crisp in the sambar. "Taking notes is how you stay a person in a room that wants you to be a name. Keep doing it. Just don't let it sign anything."

She laughed once, wetly, and wiped her eye with the back of her wrist, the one with the cold coin on it.

"Also, for the record. A deer came."

He went still.

"You were nine. You sat on a mountain all night in the fog with no flashlight, and you *stayed*. That's the deer. Nana knew it was a lie and she let you keep it, because it was the true kind." She pushed the second bowl at him. "Eat, eat. He's right."

"A deer came," Kino said, almost to the tumbler. "Okay." The laugh cracked at the end. "Don't tell Nana. She'll take credit."

At the door he held it for her. Outside, First Street had gone blue.

"Then we go see your mother," he said. "Why she's been keeping your aunt's mail."

"I ask."

"You ask. I'll watch her hands."

* * *

The fog had beaten them to Daly City. It had come over the ridge in one slow sheet, and it was a quarter to eight, and the porch lights on the hill were haloed like streetlamps in an old movie. Appa's car wasn't in the driveway. Behind the house, Aunty's cottage sat dark, curtains drawn the way she'd left them.

Amma opened the door before Arenya could knock. House sari, cardigan, reading glasses pushed up into her hair.

"You drove to *Livermore*," she said, which was how she said hello. To Kino: "Sit. There's rice."

There was rice and sambar and two kinds of curd, and a steel plate already set at Kino's place. In the puja alcove, the brass lamp stood dead upright, though the window over the sink was open a crack.

On the table lay a manila folder, **SUSI — ACCOUNTS**, in Amma's capitals. One of her hands rested flat on it. With the other she wiped a counter that was already clean.

"Appa went for washers. For the tap." She didn't look at the clock. "At twelve-thirty. Seven hours, for washers." The rag went round. "So. Eat. Then you tell me why my sister's temple called this house tonight asking about a check."

Arenya ate one bite of rice, because refusing Amma's rice was a declaration of war, and she put both hands flat on the table.

"Amma, I'm not going to lie to you while I chew. Check 2207. A hundred and one dollars, taken by a man who wasn't the temple's priest. Someone endorsed it. I'm not accusing you. I want to know whose name is on the back, and whether you knew when you hid that folder." Quieter: "And you said *my sister's temple.* Not ours. Why?"

At *2207*, the wiping hand stopped. Not slowed. Stopped, mid-circle. Then Amma folded the rag into a neat square, which was what she did when she'd decided something.

"Because she chose it. Ours is four streets away, and everybody's aunty is at ours. She drove one hour so nobody from Daly City would watch her ask God for something." Her eyes were dry and furious. "She was my sister. Twenty-nine years. You don't get to ask me why with that face."

She slid the folder to the middle of the table and opened it.

The March statement. The check images printed small at the bottom. The front of 2207, in Aunty's careful hand: *for Ari.* And the back.

**R. Sowri.** Ruler-straight strokes, every letter built from right angles, the last *i* closing on a small bracket-shaped hook. Beneath it, in the same hand: *Pay to the order of H. Dalal.*

Arenya's notebook held half of that hook. She'd have known it anywhere.

"That is not your Appa's signature," Amma said. "Your Appa signs like an engineer. Small, leaning left, lifts his pen like it costs money. I knew in one second. So I asked him, the night after the funeral, and your father, who cannot lie to the gas man about the meter, said, *Leave it, Lakshmi. It's handled.*" Her voice caught. She made it uncatch. "Thirty-four years. First time I didn't believe him. So I hid it. From him. Not from you."

"I'm not asking because I think you did it," Arenya said, rougher than she meant. "I'm asking because you're the only one in this family who saw it and said something. I'm sorry I made it sound like a case." She slid the notebook half out of her bag and showed her the hooked line from Livermore. "That's the visitor's hand. The same man signed Appa's name on Aunty's check."

Kino wasn't looking at the folder. He was looking past the kitchen at the garage door, where a pair of work shoes stood toes-out on the mat.

"Those weren't there when we came in the front," he said quietly. "Did you see him go, Amma, or only hear the door?"

"Washers was true. He left at twelve-thirty, in his good shoes. *Those* were on the rack in the garage." Her mouth flattened. "I was home all afternoon. I didn't hear him come back."

The rice cooker clicked over to *warm*.

Kino's head turned toward the side wall. She watched him try to aim at something and lose it, and aim again. Two fingers went to his wrist.

"Something's ticking," he murmured. "Too even for a clock. Is there anything in the side yard that turns by itself?"

"The gas meter," Amma said. "By the garage door. Your Appa reads it every Sunday and writes it in a little book. Thirty years at PG&E, he doesn't trust their numbers." She was watching Kino's face now. "It shouldn't turn. The stove is off. The rice is electric."

The moment the word *meter* was said in the kitchen, Kino's breath went out of him. "It stopped. In the wall. And in here." He touched his sternum. "It was ticking in here too."

The silence after was worse. It was the silence of something that had already counted what it came to count.

In the alcove, the lamp leaned, very slightly, toward the hall.

Arenya was on her feet before she'd decided to be. "Heartbeat. Yours or the room's?"

"Mine. Half a beat late. But mine."

"Amma, stay at the table. Don't touch the folder." She laid a short line of Nana's salt across the kitchen doorway where it met the hall. Kino laid his behind it. His grains lay a little crooked. His hand had shaken.

Amma sat. She folded her hands over the rag and her lips moved, and it wasn't English. Between verses she said, "Three keys. Mine. His. And your aunty's, on the red string. After the funeral I looked in her purse. It wasn't there."

Arenya went to the window over the sink and looked without leaning into the gap.

The side yard, a strip of concrete under the motion light, fog standing in it like milk. The grey gas meter on its pipe by the garage door, its glass face white with frost, from the *inside*. From its base a hairline of the same white ran across the concrete to the garage door handle. Thinner than a pencil line. Straight as a ruler.

The same ruler that had been laid to Nana's side door.

Wedged between the meter and its pipe was Appa's meter book, a small green ledger with a pen clipped to it. The wind had opened it. Her father wrote small and very neat, leaning left. Today's date. *3:12 p.m.* A reading. And under it, three words pressed so hard into the paper they'd torn it.

*Ari does not sign.*

She read it out loud. Her voice only wobbled on the last word. "He wasn't writing about a meter."

"Look at the pressure," Kino said. "That isn't a threat. That's your father on our side, writing where the counting thing reads."

The frost on the meter began to clear from the top down, one dial at a time, as if someone on the other side were wiping it. She read each one as it came.

"Two. Two. Zero. Seven."

*2207.*

In the green book, Appa's Sunday reading was *4471*, the ordinary number. The dials didn't say that now. They said the number of Aunty's check.

Then the smallest needle gave one tiny tick, like a single pencil stroke.

Against her hip, in the bag, the notebook ticked back. She felt it go heavier. Just slightly. Just enough. Kino's breath caught. "Another quarter behind," he said. "Four now. Still mine. It's not your name. It's the check. Somebody's reading us the receipt. It wants us to flinch, or answer."

She took her hand off the bag like it was a burner and folded her arms to keep them out of trouble.

"A receipt needs somebody to take it. Nobody takes it."

They waited it out. Amma's verses. The hum of the rice cooker. Kino counting under his breath.

The needle didn't tick again. The frost finished clearing, and there was nothing behind the glass but dials. Then, one at a time, without a sound, the needles slid backward to *4471*. On the concrete, the ruled line broke in the middle like a snapped thread, and the fog took both halves.

Nobody had accepted the receipt, so the meter took it back.

The notebook didn't give its weight back. It stayed one stroke heavier.

The lamp straightened.

Then the front door. A key, the rattle of the chain Amma never used, a plastic bag rustling.

"Lakshmi? The washers were the wrong size, so I got both—"

Appa stopped in the kitchen doorway, on the far side of two lines of salt. A slight grey man in a cardigan who had always looked faintly apologetic. He looked at the salt. At Kino standing over it. At the open folder with the endorsement facing up.

Something left his shoulders, and it wasn't relief.

Wound twice around the first finger of his right hand, as if he'd been worrying it the whole drive, hung a brass key on a faded red string.

"So," Raghavan Sowri said quietly. He didn't step over the salt. "She showed you."

"Don't," Arenya said, sharper than she meant, and watched it land. "Don't step over it, Appa. Not yet."

He looked small in the doorway with the hardware bag on his wrist. He'd always looked like that. She'd just never been scared of it before.

"Nobody showed me. I found it. That's my job, apparently. That's Aunty's key. And there's a page in your meter book, in your writing, at three-twelve this afternoon, when you were supposedly at the hardware store. *Ari does not sign.*" Her voice went very flat, so it wouldn't do anything else. "Who's H. Dalal? Who owed her? And please don't say *it's handled.*"

Appa set the bag down on his side of the salt, carefully, as if it might hold something fragile. It held washers.

"In order," he said. It was how he'd always answered her, engineer's habits even when frightened.

"One. I don't know who H. Dalal is. In March a man telephoned. He said he was from the Livermore temple. Very polite. American. He said *Sowri-garu* correctly, which nobody does. He said, *Your sister has offered dakshina for the visiting pandit. Does the family approve?* And I was at my desk with the cricket on, and I said—"

His mouth worked.

"*Whatever my sister wants is fine.* That was all. Then the check went to him, *per family.* I was the family. I said yes for her without knowing what I was saying yes to. She never blamed me. Your Amma doesn't know that part."

He didn't look at Amma. Amma looked at him.

"Two. Who owed her: Rodel. From the notary. Twenty years ago his mother was dying at Seton, and Susmithaa sat with her nine nights when he couldn't get off his shift. After the funeral, *this* funeral, he telephoned me himself. *Mr. Sowri, they used my number. I am sorry. But you must hold her key. The side door is the one they'll come to.* So I took it from her purse at the hospital, and I lied to your mother. *It's handled.*" He lifted the red string. "This is me handling.

"Three. Rodel said if they come, the meter shows it first, because it counts. Write the family's no where the counting thing reads, in your own hand, and press hard. So at three o'clock I came in the side with her key. The needles were already wrong. Two, two, zero. I wrote it. I put my shoes toes-out by the door, the way my mother did, so whatever came would walk away. Then I drove around and bought the wrong washers, because I did not know what else a man does."

He held the key out over the salt at arm's length and didn't let his fingers cross.

"It was for the meter, Ari. And whoever was reading it. I'm not good at this. Your aunty was. Take it, or tell me where to put it."

"Amma first," Arenya said. "It's her sister's key." Softer: "You didn't sign, Appa. You said yes to a phone call. That's exactly the kind of mistake it's built to collect."

Amma got up.

She reached across the salt, the way you'd take a hot dish from someone, and took the key off his finger, string and all. She carried it to the alcove and laid it at the foot of the lamp, on the little brass plate where the ghee drips, so the red string coiled in the light.

The flame dipped once. Then it stood tall again.

"There. Now the lamp has it. Not you. Not her." She turned. "Raghavan. Step over. Wash your hands. Eat."

He stepped over both lines the way he stepped over garden hoses. Nothing happened, because nothing should. At the sink, under the running water, he said to Kino, "No name. He never said his name. Perfect, but flat. Every syllable the same weight, like reading off a card. Nobody's mouth does that with our names. I noticed and did nothing."

He dried his hands on Amma's rag, which nobody was ever allowed to do. Amma let him.

"And Rodel. I asked him how he knew about the meter. He said, *The front door of a Hindu house has a lamp. The side door has a meter. Things that count come in where things are counted.*" He hung the rag up. "Then he said something I didn't understand. *He asked me for one-twelve before it was empty. I should have known then.*"

Arenya stopped with her spoon in the air.

"Hale rented Box 112 on March nineteenth," she said, to the plate, then to the room. "But somebody asked Rodel for it before that, and wrote it in a temple register before it was even rented. Hale was only the name on the form." She looked up. "Appa. Did Rodel say who?"

"Only *he*. The way you say it about the weather."

"Then tomorrow," she said. "Daylight. Before seven-forty, when the box closes. Rodel's own register on his own counter. Not alone, and not in the dark."

"I'll drive you," said Appa. "I won't go in. I'll sit in the car and read the meter on the flower shop."

It might have been a joke. In thirty years nobody had ever been sure when Appa was joking.

"Eat," said Amma. "All of you."

They ate. Amma refilled the plates without asking. The cricket stayed off. Out in the side yard, under the fog, the meter ticked along at an ordinary pace, burning gas for an ordinary stove while Amma heated rasam nobody had asked for.

Arenya's notebook sat in the bag at her feet, one stroke heavier than it had been that morning. Kino's heart ran a quarter-beat behind where it should, and he didn't mention it again. On her palm, under the *12*, Nana's two strokes lay cool and dark.

In the alcove, the red string lay coiled at the foot of the lamp, and the flame didn't lean toward anyone.

Eleven hours, give or take, until Rodel's gate opened. She didn't write it down. She didn't need to.

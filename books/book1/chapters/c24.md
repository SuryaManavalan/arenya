# Chapter Twenty-Four: Ayam

Her sneakers and his boots went into the cubbies side by side, like a couple at a very small, very strange wedding.

Cool tile under her socks. Camphor, ghee, floor cleaner, the smell of every Sunday of her childhood. Somewhere deeper in, a nadaswaram played on a loop, turned low.

The office was one desk, one ceiling fan, and a calendar from a Fremont jeweler. A laminated price list: *Name + Nakshatra + Gotra: $21. Family Sankalpam, full: $101.* Taped beneath it: **RECEIPTS ISSUED ONLY FROM OFFICE BOOK.**

The woman behind the desk looked up over half-moon glasses. Seventy, maybe, with a gold nose stud and a pen on a beaded chain. **Mrs. K. Ramaswamy, Volunteer Office Secretary.**

"Good evening, ma. Archana? Next one is six."

"Namaste, Aunty." Arenya made her voice somebody's daughter's. "I'm looking for a receipt. An archana from March eighth. My aunt paid. She passed away, and the family's sorting her papers." She set the carbon on the desk with one finger on its corner. "Number 2207. I don't need to take anything. I want to know who wrote it, and who read it."

Mrs. Ramaswamy rolled her chair to the shelf without standing, a move she'd made ten thousand times. Pink stubs flipped under her thumb.

"Sorry about your aunty, ma. *Om shanti.*"

Stub No. 2207. *S. Sowri* on the donor line, in the hand from the tea tin. On *Archana for*, Susmithaa had written the whole name, every syllable, with gotra and nakshatra beneath. Everything a priest needed to say a person aloud to a god. In the corner, a second hand had initialed it: *K.R. 3/19.*

"That's me. Funny, now I remember. A gentleman came for this same one. Grey suit. Insurance. He said the policy needed proof of the family's religious expenses, can you imagine. I photocopied it for him. He paid ten cents, in an old coin." The aunty gaze came back, kind and immovable. "This I can't just show, ma. You're the one on here? Show any ID, I'll make you a copy also."

A copy. Another place her name would sit.

"Don't look at the clock," Kino said, low.

She'd noticed it anyway. On every tick, the second hand above the binders landed a hair short of the mark and then corrected, as if something had to count it before it was allowed to move.

"Aunty," Kino said, easy as a man at a trailhead, "has that clock always run like that? Or only since March?"

Mrs. Ramaswamy let her glasses drop on their chain. For a moment she wasn't a volunteer at all. She was somebody's grandmother, hearing something she'd half known.

"Your aunty said the same. '*Cash, Aunty, and no copies.*' I thought she was being careful about the government." From a drawer she took a spiral register: **COPY REQUESTS.** Three entries all year. *March 19. Stub 2207. 1 copy. $0.10.* Beside it, in upright, perfectly even fountain pen: ***D. Hale.*** A cream card was clipped to the page. *Meridian Mutual · Beneficiary Services.* No phone number. "He asked who reads them, and how often. I told him. The full sankalpam is read every Friday at the abhishekam. For one year from payment."

Every Friday until next March, Arenya's whole name went up to a god in a room somebody had a photocopy of.

"Aunty. I have her original carbon, in her hand. I'd like the sankalpam stopped before tomorrow. Not changed. Stopped. Could you strike it with your own pen and write that it was cancelled by family? I won't sign anything." A beat. "And may I keep Mr. Hale's card? It's the only thing in this room I'd be glad to take."

Kino let a few grains of salt fall along the baseboard under the clock. "Say it out loud while you write, ma'am. So it's you writing."

Mrs. Ramaswamy slid the card across the way you'd hand a child something hot. Then she uncapped the pen on her chain and said, clearly, "Cancelling. By family." One firm line through every syllable. Beneath it: ***CANCELLED — BY FAMILY, ORIG. CARBON SHOWN. K.R.*** and the date.

The second hand reached the mark, stopped, and didn't correct. Arenya's palm pressed once, hard, like a nib set down. Then it was cool skin again.

"There. Now the office won't read it." Mrs. Ramaswamy capped the pen, pleased and a little shaken. "But properly, ma, a sankalpam is a promise made *to Him*, not to me. My pen stops the list. It doesn't close the promise. For that, Sastri garu does a small visarjana. He says the name one last time and tells Him it's finished."

The name. Out loud. In a room Hale had a photocopy of.

Kino crouched by the baseboard. The salt had been counted into a row, evenly spaced. "It's waiting for the name."

"Who told you it needs the name?"

The man in the doorway was small and old, bare-chested under a folded angavastram, his feet wet, a steel tumbler of water in his hand. He looked at the struck stub, at the salt, and for a long moment at the clock.

"The name is for the people in the hall, so they know whose function it is. *He* already knows who she is. Star, gotra, her own hand on the plate. That is enough. Inside, door pulled, now, before arati." He set the tumbler down. "Sastri. On March eighth I was not here. My knee. The visiting man signed my register. Very neat hand." He didn't like saying it. "One thing, child. If I don't say it, you don't say it either. Not to Him, not to yourself, not even in your head. You stand there as the one the stub was for, and you keep your mouth shut until I pour the water. Can you do that?"

Every reporter's question in her rose up. She swallowed them.

"Yes, Sastri garu. It's the thing I'm worst at. But I can do it."

"Hold the door," she told Kino. "If anything counts, cough."

* * *

The sanctum was small and warm and smelled of old ghee. Sastri pulled the wooden door to, leaving a hand's width, set a brass plate under her fingers, and began, low and fast, the way a man chants who has been doing it for fifty years.

Her star was in it. Her gotra. Where the name should have gone he said *ayam*, *this one*, and tapped the plate once.

The lamp flame lay down flat, as if a door had opened somewhere it shouldn't.

Out in the hall, something buzzed on the shoe bench.

Through the gap she saw Kino turn. A cheap flip phone sat on the bench where nothing had been when they came in. It was face up and already connected, a call timer running.

A voice came out of it, small and tinny and unmistakable.

*"Ari? Kanna, are you there? Say something, just say it's you—"*

Amma.

Everything in her lunged toward it. Her mouth opened.

She bit the inside of her cheek, hard enough to taste copper, and kept her fingers flat on the brass.

*Poodles. Ranked. Groomed into ridiculous pom-poms.*

Sastri didn't stop chanting. His eyes flicked to her once. The water wasn't poured yet.

*Amma doesn't call. Amma texts. Amma never says it in that order.*

She held on to that like a rail. Her lips went white. She made no sound.

In the hall, Kino said nothing at all. Not her name, not *Amma*, nothing the phone could keep. In two steps he lifted it by its edges, the way he'd lift a dead bird, folded it shut over the voice, and pressed it flat against his chest under his palm. He planted himself in the doorway with his back to the sanctum, so the sound would have to go through him to reach her.

Then he coughed once, hard and clear. It wasn't a warning. It was a hand held out through a wall. *I'm here. That's not your mother.*

Sastri lifted the kalasam and poured. Water ran over the brass and over her fingers.

"*Samāptam.*" He said it flat, like a man closing a shop. *It is finished.*

In the office, the second hand ticked past the mark, an ordinary tick. The salt on the baseboard slumped into a loose heap, the way salt is supposed to lie.

Against Kino's chest the phone went dead. It didn't hang up; the battery was simply flat, cold as a creek stone. When he took it away the cold stayed behind: a rectangle of numb skin over his sternum the exact size of the phone. Something meant for her had been said into him, and his body had kept it.

Faint on the cheap screen: *CALL ENDED 0:41.* And a number she knew from Rodel's card. Colma Notary & Mail. She didn't say it aloud.

Sastri gave her a flower and a pinch of kumkum and finally let her talk. "Done. Whatever was promised in your name here is closed." Then, gruffly, past her shoulder: "Your friend took something for you. Feed him."

She held two fingers over the numb patch without touching it, the way you'd check a stove. "Can you feel your heart under it?"

"Cold, not pain. I can feel it." He rubbed two knuckles over his shirt. "Slow. Same cold as that freezer."

* * *

Mrs. Ramaswamy already had the temple register open to March 8, her finger under the visiting priest's line.

Most of the page was in her own round hand. Halfway down the handwriting changed, and nobody could have missed it. **No. 2207 · $101 · Archana · "Ari."** Under PRIEST: *Visiting — Pt. H. Dalal, Fremont.* The signature was one straight line with a small hook at each end, like a bracket lying on its side. Under CONTACT was Rodel's number, and an address: **c/o Colma Notary & Mail, Box 112.**

Hale had rented that box on March nineteenth. Eleven days *after* this page was written.

She started to copy the signature into her notebook. The pen dragged, as if the paper had a grain, and the line wanted to come out perfect. One hook went in, and the long stroke began to run on, smooth and eager.

She lifted the pen.

"No. I'm not drawing it." She photographed the page instead, three frames, with the clock in the corner of one. "The check. Was it deposited? I need the back of 2207."

Mrs. Ramaswamy ran a finger down the deposit book and frowned. *2207 — given to priest as dakshina, per family. —K.R.* She looked embarrassed. "He asked for it himself. He said the family wanted it so. So the back will be in the family's bank." Her eyes went to the floor by the sanctum. "That man, I never saw a car. I came from the kitchen and he was standing in the hall. Very polite. Very nice teeth. And he never took his shoes off. All afternoon he walked in shoes. Why did nobody tell him?"

"You did nothing wrong," Arenya said. "Nobody told him because he made you forget to."

"Shoes on in a temple," Kino said, low. "He doesn't think he's a guest. So none of the house rules bind him."

On the way out, he stopped by the sanctum door. Down the hall, in every third grout seam, ran a faint grey hairline, each one an even stride apart. They stopped at the sanctum threshold.

The visitor had come this far and no farther.

In her bag, the half-drawn hook sat alone on its page. It didn't finish itself. But the notebook felt a fraction heavier than it should, like a book with a flower pressed in it.

Sastri stood in the temple doorway with his arms folded until their car was out of the lot.

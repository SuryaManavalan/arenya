# Chapter Thirty-Seven: Black, No Sugar

Teddy's piece went live at six. By 6:09 it had four hundred shares and a "??" from Sacramento.

By 6:22 Arenya was walking across the Presidio in the blue-black dark, and the bridge was a string of red lights hung from nothing.

The fog sat on the Gate like a held breath. A quarter mile out. Not moving.

He was on the picnic deck, early, as promised.

Typhus Quinn was taller than he looked on a screen and more tired. Runner's shell over a collared shirt, like a man who couldn't pick which person to be this morning. Two paper coffees on the rail. A manila folder flat under his left hand. He wasn't looking at his phone. He was watching the fog.

The foghorn sounded, two low notes. Then nothing.

Eleven seconds later, it sounded again.

She felt that interval in her teeth.

He held a coffee out across the rail the way you'd offer something to a stray. "I didn't know how you take it, so I didn't do anything to it." The horn went. He waited it out, and she saw that he was counting too. "I'm going to ask you to tell me I'm wrong about something. I'd like you to actually try."

She took the cup. It was hot enough to hurt through the paper, which made it the first honest thing that had happened to her since Monday.

*Eyes on his face, love,* said the voice at her shoulder, thin as a draft. *Not the van.*

She hadn't known there was a van. Now she did. White Sprinter on Mason Street, engine off, a laminated sign in the windshield: GGNRA / FOG-SENSOR SURVEY. The lamination was new. A jogger in a black headband had already passed the hut once, too evenly, without looking at the water.

Her people were out there in the dark. Kino on the seawall with his hood up. Ava beside him, toe an inch short of the hut's bottom step, where a strand of bull kelp lay tied in a knot nobody ties. Caleb against the service wall. Malachi under the cypress, being a man who wasn't there.

She wrote *6:24, Crissy, TQ* at the top of a clean page. It was a habit. It was also so he could watch her do it.

He laid the pages on the wet rail one at a time, squaring each edge with his thumb. It was the only nervous thing he did. A scope trace, soft from folding: a long slow pulse, *11.0 s*. A tide-gauge plot from Alameda with a wobble circled in red. A photocopied notebook page of figures in a small careful hand, and at the bottom, underlined so hard the pen had torn the paper: ***WHERE FROM?*** And under it, *Unit 8 — "Melnikov"?*

"Here's what I need you to tell me is wrong," he said. "My machine isn't reacting to the bay. The bay is reacting to my machine. And my machine is eating something I didn't put in it." He turned his phone to show her stake holes in grey mud. "Last night at 7:46 something took a hand off my coil, and the bay got better. Four centimeters. Your colleague stripped the metadata. He didn't strip the waterline."

Her pen stopped on 7:46.

That was when the first stake came out. She didn't say so. She didn't look at him for a second, because her face would say it for her.

"You're wrong about one word, and it's *my*," she said. "A coil that rings with nothing plugged in isn't your machine. It's somebody else's instrument sitting on your pad. You built the part that shows up on a scope. Whatever's doing the eating has its own yards and its own trucks, and its registered agent is on Sand Hill Road. It isn't yours, Mr. Quinn. It's *in* yours."

At *Sand Hill Road* he closed his eyes for exactly one horn.

"Bayline," he said. "Bayline Remediation is a vendor under Applied Field Research. I signed the group's charter in 2023. I've never read its vendor list." He said it like a man reading his own name off a charge sheet.

"Who's Melnikov? You put your fingers on him like a grave."

"A machinist in Dogpatch. Died in August. Cardiac event, fifty-one." The coffee in his other hand shook once. "My cousin wrote that name in a column of power figures in September, with a question mark. Then he died too."

Adonis. The *T.* on the back of a stone in Colma. She wrote nothing and kept it off her face.

* * *

The jogger came back on her third pass and stopped at the foot of the steps to stretch. Heel on the rail, head down. Eight feet away. Red nails, chipped.

*Something in that van is sweeping the deck on the horn,* Abernathy said. *Cold and grey, and every pass hangs longer on your bag. Where the Lamp's kipping. Say the word and I'll stand in front of it.*

*No. You're down four and I'm not trading you for a bag.* She shifted as if the wind had got to her, left shoulder to Mason Street, the bag behind her hip.

Then the jogger's head came up, not toward the gravel where a tall man in a coat had come down out of the cypress, unhurried, hands empty. It turned the way a compass needle turns. She looked straight at Malachi and said one word to the bud in her ear.

Arenya didn't hear it. She saw him not hear it. His stride never changed. It was the most frightening thing she'd seen all week.

The jogger pivoted off the rail. Her left shoe came down on the kelp.

The squeal was thin and sharp, like feedback. The woman's hand flew to her ear, and for one second her face wasn't flat at all. On Mason Street the Sprinter's roof gave a soft hydraulic *tick*, and the cold beam went to the woman and off Arenya's bag.

Arenya swept the five pages into the folder and pressed it to Typhus's chest. "Hold that against you. Walk with me." Then, bright and annoyed, the voice you use on a friend who's late, into the tail of the horn: "*Mr. Shawani!* There you are! Do you know how long we've been standing out here?"

It was the name on his lease, the only one she was allowed to say. She was giving the listening ear something louder than the name he didn't use.

"You don't know her, you don't know him," she murmured to Typhus, "and you're having a normal meeting with a reporter."

As the woman passed, Malachi said something low. Arenya only caught the end of it. *Overpaid.*

The woman's stride broke. Half a step. Then she was walking east into the fog with her hand pinned to her ear, and she never looked back.

The Sprinter pulled off Mason without lights. The cold lifted off the deck like a hand taken off a shoulder.

"That was for me," Typhus said, very quietly. "Or for you. I can't tell which, and I don't like that I can't tell." He wrote a second number under the first on a plain white card. "The top one is mine. The bottom is Elias's personal line. He'll find out who copied your badge. I'll find out who wrote Unit 8." He hesitated, and then decided something. "Last night at 9:14, the Mill's intake logged an object it couldn't classify. *INTAKE EXCEPTION 0001, NO SOURCE.* The system has never thrown that error. It's in a lockbox in the Lantern control room, two floors above my coil. I don't know what it is. I think you do."

*Love.* It was not the put-on voice. *My wrist. Left one, where the cuff sat. It went cold in a ring. Exactly the size of it.*

She wrote *9:14* and underlined it. "If you shut the coil down, whatever's eating your machine finds another mouth," she said. "So don't make it a switch. Make it a throttle. Lowest draw that keeps your logs honest. Copy everything to someone who isn't on Sand Hill Road's payroll. And don't be alone in that control room." A beat, dry, because she couldn't help it. "Thank you for the coffee. It was awful. I finished it."

"Adonis would have liked you," he said. "He'd have argued with you about the throttle."

It was the first thing he'd said all morning that wasn't in order.

The sun cleared the East Bay hills. The horn sounded. Eleven seconds.

And then, for the first time all morning, twelve.

* * *

Wednesday didn't happen in order. It came in pieces, and the pieces were all in a hurry.

Teddy called before the car cleared the gate. Monolith's comms office had phoned his editor at 6:40, asking about his *sources*. Plural. "Give me till Friday," Arenya said. "Print the bay. Don't print *sources*."

At 9:15 Rodel left a frightened, polite voicemail. Someone had left a crate of oranges at the Colma office for the Sowri household. No card. He had not accepted it. The photo came that afternoon: oranges on his carpet, every one turned the same way. Toward the door.

The clocks ran fast all morning. The board at 22nd Street showed a train *departing* while it was still pulling in. Ava picked one honest clock, the GPS on her old watch, and wrote the offset beside every entry as if the truth might hold still if someone kept books on it.

In Nana's mailbox, between a PG&E bill and a pizza menu, was a pink carbon folded in thirds. Pressed so hard it had embossed. *Thursday, after the low water. I can tell you where they'll set it. I need you to tell El Contador I helped. Colma, the 110 stop, tonight 7. Come alone or don't come. —H*

"Nobody writes like that," Arenya said, "unless they're scared or want us to think so."

"People who ask to be vouched for," Malachi said, "are usually guilty of something worse." He didn't forbid it. He never did.

*Tell me about Porter,* she asked on the bond. *Even just the shape.*

For a long time, nothing. Then: *It's a weight, love. Like a word you've said so many times it's gone smooth. When the page opened I said "yes" before I'd had my own say.* A pause. *And the voice that asked wasn't this one. Flatter. Older. Tired, and sort of polite. Feels less like something I did and more like something I was called.*

It wasn't better. She didn't say so.

At 1:20 they pulled over on Judah outside a laundromat. Its clock said 1:23. Ava's watch said 1:20.

"Plus three," Ava said, and wrote it down. Then she turned in her seat. "Hey. You ate. Good. How are you doing? Not the plan. You."

And she waited. Actually waited.

Arenya opened her mouth to say *fine*, and the word wouldn't come. So she told the truth to the laundromat clock instead.

"Not good. Monday I buried my aunt in somebody else's grave. Tuesday I found out a stranger paid a hundred and one dollars to put my name in a temple book. I used to write *ten signs your cat is secretly depressed*. I knew which one to put at number seven so you'd keep scrolling." She made herself look up. "Here's the worst part. I'm not scared the way I should be. I'm scared the way you are on a roller coaster. Some part of me has wanted this my whole life. The cracks in the world. And now I'm standing in one, and people are dying in it, and my family's in it, and I still like being right. That's an ugly thing to find out about yourself on Judah."

Ava didn't say anything clinical. For once she didn't have a symptom for it.

"Ask me again after tonight," Arenya said, quieter. "I'll have a better answer. Or a worse one. But it'll be honest."

Across the street the clock ticked over to 1:27.

It was, by every other clock, 1:24.

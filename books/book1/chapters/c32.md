# Chapter Thirty-Two: Hat in Hand

The salt ran white under Nana's gate, a finger's width from the toes of Malachi's shoes. He had stopped exactly there.

He knew where to stop.

Arenya dried her hands on the dish towel, front and back and between the fingers, and went down the walk and stood on her side of the latch.

"Hat in your hands," she said. "That's new."

He didn't answer. He was good at that.

"Here's how this goes. You don't cross until you've said two things to my face, out here, where my grandmother can hear you through the window. Your real name. And who pays you. Not *a concerned party*. A name."

Malachi spoke toward the kitchen window, evenly, so the woman at the table could hear him.

"Mrs. Sowri. My name is Malachi Shawani. I told your daughter I was with the Department of Energy. That was a lie. I work for the Central Intelligence Agency. I'm a case officer, attached to a small domestic cell that was never meant to exist on paper, and I was sent to this city to watch people die of something I'd seen before." He turned the hat a quarter turn. "My team thinks I'm working a lead. They don't know I'm standing at a gate in Daly City. This is my own paper, and it's thin. If it comes out, it costs me, not you."

From the glider, flat: "Scar's cool. CIA. Okay. I'd have guessed worse."

"Wow," said Arenya. "Bronze medal for the cover story."

It came out dry, because dry was what she had. Then she looked past him at her mother in the window, sitting very still, and the dryness went out of her.

"You lied to my face in my own apartment. I'm not going to pretend that's fine because you took your hat off." She lifted the latch. "You tell my mother what you told me, including the lie, and you don't ask her to forgive it. Come in, Malachi Shawani. Don't scuff the salt."

He stepped over in one long stride, high and clean. Nothing touched the line.

The salt noticed anyway. Where his shadow had passed over it under the porch light, a stretch of the white line the width of a hand had gone grey, the color of pencil rubbed on a thumb.

Nana came down one step with the wooden spoon still in her hand. She looked at the grey, then at the left pocket of his coat.

"Not you," she said. "What you carry. Give them here, the way they are, in the cloth. Arenya. The blue jar under the sink, the one with the lid."

Malachi laid the handkerchief across her palm by its knot. "Left on the lamp table at the temple. I haven't put them on."

The bundle went into the blue jar the way a sleeping cat goes into a box, one soft fold and then still. Arenya pressed the lid down. Mist rose inside the glass from the bottom up, as if someone had breathed into it, and through it came a sound almost too small to hear.

*Tk.*

A pencil point touching paper, once.

"A man's shadow doesn't stain my salt," Nana said. "What's in his pocket does. You carried them clean. It still marks. It just marks less." She tipped her head at the grey. "Fresh salt over the old, mija. Don't sweep it. Sweep it and you've only moved it."

Arenya poured. The white settled over the grey, but the grey didn't fade underneath. You could still see where it was, the way you can see a scar under a sleeve.

Nana set the jar on the top shelf, a hand's width from Susmithaa's canister.

*Don't look at the shelf, love,* said Abernathy. *There's a thread off that jar now, finer than anything we've seen, pulled tight. Downhill. The 110 stop. That tick was a pencil checkin' a line. We've been counted again.* And then smaller, in the low register he never let her hear for long: *And the walled-off voice. I haven't forgot I owe you it.*

* * *

Over pozole, Malachi told it with his hands flat on the table where Amma could see them. The market eleven years ago, and the file that came back with one line: *forget*. "I did. That's the first thing I'm ashamed of." The nine deaths his cell had counted. Then a last thing, which nobody had made him say: earlier that week he'd run a highway patrol query to find Arenya's car, and it was on the record under his name. "If anyone from my agency comes to this door, that's how."

Amma turned the capped Sheaffer end over end once and set it square with the table edge.

"Nine that your four people count. And for eleven years you forgot, because someone wrote one line on your paper." She looked at the pen. "My husband also signed what someone put in front of him."

She left it at that. It wasn't forgiveness and it wasn't a sentence, and Malachi, to his credit, let it lie.

"Now I am telling you something," Amma said. "That paper has my name on it too. *L. Sowri*, with a tick. Tomorrow my husband sleeps. If anyone in this house goes to that door, it is me. I am the one with the pencil already next to my name."

"No."

It came out too loud for Nana's kitchen. Arenya put her hand flat on the table beside her mother's, not on it.

"Amma, check me like I'm wrong. Who ticks a box before anyone has signed? Someone who expects to need it. Appa's R is one. If you walk out to that door, you're not standing in front of him. You're the second. You're the person they ticked the box for." Her voice cracked and she kept going. "I held that cold in my hands this afternoon. I'm not giving it back to you at nine tomorrow so you can go and be brave at a gas meter."

Then they laid it out, mostly Kino, like a trail. Malachi at the curb, outside the salt, where the runner would have to stand. Arenya inside the gate. Caleb on the glider. Kino in the bus shelter at the bottom of the hill by 8:10, to see which way the man walked. They would trade the runner his eyes back, and they'd trade nothing else. If Malachi touched the brim of his hat, nobody answered until he'd finished. Nobody would say Susmithaa's name within the man's hearing, or Appa's, or Amma's.

Amma looked at her daughter's palm lying on the table for a long time. Then she put her hand into it, with the grip she'd used crossing El Camino when Arenya was six.

"Behind you," she said. "Yes. But barefoot, the way I stood this morning. And I keep this in my pocket." The Sheaffer. "Not to write. So that I know where it is."

Nana turned off the burner and set her terms with the spoon. "One. The jar goes down on my comal, the wooden side, from nobody's hand. Two. Our copy of the form doesn't leave this house. If he wants it closed, he closes it on *his* paper, with his own pencil, and you make him say out loud that he's done it." The spoon found Malachi. "You told us you're good at that. Three. Nobody here says the dead girl's name to him. He hasn't earned it."

* * *

Nana's porch light stayed off on Halloween. When a small witch came up the walk anyway, Nana met her at the salt and passed three Mazapanes across the line with the wooden tongs.

"¿Y tú, quién eres?"

"A witch," said the girl, and nothing more.

Nana nodded, satisfied.

Arenya slept in pieces on the floor against the bed frame, a hand's width from Amma's bare foot. At 2:17 she was awake before the jar was.

*Tk.*

Before dawn she asked the bond once more. *Say it now, before nine.*

Abernathy answered her straight, with no patter, and that told her more than the words did. The 110 was familiar, he said, the way a lift that drops a floor too fast is familiar. When the trolley woman said *pink transfer*, he'd felt *known*. And under the copy's voice the night before, back when it said *Kanna?*, there'd been another voice, walled off. Patient. It had known his name before he gave it. Something shorter than Abernathy.

*I'm not hiding it, love. I just can't reach it.*

* * *

At 7:48 on Sunday morning the fog had made the hill the edge of a page.

Nana laid the comal on the salt by the gate, iron face down, wooden handle toward the street, and lifted the blue jar down with a dish towel. Mist beaded the inside of the glass.

Kino stood in the doorway with his boots laced. Arenya put a hand on his sleeve for one second.

"Bottom of the hill. You watch which way he walks and you don't talk to him. I want you back, not heroic."

When he was gone she read Ava's text again. It had been on her screen since 7:49.

*Oven swung SW at 2:17 and has held 5.5 hrs. Not drifting. Pointing. Whatever's coming is coming to you, not past you.*

At 8:20 Appa woke too fast, the way men do in hospital waiting rooms. Overnight the grey had spread past the knuckle of his thumb and taken on the faint sheen pencil leaves on skin. He looked at it. He looked at Amma's bare feet on the linoleum. He didn't ask anything.

At 8:39 Caleb's voice came through the screen door. It started too loud and then dropped.

"Warm. Scar's warm. Like a hand's been on it."

Everyone went to their places. Arenya stood inside the gate. Amma came up behind her, barefoot, and Arenya felt her mother's hand settle on her shoulder blade. Malachi went out to the curb with his hat on and his empty hands where the street could see them.

Then Kino's voice came up through the fog, deliberately pitched to carry, because that was the rule.

"Two coming. Runner first. Dalal-ji behind him. No glasses, walking in his prints. Bus hasn't left yet." A pause. "Faces in the bus windows. They're all looking up here."

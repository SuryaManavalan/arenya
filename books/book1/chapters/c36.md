# Chapter Thirty-Six: Two's Gone Soft

At 11:11 on Monday night, a weather nerd posted that every tide gauge in the Bay had read flat for eleven seconds. *Sensor glitch??* Ninety-one replies, no answers.

At 6:58 on Tuesday morning, Ava opened her front door for the paper and said, "Okay, no."

A rope of bull kelp lay on the welcome mat. Wet, tied in a careful knot. No sand on the steps. No footprints.

Arenya crouched beside it in yesterday's sweatshirt and didn't touch it. The knot moved.

*Tick. Tick. Tick.*

"Three," she said. "Not eleven."

*Brass, love,* Abernathy said, very still behind her ear. *And old paper. Like the inside of a clock in a reading room. Salt and rot is the sea's. The brass and the library is somebody's shop.* The cockney had gone thin at the edges. *Smells like somewhere I've worked. Can't tell you where, and that's the bit I don't like.*

At the sink Ava put on dish gloves and waited a full minute by the stopwatch. Nothing. Then she leaned in to write, her hand hovering over the knot, and it ticked three times, slow and even, like a clerk confirming a signature.

To her. Arenya's palm stayed cool.

"Data," Ava said, not lightly. She bagged it, labeled it, and put it in the fridge, because Nana's rule was that you don't throw out a gift. The plastic fogged from the inside. "It came to my door, not yours. Whoever sent it knows which one of us drives."

Arenya wrote Teddy's six hundred words at Ava's desk. Tide gauges, cranes, port-property signs, an unmarked fleet truck idling. Facts and timestamps. She left out the kelp, the tide-woman and Kino's arm.

Witnesses are people, not quotes.

* * *

The pho shop on Noriega had sweating windows and a TV over the register playing the Colma fog clip with the sound off.

Malachi took the seat facing the door. Corinne Abate was in the back booth with her coat on and a broth she wasn't eating, and when Caleb came in behind him, her face did nothing at all. Malachi respected that.

"Who said my name," he asked, "and where? Exact words."

She had them by heart. "Sunday night, a broker's board we pay to read. *True name, one asset, Bay Area, consular-adjacent, verified by a woman who keeps names. Sold. Buyer pays in water rights.*" She took off her glasses. "Nobody who means money writes that."

*A woman who keeps names.* So the pandit had a broker, or a broker had dressed him up. Either way, the page Rodel had watched change hands was his.

Caleb laid it out with his hands flat on the table: the white roll-up door, the plate, the grey steel-toes, the two taps on the roof. Corinne turned his envelope face-down, the way you'd close a stranger's diary, and slid a printout across. *Bayline Remediation & Survey, LLC.* Brisbane. One line highlighted: *subcontractor, environmental monitoring, Hunters Point lease parcel B. Prime: Monolith Systems.*

"Eleven D-series box trucks. Your D2-07 is theirs. The yard's off Tunnel Avenue, right on the county line. Roll-up doors." She looked at Caleb's hands. "Which you knew."

"Nose-out, so nobody reverses on a bad night," Caleb said. "The doors hum. You feel it in your teeth." A beat. "It's not Drop Two."

Then the price, delivered pleasantly. "Who's the girl? I can pull you tonight, a flight and a new badge. Or you give me her name and she goes in a file I control."

"No flight," Malachi said. "Somebody paid for my name the same week a Monolith sub started staking the Bay. If I run, they learn what my name buys. I'd rather they learn it buys nothing." He lined his envelope up beside her printout. "She isn't yours to file. I'm running her as an access agent. You get her product, not her name. She's the reason you have a fleet number at all."

Most of it was true, and the true parts carried the rest. Corinne read him from the bottom up, like a cable.

"Tuesday the tenth. The yard, the lease, faces, on my desk. Or I pull you whether you say yes or not." She dropped a twenty for the broth. "And the ping on your old file didn't come from Brisbane. It came from a terminal with our own access. Don't call the 703 number." A napkin, a number in pencil, no name.

At the door she glanced at Caleb's left pocket, the one he wasn't touching. "A wall is for keeping track of people who might come back," she said, not unkindly. "Don't."

* * *

Teddy read the piece at 4:52. *Clean. Running it 6 a.m. tomorrow. Who's the truck?*

Bolt cutters lay across Arenya's lap like a cello. At 5:52 on 101, at Cesar Chavez, the dash clock read 5:52. Then 5:54. Then 5:58. The tide app counted down too fast, and every heart in the car went *late, late, late.*

It wasn't a car. It was the *lanes*: a shape the length of the interchange made of taillights and a hundred thousand commutes' worth of *I should have left ten minutes ago.* It had no head. It had a front, and the front was wherever the gap was. Brake lights bloomed across four lanes for nothing.

"No," Ava said. "That's not a clock. That's a symptom." She held fifty-one and ticked two-second beats on the wheel with her thumb, slow and boring and stubborn. "We are exactly as late as we are. Not one second more."

Caleb held the off-beat on his own wrist. Kino breathed out through his teeth the way he did for deer. Over the roof, Abernathy stepped into a queue of coats in the bulk, every one tapping a watch, and the brass at his collar went cold and officious. "Ticket, please. No? Then you're not next." The nearest coats flinched. *Don't ask me where I learned that.*

Arenya let the lamp come up behind her eyes, and the freeway went see-through. The thing wasn't heading south. It was being *drawn*. At the Bayshore split its front bent east, toward the dark stack at Hunters Point, and thin bright threads of hurry peeled off the traffic and ran downhill into the shipyard like rain into a gutter.

"Don't fill the gap," she said. "Let it have the gap."

The phantom lights went out. The clock settled at 5:53.

"The Mill isn't just drinking the tide," she said. "It's drinking the city's hurry."

* * *

By 7:40 the Bay had drawn back past where it had any right to be. Sixty yards out, the second piling stood in the dark, and three iron stakes were driven around it in a triangle, with the survey rod in the middle. Across the cove, under a yard light, the box truck sat in its own yellow puddle.

With the lamp behind her eyes the triangle became something else. A ruled line ran from each stake, blue-grey and taut as piano wire, *uphill* over the mud toward the stack, and the seawater slid along them in bright threads. The rod was where all three were tied off. A pen dot at the end of a sentence.

"Pictures first," Arenya said, and shot every stake.

At the first she coaxed the line, warm and slow, a thumb working a knot loose. Kino wrapped both hands low on the iron. "*Perdón, señora.*" Caleb's hands closed under his. "On the tide's beat." It came up with a long wet *suck*: eighteen inches of black square-section iron, stamped near the head with a small ruled box, the kind you'd tick. Caleb's palms went white where they'd touched it.

The rod went quiet. Not fading. *Stopped*, like a reader looking up from the page. Across the cove the white door rattled up a foot.

Abernathy was out where the lines met, one brass-buttoned cuff laid against the second line. *Wrong page, ma'am. Nothing here.* He drew the cuff across it the way you strike a figure from a column.

It missed by a hair, and the line turned the page back.

She saw it through the bond. A column of figures in an old copperplate hand, a heading with its top line smudged, and under it a single legible word.

**Porter.**

The line leaned toward him like a nib finding its place. His collar cinched, and he made a sound with no cockney in it at all.

She crossed the mud at a crouch-run and laid her left palm on the line above his brass. "Hold. I've got you. Wrong man. Strike it, clerk."

A figure ducked under the door across the cove. Grey steel-toes, hi-vis, a long handheld light with the white door's hum in it. The beam didn't touch the mud. It lit the *lines*, and it began walking down the second one toward them, slow as a finger down a page.

"Worker out," Ava called from the riprap. "Nobody stands."

*I'll not be read by a stranger's lamp,* Abernathy said, rough. *That page had a name on it, love, and it wasn't one I gave you.*

He unbuttoned the brass cuff with his free hand and slid his wrist out. The cuff stayed on the line like a coat left on a hook. He went grey and flat behind the piling.

*Porter,* she sent after him. *I saw it. I'm not asking now.*

*After. Not on the mud.*

Kino and Caleb had the second stake out before the beam arrived. A small *pock*, and the mud closed. The line went slack under her palm, the lamp ran gold along it, and the page beside the piling *shut*.

But the empty cuff still hung on the line, and the line was going home. It reeled itself across the shine toward the stack, and the brass rode it, an arm's length past her fingers. Then two.

She let it go. *A stake is the whole point.* It was a lie, and she knew it.

The beam stopped on the water ten yards short of the piling. The man lifted a radio. His voice carried, bored and precise.

"*Two's gone soft. Logging it.*"

The third stake came up like a tooth with nothing left holding it. "That's three," Arenya whispered. "Abernathy, I'm sorry about the cuff. We're coming back for it."

*Leave the rod,* he said, thin and dry. *Touch it and they'll feel it in the stack.*

At 7:51 the man went back inside, and the white door came down. At the water's edge Kino touched two fingers to the wet. "*Gracias, abuela. Tenemos tres. Falta lo demás.*" We have three. The rest is still owed.

Under his fingers the water pushed once *against* the current, like a hand closing over a hand. Out on the flats something old settled lower with a long exhale. The reek of rust thinned until it was only salt.

Not free. Looser.

* * *

In the car, with the heater on and Caleb holding his burned hands palms-up in his lap, nobody talked.

Arenya watched the windshield fog. She was thinking of one word in an old hand, and of a man she had never once seen frightened.

In Ava's pocket, the kelp letter ticked.

Once. Twice. Three times.

A fourth.

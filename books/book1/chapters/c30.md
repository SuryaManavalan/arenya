# Chapter Thirty: We Have Our Own

Halloween morning. Down the block someone had put a plastic skeleton in a lawn chair, and fog had beaded on its skull.

The kitchen door was round the side, past the recycling bins and the curry-leaf plant in its cracked blue pot. The back step had been swept, and nobody swept it on a Saturday. The door stood open an inch. KQED murmured inside.

Appa sat at the kitchen table in his old PG&E fleece. Amma's chair was empty and her temple bag was gone from its hook. In front of him was a cup of filter coffee gone cold and a manila envelope with no stamp and no postmark, addressed in block capitals.

**R. SOWRI — NEXT OF KIN.**

It was still sealed. His green Sheaffer lay across it with the cap on, the pen he'd signed her college forms with.

He didn't look surprised. He looked like a man who had sat up all night deciding not to open something.

"Kanna," he said.

The last thing to call her that had been a kitchen light with nobody in it. For one second her body couldn't tell which one this was. She made herself look at real things: the fleece, the glasses, the skin on the coffee.

It was him.

She tipped Nana's salt onto the swept step in a short grey line beside Kino's white one and stepped over both. She slid the Sheaffer off the envelope with two fingers, toward his side of the table, cap still on.

"Don't open that. Don't sign anything. I know about the check, and I know somebody texted a phone at eleven-fourteen last night with your name on it and the words *we will come to him*. So I'm going to ask you one thing, and I need the answer not to be *it's handled*." She breathed. "In March. Who handed you the pen?"

He took his glasses off his head, folded them, and set them on the envelope where the pen had been. He was an engineer. He liked something with weight sitting on paper.

"Not handled," he said. "I will stop saying that."

The burner ticked.

"March nineteenth. Rodel's counter. Susi wanted a witness, and I was the only brother she had. Rodel's pen skipped, one of those ballpoints on a chain. There was a man behind us in line, very polite, navy coat, a mailbox key in his other hand. *Here, use mine.* He held it out cap first, like that is good manners." Appa looked at his right hand. "I am a man who takes a pen when one is offered. I wrote the R."

He turned the hand over. A grey stain ran along the inside of his forefinger and the pad of his thumb, where a pen rests. It wasn't ink. It was pencil lead, faint and even, and it had plainly never come off with soap.

"Susi put her hand on my wrist. Not hard. She took his pen out of my fingers and gave it back to him. *Thank you, we have our own.* Then she took this one out of my shirt pocket and held my wrist while I wrote *Sowri*. So the name on her paper is half mine and half his. The R is in his ink." He breathed out through his nose. "An hour later Rodel telephoned to say the same man had rented a box."

He touched the envelope's corner.

"This was on the step at five, with grey dust along the step in a straight line, like chalk off a ruler. I swept it. Then I decided that if they need a next of kin to sign something tomorrow, it should be me. They have my R already. I am sixty-four. Better my hand than yours, kanna. That is what I meant by *handled*."

Behind her, beyond the salt, the curry-leaf plant stirred in its pot. There was no wind.

"No."

She sat down close enough that their knees almost touched and turned his right hand over in both of hers. It was cold, but only along the stain.

"Appa, listen the way you'd listen to a load calculation. They don't want *a* next of kin. The text says *custody chain incomplete*. If you sign whatever's in there, you're not taking the hit for us. You're closing the chain *for* them. That's handing him the pen back cap first." Her voice cracked on *cap*. She kept going over it. "Itzel Lucero says to tell you that you can stop being brave alone now. She said you came to her porch in March and wouldn't come in."

His hand went slack. For a moment the retired engineer was gone, and in his place sat a younger brother who'd been told to wait in the car.

"Susi was already at Itzel's table with the salt," he said. "Itzel held the screen door open. And I stood at the bottom of the steps and thought, *If I go in, I am one of the household. Then they write Lakshmi down. Then they write you.*" He laughed once, wetly. "Itzel said, *Raghavan, a man standing outside is still standing somewhere.* I thought it was an insult."

He wiped his eyes on his sleeve and stood. Coat off the hook. Keys in the left pocket, wallet in the right. He left the envelope where it was, glasses folded on top, and tucked the Sheaffer into his shirt pocket and buttoned the flap over it.

"Mine," he said. "Only ever mine, from now on."

At the door he stopped at the two lines of salt, and she watched him remember the bottom step in March. Then he stepped over them in one long stride.

The curry-leaf plant went still.

Behind them the beige wall phone rang once, stopped, then rang once more and stopped again.

Like somebody counting.

Nobody answered it. Arenya carried the envelope out by its edges like evidence and put it in Kino's arms, and he laid it on the truck floor under Nana's old blanket with a pinch of salt on top.

Malachi had walked the house while they talked. By the side door, the face of the gas meter had fogged from inside, and someone had written in the fog with a fingertip, in neat block capitals: **R.S. — SUN 9:00.** The dials underneath read 4471, the true number. While he watched, the letters ran like breath on a mirror.

* * *

Amma's temple didn't look like a temple from the street. It had been a Lutheran church. The steeple was painted saffron now, under a vinyl banner: SRI VENKATESWARA SATSANG — SAT. ABHISHEKAM 7:30 AM. Kids in Halloween costumes ran between the cars with silk pavadais bunched under their capes. A Spider-Man in a veshti went by at full speed.

It was the most ordinary place Arenya had been in four days. That should have been a comfort.

In the old vestibule stood a long wooden shoe rack. Amma's brown Clarks were on the second shelf, toes in. And on the bottom shelf, at the end, sat a pair of men's black oxfords, size ten or eleven, polished, toes pointed out at the door, laces tucked inside.

Malachi crouched as if to tie a shoe he wasn't wearing. Packed into the seam where the upper met the welt was a fine grey dust, shining faintly, the way the ruled line on the step had shone. Ix put his nose in the left shoe and sneezed once, hard.

"*Pencil*," Kino murmured. "*Sí, flaco.*"

"If a man in socks offers you a pen—" Malachi began.

"We have our own," said Arenya.

The back kitchen was all steam. Amma stood at a big pot in her plain Saturday cotton, a wooden paddle in both hands. She looked up and saw the three of them in jeans and socks, and saw Appa's face, and her voice dropped a register. "Raghavan. What happened?"

At the steel prep table by the open back door a man sat grating ginger. White veshti, sacred thread, silver at the temples, reading glasses on a cord. He had a plain blue ballpoint tucked behind his right ear, the way a shopkeeper keeps one, and he was wearing black dress socks.

"This is Dalal-ji," Amma said. "From Fremont. He does the archanas at Livermore. He came to help with the pepper." That came out a little dry. "He knew Susmithaa."

He stood and folded his hands. His voice was soft and enormously kind.

"Sowri-garu. My deepest condolences." His eyes rested on Arenya mildly, the way a man looks at a child whose horoscope he has already read. "Your sister did a beautiful archana for this one in March. One hundred and one dollars. Very generous. Sit, sit. There is plenty."

Appa's grey hand closed around Arenya's fingers hard enough to hurt.

She slid her free arm through Amma's until the paddle tipped down. "Thank you, Dalal-ji. We won't sit. And we won't take a pen, or a pen cap, or a receipt. We have our own."

Malachi put himself one pace to Dalal's left with his hands empty. "Mr. Dalal. A box in Colma was held for you on vacancy. You'll forgive us. We're leaving by the door that's open."

Kino filled that door from outside, in his socks, with daylight behind him.

Amma laid the paddle across the pot, reached past her daughter, and turned off the flame, without hurrying. Then she spoke Tamil over the rim of the pot, low and flat, in the voice she used on contractors.

"*You cashed it. I saw the back of that check. My husband never wrote like that in his life.*"

Something crossed Dalal's face. It wasn't fear. It was more like a man revising an estimate. He lifted the ballpoint from behind his ear, and Malachi's weight shifted. Dalal only laid the pen on the steel table, flat, with the cap pointed at himself.

"Box?" he said gently. "I don't keep boxes, sir. I keep names." To Amma, in careful Tamil: "*I said her name to God, amma. That is my work. God has many ears in this country. Some of them pay.*" And to Appa, softer still: "Tomorrow at nine, someone will come to your side door with a pen. It will not be me. It will be someone who does not know the words." He picked up the grater again. "If you want someone who does, I am here until the prasadam."

They walked out past the cinder block into the parking lot in their socks.

Amma turned Appa's grey hand over in the sun, and her mouth went thin. "*Since when.*"

"Since March, Amma," Arenya said, before he could take it on himself. "We'll tell you everything. In the truck."

Kino and Malachi went back in for the shoes together. The oxfords they left where they were, toes out, waiting.

Amma stepped into her left Clark and stopped. She bent, slid two fingers inside, and drew out a slip of paper folded once. It was pink, the kind the temple office tears off for every archana.

**ARCHANA — SRI VENKATESWARA — SAT 10/31**
**NAME: LAKSHMI SOWRI · STAR: ROHINI**
**PAID ✓**

It was written in blue ballpoint, in a neat, careful hand.

She folded it again along the same crease and held it out to her daughter flat on her palm, the way you'd hand someone something hot.

"I did not ask for this," Amma said.

# Chapter Thirty-One: Since When

On 280 South the fog sat on the hills like wet wool, and the heater in Kino's truck only worked on high.

Arenya laid the pink slip on her knee, ink side down, and told her mother everything.

She started with August and the man on the Montgomery platform who'd stared at a stranger for forty seconds and fallen down. Then the nine. The wall of string she'd taken down before Deepavali. Teddy's keycard. Then March, and a hundred and one dollars, and a polite man handing Appa a pen.

"And since this morning you're on it too, Amma. So you get everything, out loud. Nobody writes any of it down. And nobody in this truck says *handled* again."

Amma didn't interrupt once. One hand rested on Ix's head without her knowing he was there. At *Susi-athai* her thumb stopped moving.

"March," she said, very level. "Susi called me. A Sunday. A family archana for health, she said, and the priest wanted everyone's star. I was making dosa. I told her yours from memory, and your father's." She looked at the back of Appa's head. "I didn't say mine. She didn't ask for mine. So somebody else knew it."

"Lakshmi—"

"No. You had since March."

The cab went cold along the floor, a draft with no window open, the way a room cools when a door opens somewhere else in the house. Ix's ears went flat.

"Amma," Arenya said. "Say it with me. Her whole name."

"Susmithaa Sowri," said Amma. She said it the way she'd say it to a clerk who had spelled it wrong.

A beat later, roughly, from the front seat: "Susmithaa Sowri."

The cold didn't leave. It *pulled back*, sliding off their ankles like water down a tilted floor, and pooled behind the seat, under Kino's jacket, where the envelope was.

* * *

When Kino killed the engine at Nana's gate, everyone heard it. A small dry *tick* from behind the seat, like a fingernail on paper. Then another.

"No entra cerrado, mija," Nana said from the gate. "Nothing sealed with their glue comes in my house. On the salt. Half in, half out. You carry it with the hand you don't sign with, and not near your chest." She took a wooden spatula from her apron, worn black at the edge from years on the comal, and held it out handle first. To Kino. "He opens it. With wood. Not a blade, not a pen. Whoever reads it is the one whose name is on it, or whoever stands for that name."

Arenya pulled her right hand up into her sleeve and carried the envelope down the walk at arm's length in her left. Its ticking ran up her wrist like a pulse that wasn't hers. She set it on the line, half in and half out, and the ticking stopped mid-count.

"It's Appa's name," she said. "So it's Appa's call."

Appa looked at it for a long time.

"Since March," he said. Then, to her, quietly: "Yes. I want to know. I don't want to *not* know any more."

He held out his clean left hand without looking, and Amma took it and stepped onto the salt beside him. Arenya took the other side. The grey hand hung between them, closed.

The flap didn't tear under the spatula. It *peeled*, dry, like an old bandage. Inside was a carbon set, white over pink, folded in thirds. Kino flicked it open with the wood.

> **RETURN OF PERSONAL EFFECTS — CUSTODY RECEIPT**
> REF: PROXIMAL / 11
> ITEM: One (1) container, sealed. CONTENTS: SOWRI, SUSMITHAA. CONDITION: intact.
> PRESENT LOCATION: c/o LUCERO, I.
> RECEIVED BY (NEXT OF KIN): **R** ________________________
> ALTERNATE SIGNATORY: L. SOWRI ✓
> *On countersignature this entry is closed and any balance carried forward to the next proximal reference.*

The **R** wasn't typed. It was pencil, the same soft grey as Appa's thumb, with a small tremor in the downstroke where someone else had been holding the wrist. The tick beside Amma's name was the same grey. In the corner was a faded round stamp, a shepherd's crook inside a circle. The pink copy was complete except for its lower-left corner, which had been torn away in a ragged little triangle.

Nana read *LUCERO, I.* upside down from the step. She said nothing. She only reached behind her without looking and put a hand on the doorframe of her own house.

"That's what the slip bought," Amma said. Her knuckles had gone pale on Appa's hand. "If you won't, they ask me."

*Guv,* said Abernathy in Arenya's ear. *Two threads. Grey one from that R into your dad's thumb, tight as fishing line. That one's set. Thin one from your mum's tick down to her left foot, where the slip was. Slack, but takin' up slack. Slow. Get that foot bare and I reckon it's got nothin' to bite.*

Arenya knelt on the salt. "Amma. Left shoe and sock. Now. Please."

Amma held her shoulder for balance. Her bare foot on the salt was brown and narrow and the coldest thing in the yard.

"Oh," she said, surprised. "That's better."

*Gone limp as wet string,* said Abernathy. *Your dad's still taut.*

Nana took the sock in the comal tongs and dropped it on the coals. It burned like any cotton sock, quick and blue-edged, except that for one second the smoke smelled like a freshly sharpened pencil. Everyone looked at the form. Nothing on it changed. The grey tick beside *L. SOWRI* stayed exactly as neat as before.

"So," Nana said. "Fire takes the thing. Not the writing." She set the heavy stone pestle from her molcajete square on the form, half in the salt and half out, like a paperweight on a grave.

The screen door creaked. Caleb stood in it with a dishtowel over his shoulder, looking at the crook.

"That's a runner's book," he said. "They don't use it on the crew side. It's for the ones who *close*. I saw one once, two Februaries ago, in Pacifica. Linda Mar, a stucco house with a boat in the drive. Mr. Okafor. His wife was ours." He didn't say *proximal reference*. They could all hear him not saying it. "The runner stood at the curb all night, clipboard flat against his chest like a hymnal. Black oxfords, grey along the stitching. Lanyard turned backward. At seven-forty in the morning Mr. Okafor came out in his robe and signed on the hood of his car to make him go away. The runner thanked him and walked to the bus stop on Highway 1 and got on the 110. Never a car." He swallowed. "He's polite. They're always polite. He takes his shoes off if you ask."

"So he comes," Amma said, "and he stands, and he waits for one of us to get tired." She lifted her chin. "Then we don't get tired."

Inside, with eggs and café de olla in front of him, Appa took the Sheaffer out of his pocket with his left hand and slid it across the oilcloth, not to his daughter but to his wife.

"Keep it till Monday," he said. "I don't trust my hand."

* * *

By noon Appa was asleep sitting up on Nana's couch with his grey thumb facing the ceiling.

Amma wouldn't put a shoe on her left foot. "It's cold," she said. "Not the floor. *Me*. From the ankle."

Arenya sat on the kitchen floor and held the foot in both hands. It was like holding a glass of water left out overnight.

"Tell me when it's warmer. Or when it's not. Either is data."

"You and your data."

"It's all I've got, Amma."

At 3:12 the hall phone rang once and stopped. Nobody moved.

*They're checkin' the line's still answered by nobody,* said Abernathy from the counter. *Polite lot.*

Kino and Malachi spent the afternoon in San Bruno. Where the oxfords had stood on the bottom shelf there was now a clean rectangle in the dust, two shoes' worth, crisp-edged, as if they'd been lifted straight up. Dalal's reading glasses lay folded on the lamp table by the shrine door with the cord coiled around them like a small neat snake. Malachi picked them up by the cord and wrapped them in a clean handkerchief.

At the bus shelter on El Camino, a woman with a wire shopping trolley was glad to talk to Malachi's face. "The polite one? Saturdays, the 110 to Linda Mar and back. And he asks for a *transfer*. A paper one. They don't do paper anymore, I've told him. The driver gives him one anyway. Pink."

At the Daly City station, under a concrete overhang that smelled of brake dust, two prints in grey dust sat in front of the Bay C bench, toes out. On the posted timetable, in faint pencil, someone had ticked the first Sunday run toward the hill and written beside it: *8:12*.

"Hillcrest by eight-forty," Malachi said. "A walk up to a gas meter with time to spare before nine. He's not hiding it. He's expected."

Arenya read Kino's text about the pink transfer with her hands still around her mother's foot.

*Somebody up the line wants him ridin',* Abernathy said. Then, more quietly: *I keep thinkin' the 110 sounds familiar. Don't ask me why, guv. I'd rather you didn't.*

She didn't.

Just after five the foot gave. It was small, like a knot slipping. Amma said "*Oh*," surprised, the way you'd say it when a stair turns out to be there after all.

The cold went into Arenya's hands. It came up through her palms to the wrists, patient and complete and not unkind, and it lasted three heartbeats. She counted them, because that was what she did. Then it was in neither of them.

Amma flexed her toes and put on her sock, then her shoe, and looked at the shoe on her own foot for a long moment as if it belonged to a stranger who'd been very kind to her.

"Kanna. It's gone."

"I know."

"Wash your hands with salt before you eat, mija," Nana said, without turning from the stove.

At 5:58, in an empty bakery on Guerrero, the oven light Ava had been watching all afternoon swung once, toward the coast, and held there for exactly as long as she held her breath.

At 6:20 Kino's truck came up the hill. Malachi got out and walked to Nana's gate, and stopped there, outside the line, with his hat in both hands.

Behind Arenya, a chair scraped. Amma stood up on two warm feet.

"He is waiting to be asked," she said.

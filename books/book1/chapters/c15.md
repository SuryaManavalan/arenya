# Chapter Fifteen: Don't Be Rude About the Chili

Two days of soft, buffering nonsense. Then the buffering stopped.

Arenya was standing in her parents' backyard in Daly City, and the backyard had too many directions. She could see the fence from the front and from behind at the same time. She could see the curry-leaf tree from inside its own growing. Fog poured along the ground, and it wasn't weather. It moved like a tide with an opinion. Thick. Patient.

Breathing.

At the bottom of the garden stood Susmithaa Aunty's cottage, except the cottage window *was* the brass lamp, the kuthu vilakku, grown to the size of a temple, and every one of its wicks was burning.

Her aunt stood barefoot at the center of it, and she was not her aunt. Or she was, the way a sunrise is a light bulb. A woman made of lamps. A thousand small flames in the shape of a woman, braids of fire with tiny silver charms ringing in them. Far beneath her, the way you see the bottom of a pool, a small woman in a cardigan stood in the real garden, perfectly still, staring at the street.

Half a step behind the burning woman, a lanky figure flickered in and out of the frame. Shabby long coat. A light swinging on a chain like a lantern. He was swearing steadily in something that might have been cockney.

Four men stood around them.

Arenya couldn't hold on to their faces. The faces slid off her attention like rain off glass, smudged thumbprints, each man wearing a halo of grey static. Between them a net was being drawn tight: humming filaments that *pulled* like magnets, dragging flame after flame toward the center and snuffing it. One of the men wore a light grey jacket.

She had seen that jacket before.

It had been on her wall.

*This is a dream,* she told herself. *This is the cheese-before-bed dream. You took the wall down and your brain is putting it back up in Amma's garden.* She said it twice. It didn't help, because her feet were cold, and you don't feel cold feet in dreams.

The flames were going out one by one. Susmithaa turned her head through an angle that wasn't an angle and looked straight at her.

"Kanna." It was the most frightened Arenya had ever heard her. "You came. Of course you came. *Listen to me.*"

"Aunty." It came out small, six years old and lost in the Serramonte mall. She swallowed and tried again, in the sharper voice she used when she was scared. "Okay. I'm listening. I'm *listening*. Tell me what to do."

She didn't wait for an answer. She reached, the way you'd reach for someone slipping on a wet step, toward the nearest flame in her aunt's burning hand. And as she reached she made herself look at the man in the grey jacket. Not his face; her eyes kept skidding off it. The jacket. The collar, the seam at the shoulder, the way it hung. *Details. You can always hold on to details.* She pinned him the way she'd pin a still frame. *Tartine. Corner table. You.*

"I know you," she said, and her voice shook. "I've got you on a wall."

The flame came to her hand and did not burn. It felt like Aunty's palm on her forehead when she'd had a fever at eight: dry and warm, smelling faintly of camphor and ghee. One silver charm rang against her knuckle, a bell no bigger than a grain of rice. For half a heartbeat the net's pull slackened around that single wick, as if it had to work out where the wick belonged now.

Susmithaa saw it. Something crossed her burning face that wasn't relief. It was a decision.

"Good girl," she breathed. "Hold that. Don't let go of that."

Then the man in the grey jacket turned toward Arenya.

He didn't turn his head. The static around him turned, the whole smear of him shifting like a satellite dish finding a signal. The collar was there, and the shoulder seam, and the way the jacket hung slightly long on the left, as if something heavy sat in that pocket. Then the details began to slide, the way a word goes strange when you say it forty times. One filament peeled away from the lamp-woman and drifted across the fog toward her. It didn't hurry. It was curious.

"—bleedin' *wire*, bleedin' *magnets*, who brings *magnets* to a—"

The lanky man had heard her voice. For one frame he was in two places at once, behind Susmithaa and at the edge of the lamplight near Arenya, coat flapping in a wind that wasn't blowing. Then he was only in the second place, slope-shouldered and wrong, standing between her and the man in grey.

"No no no no. *Oi.* Your Ladyship. Don't you *talk* to 'im." The patter was going too fast and too thin, a man whistling past a graveyard. "You don't *know* 'im, you've never *met* 'im, you're a nice girl 'avin' a cheese dream, ain't that right, lads? Nothin' to see!" He jerked his chin at the four smudges, grinning with too many teeth, then dropped his voice for her alone. "They count what they can see, love. Don't let 'em count *you*."

He swung the lantern hard and overhand and brought it down on the nearest filament, where it bit into a cluster of Susmithaa's flames. With his other hand, the one that never quite had the right number of fingers, he cupped the air around the flame Arenya held, like a man sheltering a match.

"Take that one. Go on. Just the one. *Hold* it, don't let go of it whatever she says." Halfway through the sentence his voice slipped, and for half a word it wasn't cockney at all. It was low and old and very sure. *"—keep it lit, child—"*

He caught himself, and the grin came back, wobbling. "Union rules. Hold the light. *Madam!*" Over his shoulder now. "Madam, they're closin' the east side, I can't hold the east side and the kid, pick one, *pick one*—"

Arenya closed her fist around the flame, hard, the way you hold a phone in a crowd. Her other hand was already writing in the air, because it didn't know it wasn't holding the coffee-stained notebook. *Collar. Seam. Hangs long on the left. Something heavy in the left pocket.*

"Left pocket," she said out loud, to herself, to the flickering man, to anyone. "Jacket hangs long on the left. Left pocket. Left pocket. Left—"

The words were already going strange in her mouth. She hung on to the shape of them like a rail.

The lantern came down on the filament with the sound of a struck tuning fork. For an instant the light on the chain flared white and the wire glowed cherry-red along an arm's length of it. Three wicks in the knot shuddered and leaned toward freedom.

Then the net did what magnets do. It took the heat and pulled it in. The glow ran down the filament like water down a drain, out of the lantern and into the knot, and the three wicks went out together—*pff, pff, pff*—little bright deaths. The lantern guttered to an ember on its chain. Two of the smudged men turned their static toward the lanky man. There was no anger in it.

Only inventory.

But the flame he was cupping stayed lit. Between his wrong-fingered hand and Arenya's closed one it burned as steady as a pilot light, and the curious filament slowed. It couldn't quite see past him.

In the center of the fire, Susmithaa stopped fighting for the east side. She let it go, a whole flank of her flames snuffed in one long exhale, so that she could turn fully toward the two of them. Down in the real garden the small woman in the cardigan swayed on her feet.

"I pick," she said. "*The kid.* Always the kid, Abernathy. Stand there. Don't move from there."

*Abernathy,* Arenya thought, and the name landed somewhere familiar and somewhere not.

The filament drifted closer. She didn't step back. She leaned away from it a little and kept the flame tucked against her chest. "Don't let go. Got it. I'm not letting go." Then, quieter, through her teeth, to the man in the static: "Whatever you are, I'm going to remember you."

*Left pocket.* She had the words. The picture was already leaving her. The collar went first, then the seam, then the long left hang, smearing into grey the way a dream face does when you try to describe it over breakfast. She was holding a sentence with nothing left inside it.

He didn't lose her, though.

The curious filament found the edge of Abernathy's cupped hand, slipped around it like a cat around a doorframe, and touched the inside of her wrist. Cold. There was a small, precise *click*, like a turnstile. Like a barcode reader.

*Counted.*

"No," Susmithaa said, and it was the voice from the engagement dinner, low and certain, sandalwood and silver rings. "Not her. You don't get her."

She stopped fighting.

Every flame left in her, a few hundred little lights, turned at once the way a flock turns, and *poured*. It came down her burning arm and across the impossible distance into the one wick in Arenya's fist. It wasn't heat. It was weight: every lamp lit in that window for every year Arenya had been alive. Her hand was full of morning. The net clenched shut on the place where the woman had been and caught nothing but ash, and the faint ring of silver charms falling.

What was left of her face was a few flames wide. It was close enough to kiss Arenya's forehead, and she did.

"You reached, kanna. That's all it ever was, reaching." The voice was fraying. "They aren't the war. They're people who think they can win it before it starts. Something old is waking. The doors are going to open, and every lamp will matter. Keep it lit. Go with all the light you can carry." A ghost of the old mischief crossed what remained of her. "And look after that one. He'll say he's looking after you.

"*He's lying.*"

Then she was gone.

Abernathy's coat tore loose from the place where she'd stood, as if a hook in him had been unpicked and set into someone else, and he was dragged across the fog to Arenya's side whether he liked it or not. Far below, in the real garden, the small woman in the cardigan folded down onto the wet grass beside the curry-leaf tree. She went gently, like someone sitting down to rest.

The four men stood around an empty net. Three of them were already turning away, the way crews turn at the end of a shift.

The one in grey didn't. His static had gone very still. It was the stillness of a man taking out a pen.

* * *

Arenya woke in her own bed in the dark.

Her right hand was clenched so tight her nails had cut half-moons into the palm, and the palm was warm, the way a cup stays warm after the coffee's gone. On the inside of her wrist was a cold spot the size of a coin.

The clock said 3:19.

At 3:20 her phone lit up on the nightstand. **Amma.**

And from the kitchen, through the half-open door, she heard her kettle being filled, and somebody swearing at it under his breath. In cockney.

She didn't look toward the kitchen. She couldn't, not yet. The kettle and the swearing went into the same drawer in her head as the garden and the fog and the lamp-woman, and she slammed the drawer with her whole body. Her left hand found the phone. The right stayed a fist and wouldn't open.

"Amma?" A croak. She cleared her throat. "Amma, it's three in the morning. What happened?"

She already knew.

In the kitchen doorway the kettle clicked off and the swearing stopped. He stood very still, one shoulder against the frame, the coat hanging wrong even at rest. In his hands was the lantern, burned down to a dull orange ember, and he held it the way you'd hold a sparrow.

*Don't answer straight off, love,* said his voice, thin and close, somewhere that wasn't quite her ear. *Let her get the news out herself. Better comin' from her than from your face.* A pause. *That's your aunt's light in your fist. Near enough all of it. Keep that hand shut till it settles, and don't go showin' it to anyone, because it'll still be warm tomorrow and it oughtn't be.* He tried the grin. It wouldn't hold. *Kettle's on. I can't drink it, I can't pour it, and I filled it anyway. Muscle memory. Don't ask whose.*

He looked at the coin on her wrist, and the patter stopped.

*That cold spot. Somethin' counted you. Don't scratch it. Don't look at it too long, neither. We'll talk after your mum.* Then, quieter, not meeting her eyes: *She said somethin' at the end. About me. You 'eard it.* The cockney went thin for a breath. *I don't know what she meant. I'd tell you if I did. I'm sayin' so now, before you start wonderin' alone. I don't know.*

On the line there was only breathing, wet and ragged and very far from the phone, as if Amma had forgotten she was holding it. Behind her a television was going in an empty room. A man's voice, Appa's, said *okay, okay, okay* to nobody.

"Kanna." Amma's voice was shaking. "It's Aunty. Appa went out because the dog next door would not stop. She was in the garden. Only in her cardigan, in this cold, sitting by the curry-leaf tree like she was waiting for somebody. He thought she was sleeping." A sound came out of her that wasn't a word. "They took her to Seton. They are saying they did everything. That's what they are saying."

Then came the part that frightened Arenya more than grief would have. Her mother's voice dropped, the way Amma lowered it in temples and at the DMV.

"And the lamp went out. Her lamp in the window. Thirty-one years, kanna, I never once saw it out. Appa went to look and there was oil still in it." A breath. "And there is a man here at the hospital. American, in a suit, very polite. He was here *before* us. He asked the nurse for Susmithaa Sowri by her full name, and he asked me…" She stopped. "He asked if she has a niece. Why would he ask that? Kanna, just come. Please come."

Arenya sat in the dark with her nails in her palm, a dead woman's morning shut inside her fist and the cold coin on her wrist, and said the only thing there was to say.

Behind her, in the kitchen, the kettle ticked as it cooled.

* * *

The first thing was the smell: burnt hair and hot plastic, rising off the top of his own skull.

The second was that he was sitting. Front passenger seat of the grey sedan, belt across his chest, hands open on his knees. Fog beaded on the windshield. Half a block up, a porch light snapped on at a stucco house, and someone inside was screaming a name he didn't know. *Aunty,* maybe. The word went into him like a nail.

At his feet the canister ticked as it cooled. Its little status light was green, which meant *full*. Behind the inspection glass there was only a grey drift of ash.

His right hand was inside his jacket. He didn't remember putting it there. It held a pen, and from the pen a cold thread ran up his forearm like fishing line with something small and alive on the far end. Somewhere to the south-east, it pulled. A woman.

*Awake.*

Dorsey sat behind the wheel with his chin on his chest. Pruitt and Hale were slumped in the back. All three had their eyes open. Nobody was blinking.

The Crown crackled, and his molars buzzed with it. *"—ive, status. Team Four, sta—"* Shepherd, cut to pieces. *"—canister reads full. Confirm col—"*

In the back seat, Hale blinked. His head turned, slow and even, the way a security camera turns, until it was pointed at Caleb.

Fourteen months of habit knew exactly what his face should be doing. For the first time, it wasn't doing it.

*Face. Do the face.*

He'd done it ten thousand times without thinking, and now he had to think, which was like trying to remember how to swallow. Jaw slack. Eyes soft, aimed eighteen inches past the glass. He breathed the way the others breathed, slow and even, while his heart hammered under it like somebody locked in a trunk.

"Lead," he said. Flat. Good. Flat was easy; flat was fourteen months. "Canister reads full. Collection confirmed. Team Four standing down."

Under the jacket his left hand let go of the pen and closed around the two Vesper doses. Not because he wanted one. Because he needed to know they were there. His right hand slid off his knee, slow as a sleeper turning over, and came to rest beside the seatbelt buckle. His thumb found the release and did not press.

Static. Then Shepherd, unhurried as a dentist: *"—copy, Four. Full canister. Good wo—"* The buzz in his teeth changed pitch, as if a finger had been laid on a wire. *"—Strand. Your filament's still out. Why is your filament still out?"*

It was. The cold line ran up his arm, south-east, to whoever was awake on the other end. In the back seat Hale's camera-head stayed on him. Dorsey's fingers twitched on his thigh. He was coming up.

"Residual tether," Caleb said. Bored. A man reading a gauge. "Target went down hard. Lift was messy. Something kicked back through the line. I'm reeling it in."

He did it as procedure, so it would sound like procedure. He pinched the thread where it crossed his wrist and *folded* it, the way you'd fold a wet paper chain, then pushed the fold back along the line toward the woman and shut it like a fist. He didn't snap it. He didn't want to hurt her, and he didn't want Shepherd to feel a snap. He wanted it quiet. A spent line.

The fold took. He felt it travel away from him like a knot down a hose and close somewhere warm. The pull dwindled to the ghost of a pulse, the kind he'd only notice holding perfectly still.

Not cut. Not gone. Somewhere a woman wore a cold coin on her skin that only he could find.

It cost him. Something behind his left eye cramped like a hand that had held a pen too long, and for one second the fog on the windshield had edges, and faces in it. Then it was fog again. The Vesper he hadn't taken, sending its first bill.

*"—copy. Tether clo—"* Shepherd sounded satisfied, or as close as he got. *"—Four, proceed to Drop Two. Canister goes hand to hand. No stops."*

Hale turned back to his window. He'd bought it, or he'd stopped looking. With Hale there was no telling the difference.

Dorsey sat up, started the engine without a word, and signaled on an empty street at three in the morning. The sedan rolled past the stucco house with its porch light and its screaming and turned toward the freeway.

"Canister's mine till the handover," Caleb said, in the old Lead voice. He lifted it out of the footwell and set it on his lap, both hands around it like a man holding a sleeping child. It was warm. Ash shifted inside. He made himself not think about whose. "Drop Two gets it from me. Not off the floor."

Nobody argued. Nobody ever argued with Lead.

The on-ramp light came up long and red, an empty intersection, fog haloing the lamps. His thumb found the release. His other hand slid under the canister to the door handle.

*Not for me,* he thought. *For her. For whoever's in here.*

Dorsey braked the way he did everything, smooth and on time.

*Click.* In a car this quiet, the buckle sounded like a rifle bolt.

Three heads came up together.

Fourteen months of someone else's reflexes were still in his legs, and for once he let them work for him. Belt off, door open on cold fog and wet asphalt, canister clamped to his ribs before the click had finished echoing. Left, away from the freeway, toward the black gap between two dark houses.

Behind him someone said "*Lead*" in a voice with no feeling in it at all.

He ran.

They didn't chase him. That was the worst of it. The light changed to green and the sedan sat through it, three men waiting for instructions the way a television waits for a channel. He went over a low wall and through a side yard—wet ivy, a recycling bin, a dog two houses down that didn't wake—and into the black slot between two garages, back against stucco, the canister warm against his ribs like something breathing.

Through the fog, the sedan's indicator ticked. Then it pulled away toward the freeway, unhurried. Going to report.

The Crown hummed in his teeth. Thinner, but there. And for the first time in fourteen months, the voice in it wasn't giving a status call.

*"Caleb."*

Soft. Patient. A man calling a cat in off a road. Nobody had said that name since the day the Crown went in. He'd forgotten it had a sound.

*"That canister's empty, son. You know it, I know it. Whatever you think you're carrying, it's ash. Come in on your own and this stays a malfunction. We'll fix the Crown, and you won't remember being scared."* A pause. *"You've got the tether folded, too. Clever. It'll unfold. They always do."*

On his forearm, the thread gave one faint pulse, south-east.

Caleb slid down the stucco until he was crouched in the wet dark with the canister in his lap. He said it out loud, because he no longer knew how to say anything inside his own head.

"My name's Caleb." Cracked. Too loud. He dropped it. "You said it like you own it. You don't. You don't get to say it again."

The Crown hummed. He could feel the man smiling in the static.

"It's not empty," he said, low. "Her name was Susmithaa. I remember the sound she made. I'm not coming in, Shepherd. Not to get fixed, and not to forget. I'd rather be scared."

*"Susmithaa,"* Shepherd said, tasting the name like a wine he hadn't ordered. *"Noted. Be seeing you, Caleb."*

The hum stayed. That was how Caleb knew he was listening.

So he stopped talking to the voice and started thinking like Lead, because the worst of it was that the voice was right: they'd track the Crown. And the thread on his arm was a trail he mustn't follow, because if he walked to that woman, he walked the team to her door. He patted his pockets. Dead burner. Knife. Keys. Three hundred and eighty dollars. *Water,* he thought. *Metal. Mass. Anything to muffle a signal.*

At the far end of the slot the yards fell away into a drainage cut: a concrete channel, black water, and the mouth of a storm culvert big enough to crouch in. Old corrugated steel, rusted to lace at the lip. The water was ankle-deep and so cold his feet went numb at once.

Three steps in, the hum in his teeth thinned. Five, and it was a mosquito in another room. Ten, and it was gone.

Silence. The first real silence in fourteen months, with nobody in his skull.

Then the silence filled with everything the Crown had been holding back. His hands began to shake and wouldn't stop. The cramp behind his eyes spread like spilled ink, and the dark grew grain, faces in the rust. His left pocket felt heavier than two little doses had any right to. And he remembered. All of it. Rooms, nets, names, the sounds people made.

He sat down in the water because his legs decided to.

Then, because a soldier takes inventory when everything else has failed, he started one. Out loud, flat and quiet, so the steel could hear.

"Tartine. The old man in the scarf. Yuri something. That was us." His jaw worked. "Tonight. Susmithaa. Daly City." The names kept coming, each one landing in his chest like a round he couldn't un-fire. "Dorsey. Pruitt. Hale. I never learned your real names. Fourteen months and I never asked."

His voice went, and came back too loud. It slammed off the corrugated walls and he flinched and clamped a hand over his own mouth. He was laughing, or something near it, with no air in it.

"*Gallows,*" he whispered into his palm. "Good. Still got that."

He wiped his face with the back of his wrist and looked at the inspection glass, at the grey drift behind it. The ash of a woman's whole life of light. The green status light pulsed on it, patient.

"I don't know where you go from here," he told it. "I don't know who to give you to. But I'm not handing you back." He drew the canister into his chest and rested his chin on the lid. "Not till somebody who can use you tells me what you are. Somebody not them."

He left the Vesper where it was. The pocket stayed heavy. He kept his hand away from it and counted drips, and waited for the shaking to stop, or for himself to stop noticing it.

It was 3:14 in the morning.

At three in the morning the fog lay over the Bay like a sheet over furniture, and Kino Balam sat on the cold rock above the East Peak fire road and asked.

He'd been asking since midnight, the way Nana had taught him: breath low, eyes soft, no demand in it. Below him the bridge towers stuck up out of the white, and the Sutro mast, and nothing else. The city was a glow under milk.

Ix woke in him before he knew why. It was like a cat standing up on your chest: weight shifting, claws not quite out. The jaguar was looking south.

Then the mountain moved.

Nothing shifted. The manzanita didn't stir. Not one pebble rolled. But he felt it through his palms and up through his tailbone, slow and enormous, a sleeper turning over in a bed she'd lain in for ten thousand years. She turned her face toward the city, toward something lit down there under the fog.

Her back came around to him.

The mark on his left forearm went cold.

Ix made a sound Kino had never heard from him. Low and unhappy. Almost a whine.

"Easy," Kino said. He laid the forearm across his knee and looked at the Lady's thumbprint, grey as river stone, a bruise with no heat in it. "I felt it."

He pressed his palms flat to the rock and asked again. Not for anything. Only *who*. He kept his breath slow. He didn't let the question curdle into *why not me*, though it tried. Under his tongue the Spanish was ready, the words he'd only ever said to Nana—*no me dejes*—and he swallowed them.

The rock gave him *where* the way a stove gives heat in the dark. South and low, under the fog: a small, steady warmth like a candle in a window. For one breath he smelled something that didn't belong on Tam. Burnt butter, and something sharp and medicinal, like a church that wasn't his. Ix's ears went flat. He didn't hate it. He respected it, the way he respected the bigger cats in Kino's dreams.

He took out his phone. It was three in the morning and she'd be awake anyway.

Nana picked up before the first ring finished. Her kettle, the radio murmuring in Spanish, and then the long pause of a woman sitting down.

"Mijo. I felt it. Like the bed tipped." Another pause. "Listen to me. She didn't turn her *back*. You're thinking like a boy whose mother looked at the baby. She turned her *face*. When the old ones look at something, it's because something new came into the house. A big fire. Old. Older than us, maybe. Not from here." The kettle began to scream, and she ignored it. "The land doesn't stare at what it isn't worried about or curious about. So which is it? That's your work. Not *why not me*. I can hear you thinking it from Novato."

He pressed down and tried to narrow the warmth. It blurred. South, low, under the fog—and then only fog, and his own pulse.

"Face, not back," he said, and breathed out something close to a laugh. "Okay. I hear you. There was a smell. Burnt butter, and something like a church. Not ours. Ix doesn't hate it. I can't tell you if it's worried or curious. I lost it in the fog."

"You didn't lose it," Nana said. Down the line came the scrape of a chair, pulled out for him. "You were *shown* it. There's a difference, and you'll learn it." Then, drier: "And bring the thing you're not saying. It fits on my table too."

He didn't answer that.

"Pon agua, Nana. The kettle. I'm coming down at first light."

He hung up before she could hear his voice change.

At 5:48 Ava Lindqvist woke with her face wet and both hands raised in front of her, cupped, as if she were holding something that would go out if she breathed on it.

There was nothing in them.

The radiator ticked. Her roommate's door was shut, as always. Grey light. Fog on the window.

She'd dreamed of a woman made of lamps standing in a garden in the sky. Four men pulled a net, and the lamps went out one by one until there was only one left, in somebody else's hand. She knew it was a dream. She had a literature review on dreams. But her palms were warm—just the palms, the way they are after a mug—and she hadn't held a mug.

"Okay," she said, very low, to no one. "Case report. Subject: female, twenty-five. Woke at 5:48. Bilateral palmar warmth, no stimulus. Tearful, cause unknown. Dream content: lamps, net, four men." Her voice wobbled. "Four men. Great. Very specific. Very *not* a coincidence, brain, thank you."

No fever. The warmth sat in her palms, steady, and it smelled faintly of camphor and ghee. She decided she was inventing the smell. She wrote it all in the dream log with the time, because that was what you did.

In the kitchen the microwave didn't blink. It read **2:41**, steady. So did the oven. Not flashing, not counting. The clocks had lost power and then decided to stop instead of reset. Her phone said 5:51, two percent, nothing overnight.

The oven door was black glass. Ava stood in front of it in her sleep shirt with her reflection faint in the grey light, and in the reflection her hands were cupped at her chest.

She looked down. Her hands hung at her sides.

She looked back. The woman in the glass held a single flame, gold, the kind that sits on a brass lamp, and it lit the underside of her reflected chin. It didn't flicker when the radiator ticked. The camphor was very strong now.

Her first instinct was to look away. She made herself not.

"Okay. Okay okay okay. Hypotheses." She raised one finger; the reflected finger rose too. That much matched. "One: sleep deprivation, hypnopompic imagery, common. Two: me. I'm what's wrong. Three…"

She didn't say three. Her mother's kitchen rose up in her head and she shoved it back down. *Not now. Document.*

She filmed the oven door at two percent battery, narrating like a protocol, because her voice held steady when it had a job. "5:51 a.m. Oven clock reads 2:41, static. Reflection shows a lit flame in the hands. My hands, direct observation, are empty."

Then she did what she'd tell any participant to do. She moved. She stepped left, and the flame stayed in its cupped hands. She crouched, and it didn't leave the frame. A hallucination would drift, or lag.

It didn't drift. It was perfectly, insultingly consistent.

She held her breath. In the glass the flame guttered, as if her breath had been feeding it. When she let the air go, it stood up again, steady and gold.

"Hi," she whispered. "If you're a symptom, you're a very *consistent* one."

And past her reflected shoulder, the window behind her was not her window. A brass lamp on a low shelf. Marigolds, browning at the edges. An old woman's hand, a ring on the smallest finger, reaching for the wick to pinch it out.

The hand stopped.

It did not pinch.

"I know you," Ava said, barely a sound. "Not you. The lamp. I don't know why I know the lamp."

The oven clock jumped from 2:41 to **5:52**. The microwave beeped once, like something waking. In the glass, the hands were empty. So were hers.

She plugged in her phone beside the toaster and didn't watch the footage. She typed with unsteady thumbs: *5:52. Flame responds to breath. Second scene in reflection: brass lamp, marigolds, old woman's hand, ring on little finger. Hand stopped. Did not extinguish. Smell: camphor. Clocks reset.* Then, because the protocol said to: *Subjective state: scared. Not psychotic-scared. Different scared. (Flag: is that a thing I can tell?)*

She set the phone down and, very carefully, cupped her hands again before the glass.

"Don't go out," she said. "Whoever you are. I've got you."

* * *

Kino stood a moment longer with his back to the Lady and his face to the city glow, his forearm bare to the fog. It wasn't a prayer. He didn't trust himself to pray, not tonight. He said it quietly, to the mountain and not to Nana.

"I'll go where you're looking. You don't have to turn around for me."

Then he started down the fire road in the dark, without a light, the way he walked on patrol. Past the water tank the wind off the ridge changed. It stopped hitting his back and came level with his shoulder, the way someone falls into step beside you.

He didn't look up, and she didn't turn. But for the length of one switchback the cold in his forearm eased, as if a thumb had pressed there in passing.

At its eastern edge, the fog had begun to go pink.

* * *

In the Inner Sunset, the oven glass showed nothing now. No flame, no lamp, no window that wasn't hers. Only a tired woman in a sleep shirt holding nothing.

But her palms were warm. Not on the skin. Under it, steady, about the size of a candle flame. It stayed when she breathed and it stayed when she didn't. It didn't need her breath anymore. It had only wanted to know she would hold it.

On the counter the phone buzzed once: a calendar reminder she hadn't set. No title. Time: 3:20 a.m., already past. When she tapped it there was nothing there.

Outside, the first N-Judah of the morning ground by on Irving, and the camphor finally left the room the way a guest leaves. Slowly, and looking back.

* * *

Seton's emergency entrance hummed like a refrigerator. The automatic doors opened half a second too slowly, as if they were thinking it over.

Past triage, the family room was a box of beige chairs and a dying ficus. A muted TV on the wall ran an infomercial for a knife that could cut through a shoe. Amma sat under the fluorescent lights in her nightdress and Appa's PG&E fleece, her plait half undone, holding a paper cup of water she hadn't touched. Appa stood at the window with his back to the room. He hadn't sat down. Arenya understood from his shoulders that he wasn't going to.

When Amma saw her she made a sound that wasn't a word and held out one arm. Only one. The other hand wouldn't let go of the cup.

On the chair beside her, wrapped in a hospital blanket the way you'd wrap a child, was Susmithaa Aunty's brass lamp. A crack fine as a hair ran through the glass chimney.

"They let me keep it," Amma said into Arenya's shoulder. "I wouldn't give it to them. Is that stupid? I wouldn't give it to them."

"It's not stupid." Her throat closed on the second word, and she waited it out. "You keep it. Nobody's taking it."

Past her mother's shoulder, through the open door, she could see the nurses' station. A man in a dark wool coat leaned on the counter: tall, very still, much too awake for four in the morning. He held a lanyard ID face-down under two fingers, the way you'd hold a card you didn't want read. The night nurse was shaking her head. Arenya watched the nurse's eyes slide down the hall toward this room and away again. It was the glance of someone who had just been asked a name.

Amma pulled back and looked at her properly. "Kanna. Your wrist. Why are you holding your wrist?"

She hadn't known she was. Her left hand had been clamped over the cold spot the whole drive.

"Steering wheel." Too fast. She said it again, slower, the way you fix a typo. "I gripped it wrong the whole way. Pinched a nerve, probably. It's nothing, Amma."

She made herself let go. The cold stayed anyway, a coin pressed into the skin. She tucked the wrist into the crook of her mother's elbow, inside the fleece, where nobody could see it. The right hand stayed shut. She hugged Amma with her forearms and chin, like someone carrying groceries.

*Don't look, love.*

Abernathy stood at her shoulder, his shabby coat not quite moving in the air-conditioning, flickering at the edges.

*Don't look at the nurses' station. Look at your mum. That bloke in the coat, guv—he's got no light on him at all. Not hidden. Not shuttered. Nothing. Like a hole in a fence you could put your hand through.* A pause, and something older crept under the next words before he caught it. *I've never seen a living man like that. And I don't like how it itches, that I might have.*

She didn't look. She looked at her mother's hair.

*If he's askin' after a niece, he'll be down that corridor in a minute, and you want to be a girl cryin' on her mum, not a girl with a mark.* The flicker of him moved toward the blanketed shape on the chair. *And that lamp. It's not out, Arenya. It's listening. Keep it close. And keep your fist shut, yeah? Please.*

It was the *please* that landed. Abernathy didn't say please.

"Amma." Into the hair, barely moving her lips. "Is that the man? The one who asked about the niece?"

Amma went stiff in her arms, which was an answer. "He was here when they brought us in. Very polite. He said *niece*, kanna, not *daughter*. Like he knew which word to use." Her fingers found Arenya's sleeve. "Appa told him we have no niece. Appa is a bad liar."

At the window, Appa's shoulders rose and fell once.

Arenya got the man in pieces, the way you read a menu over someone's shoulder. Early forties. Shaved, but only since yesterday. Black oxfords with a white line of winter salt along the welt, which meant he wasn't from here. His hands lay perfectly still on the counter: no tapping, no phone, the hands of a man who'd been taught to keep them where people could see them.

The nurse said something short and final. He nodded as though she'd given him exactly what he came for, tucked the lanyard away without turning it over, and straightened.

Then he looked down the hall. At the open door of the family room. At the lamp in its blanket. At her.

He started walking. On the TV, the knife went through another shoe.

He stopped two paces inside the doorway, which was as far as courtesy allowed, and kept his hands where the family could see them. He looked at the lamp a moment longer than he looked at anyone. Then at Appa's back. Then, last, at Arenya.

"Mrs. Sowri. Mr. Sowri. I'm sorry for your loss, and I'm sorry about the hour." Low and even. "My name is Shawani. Department of Energy." He showed the card for exactly as long as it took to read and put it away. "I asked the nurse about your sister-in-law because her name came up in a review I'm part of. I should have waited until morning. That was a mistake."

His eyes came back to Arenya. Too clear. They rested on her clamped hand and the wrist hidden under her mother's elbow, and didn't linger, and he said nothing about either.

"Ms. Sowri. We've met, in a way. I won't ask you anything in front of your mother. I'll be by the vending machines as long as you need." A beat. "When you're ready, there are two or three things I think you'd rather hear from me than from the people who come after me."

Arenya didn't let go of her mother. She turned only her head, and used the low, flat voice she kept for strangers who called her *hon*.

"You were in my apartment on Friday. You told me to stop. Now you're at my aunt's deathbed with a lanyard." She glanced at Amma, at the lamp, back at him. "Say 'review' one more time and I'll ask that nurse what kind of Department of Energy works the ER at four a.m."

Her right fist stayed shut against Amma's back. The coin on her wrist ached.

"Give me ten minutes with my mother. If you're still here after, you can buy me a terrible coffee and tell me who 'the people who come after you' are. Names would be nice. I take notes." She tightened her arm. "Stay where I can see you, Mr. Shawani. And don't come any closer to that lamp."

"Ten minutes." He didn't argue the terms. He took one pace back, out of the doorway, and stayed in her line of sight. "The coffee here is worse than you're imagining. I'll buy it anyway." His voice dropped for her alone. "Names, then. I'll give you one tonight, and you can decide what it's worth."

He inclined his head to Appa's back, the small bow of a man leaving a house. "Mr. Sowri. I'm sorry."

Then he was in the corridor by the vending machines, hands visible, facing the family-room door and not the exit.

Arenya did the thing she'd actually been asked to do. She sat Amma down beside the lamp, crouched in front of her, and took the paper cup out of her hands before it crushed. Left hand. The right stayed closed.

"Amma. Drink some of this, or I'll make you eat the ficus." It was a bad joke, and Amma almost laughed, which was the point. "Tell me what Aunty said last. Anything. Don't edit it, even the small stuff."

She set the coffee-stained notebook on her knee and didn't open it. "Appa," she said, softly, to the window. "Sit with us. Please. Ten minutes."

Out of the corner of her eye: the corridor, the coat, the hands in plain view. She was counting. Ten minutes wasn't a figure of speech.

Amma drank half the water in small swallows, like a child taking medicine.

"Dinner was Sunday. She made too much rasam, as always." Her eyes went to the blanketed lamp. "She was happy. Silly-happy. She said her Thursday friend was teaching her to make tamales and she was very bad at it. The grandmother in Daly City. Itzel." She sounded the name out carefully, the way Aunty must have. "She said, *If I die first, Itzel will come with pozole, and you must eat it and not be rude about the chili.* I told her not to say such things at the table." Her mouth worked. "That was the last thing. *Don't be rude about the chili.*"

Behind them a chair scraped.

Appa had turned from the window. His face was grey and dry. He sat two chairs away, not beside anyone, his big hands loose between his knees, and spoke to the floor.

"In March," he said. "She called me at work. She never called me at work. She said, *Ravi, if an American ever comes asking about my niece, you tell him there is no niece.* I asked her, which niece? She has only one." He lifted his eyes to Arenya. "She said, *Exactly.* And she hung up." A breath. "I thought she was being dramatic. Tonight I told him there is no niece." His voice cracked quietly on the last word, the way the chimney had. "I am a very bad liar, kanna."

Arenya didn't write it down, because Amma would see. She said it back to herself instead, twice, the way she memorized quotes. *If an American ever comes asking about my niece. Exactly. March. Itzel. Pozole. Don't be rude about the chili.*

She moved to the chair beside him, close enough that her shoulder pressed his arm, and took his hand in her left.

"You're not a bad liar, Appa. You're a bad liar because you love somebody. It's the only kind of bad I'd want." She squeezed once. "You told him the right thing. Anyone asks, they ask me first."

His hand closed on hers too hard, the way a man grips a railing on a boat, and then he remembered himself and eased.

"They gave me her things." Amma patted her purse, where a plastic hospital bag had been stuffed. "Her phone is in here. The nurse said it kept buzzing at three o'clock and nobody answered." She didn't open it. "I couldn't look. You look, later." Then, with the certainty of scripture: "But she said Itzel will come. So she will come. We will have to clean the house." The idea of a job straightened her spine an inch.

Arenya's knee had come to rest against the lamp's blanketed base. The brass was warm. Not body-warm; warm the way a chair is when someone has been sitting in it a long time and just stood up. For a second, less, she would have sworn the warmth moved. It didn't spread. It gathered, the way a cat resettles against you, toward the side of the lamp that faced her closed right fist.

Her phone said nine minutes.

In the corridor, Mr. Shawani had bought two coffees. He held one in each hand and hadn't sipped either. He was looking at the door, patient as furniture.

She let the last minute sit, because it was the only gift she had left to give. Then she kissed her mother's hair and took the plastic bag out of the purse with two fingers. "I'll keep Aunty's phone safe. Nobody touches it but me." And to Appa: "The lamp doesn't leave this room. If anybody who isn't family walks in, you call my name. Loud, Appa. Be a bad liar at the top of your lungs."

She laid her left palm on the blanket over the lamp for a second, not sure why, and walked out.

* * *

She took the nearer coffee without thanking him. She didn't drink it.

"Names. You promised one. Start with who sent you to my aunt."

"Nobody sent me to your aunt." Malachi kept his back to the wall and his eyes on the family-room door. "Her name was on a list I keep. People who travel to odd places and keep odd company. I put it there in September. I never knocked on her door. That's mine to carry, and I'll carry it."

He didn't touch the second cup.

"The name is Quinn. Adonis Quinn, who died at Tartine. He worked for Monolith, and Monolith's founder is his cousin. I don't know that they're the ones doing this. I know your wall had a man in a grey jacket on it, and my people have three more like him. And I know somebody reached this hospital before your family did. It wasn't me. I got here second."

A pause. Then, almost reluctantly: "My card says Energy. It's a courtesy, and I'd rather not say what it's really a courtesy for. Not here, not yet."

He looked at her then. At the left hand clamped over the wrist.

"Now one for one. What happened to your arm, Ms. Sowri? Don't say the steering wheel. I watched you drive in."

"Wow. You watched me drive in." Her mouth did something that wasn't a smile. "That's either surveillance or a crush, and I'm not sure which is worse."

She kept the coffee in her right hand, so the fist stayed closed around paper, and took her left hand off her wrist for the first time since the car. On the inside of the wrist was a pale circle the size of a coin, a little greyer than the skin around it. No bruise. No nail mark.

"I woke up with it at 3:19. It's cold. It's been cold for an hour, and I'm told it isn't frostbite." She turned the wrist so he could see it, but didn't hold it out. "That's your one. Here's my price. Adonis Quinn I already had. I want what's *not* on my wall. Who are 'your people'? And the polite American in the suit—do you know his face?"

Malachi looked at the wrist for exactly as long as it took to memorize it. He didn't lean in. But he'd stopped holding his coffee like a prop.

"I haven't seen that before. I'd tell you if I had. I'm not going to touch it, and I'd ask you not to let a doctor photograph it. Or anyone with a clipboard." A beat. "It's a bad habit of mine. If I don't understand a mark, I assume somebody else does."

He answered in the order she'd asked.

"My people are three analysts and a phone. That's all. A cell, not a department. Nobody upstairs would sign for it if I fell off the earth tonight. As for the man in the suit: no. I never saw his face. The night nurse says a man asked for your aunt by her full name at about twenty to four, before the ambulance paperwork was even filed. Polite. Signed nothing. He knew to say *niece*."

His gaze dropped to the cup in her right fist.

"You're holding that very carefully. I won't ask. But whoever's counting the people your aunt left behind, I'd rather it was me who noticed first. Is there anywhere you can be for the next few days where you're not alone and not at your apartment?"

She saw her knuckles go white and loosened exactly one finger, so the paper wouldn't crumple. A small concession. She hated that he'd seen her need it.

"Three analysts and a phone. So you're me with a lanyard. Great. Bronze medal." She set the coffee on top of the vending machine and put her left hand back over the wrist. "I'll be at my parents' house all week. It's Tamil, so there'll be forty aunties and a rice cooker the size of a car. You will not be coming to that house. If my father sees you again he'll tell you there is no niece, and he'll cry, and I'll never forgive you."

She tore a corner off a clean page and wrote the number of her burner. She held it out and didn't let go when he reached for it.

"One more. 'Nine names on a list.' Is Susmithaa Sowri the first of them to die, or the first you *noticed* die? Think before you answer. I'll know the difference by your hands."

His hands stayed open and still, and he let her read them as long as she liked.

"I've mixed two numbers, and that was careless of me. Nine are the dead. Freeze deaths, confirmed by my team. None of those nine were on any list of mine beforehand, and I think that's the whole problem. The other list is people my analysts call *unusual*. Your aunt was on it. She's the first name on that list to die." A pause. "I don't know if she's the first to be killed. Only the first I noticed."

His eyes went to the family-room door and back.

"And she didn't freeze. The nurse says she was found sitting by a tree in her cardigan. No fixed stare, no seizure, nothing like the others. Whoever did this either changed method or did something I haven't seen. I don't like either answer."

He took the paper by the edge she was offering and waited until she let go. He folded it once and slid it into an inner pocket without looking at it.

"I won't come to the house. I'll call once, tomorrow, and say only *it's me* and a time. If you don't answer, I won't call again." A beat. "Ms. Sowri, I'm sorry about your aunt. I mean that more than I'd like to."

She'd watched his hands the whole time. They hadn't moved. She hated that they were the first thing about him she believed.

"Nine dead. A list of *unusual*. And my aunt, who kept a lamp in her window for thirty-one years, lands on the wrong list and dies the wrong way." She said it flatly, a headline she was trying to make fit. It didn't. "She didn't freeze. Write that down, Mr. Shawani. If I find out that's why you stopped looking, I'm going to be very unpleasant."

She shifted her bag on her shoulder, where Aunty's phone sat under the notebook.

"Tomorrow. *It's me* and a time. If I don't answer, it's because I'm burying somebody. Not because I'm done." Quieter: "Thank you for saying sorry. I didn't think a man with a lanyard knew how."

She didn't offer her hand. In the doorway she stopped where she could see Amma and Appa and the lamp in its blanket, and said over her shoulder without turning: "Don't follow me in. And if you see a polite American, don't be polite."

He didn't follow. When she glanced back a minute later, the corridor held two coffees on top of a vending machine and nobody else. She hadn't heard him go.

Amma had fallen asleep sitting up, her head on Appa's shoulder. Appa was awake and looking at nothing, his big hand over hers. Arenya sat on the other side of the lamp and put her left palm back on the blanket.

Warm. Still warm.

At 5:51 the window behind Appa went from black to the color of weak tea. In her bag, under the notebook, Aunty's phone buzzed once, a single short pulse like a knock, and went quiet.

She didn't take it out. Not yet.

But her right fist, for the first time since the garden, uncurled a quarter-inch on its own. It loosened the way a hand does when it has decided it's safe enough to sleep.

* * *

Daly City at twenty to seven was fog and wet stucco. The houses climbed the hill like teeth, and every porch light was still burning against a sky that hadn't decided to be day.

Nana's door was unlocked. It always was when she was expecting someone.

Kino toed off his boots onto the newspaper she kept there for mud, side by side, the way he had since he was six. The kitchen smelled of rice and ginger and the burnt-sugar edge of a kettle she never let go cold. There were two cups on the oilcloth. One was full and steaming. The other was upside down on its saucer, the way you set a place for someone who hasn't come yet.

Or someone who isn't coming back.

Nana sat with her back to the window and her hands around her own cup. She was smaller than he remembered. She always was. Her eyes went to his face, then to his left arm, as if she could read the birthmark through the sleeve.

"Sit. You walked down a mountain with something on your tongue all night. I can hear it rattling in your teeth." She nudged the full cup toward him with one finger. "Drink first. Then tell me. Then I'll tell you whose cup that is."

She didn't look at the upside-down one. She was very carefully not looking at it.

The chair was the same chair. It creaked in the same place. He drank the way she'd taught him, with both hands, all of it, not talking. It was too hot. He drank it anyway. Then he set the cup down and turned it so the chip faced away from her.

"Nana." He got that far and stopped. His thumb found the birthmark through his sleeve and pressed, checking it was still there. "It wasn't only tonight."

He made himself look at her.

"Since September. Every time I dream on her, she's lying the way she always lies, and then she rolls. Slow. Like somebody in bed turning toward a door they heard open. I'm on her shoulder, and I slide off, and she doesn't notice." His voice stayed level, the levelness of a man holding something heavy out at arm's length. "Five, six weeks. I didn't tell you."

The kettle ticked on the stove.

"I told myself it was so you wouldn't worry. *Mentira.* I didn't want you to look at me the way you're looking at me right now. Like you already knew." His jaw worked. "*¿Ya sabías, verdad?* You set two cups."

At last he let his eyes go to the overturned one.

"Okay. I told you. Now tell me whose cup."

Nana let him sit inside what he'd said. She refilled his cup without asking. Then she reached across and turned the chip back to face her, where it belonged.

"*Sí, ya sabía.*" No apology in it. "Not the dreams. You hid those good. But you stopped eating my tamales on Sundays and started eating them standing up, like a man who's late." She sniffed. "Five weeks. *Ay, mijo.* You think you're the only one in this family who can count?"

She laid her hand flat on the overturned cup, the way you'd lay a hand on a sleeping dog.

"Hers. Thursdays, eleven years. A Tamil lady from down the hill, the cottage behind the blue house on Hillcrest. Susmithaa. She took her tea with too much sugar, and she brought me this terrible sweet that tastes like a candle." The corner of her mouth moved. "She kept a lamp in her window the way I keep the kettle on, so you know what she was. And you smelled her this morning, *verdad?* Butter and church. That's ghee and camphor. That was her, going." Her thumb tapped the porcelain twice. "I woke at a quarter to three and turned this over. Nobody had to call me."

Something in Kino's chest went very still. He'd spent half the night on a mountain chasing that smell. It had been a woman who drank tea at this table every Thursday, in the chair across from his.

Nana leaned in, and her voice changed into the one she used for teaching.

"Now listen, because you have it backwards and it's been eating you. When you're asleep and you hear the door, who do you turn away from? Nobody. You turn *toward the door.* The Lady didn't roll off you, Joaquín. Since September she's been listening to something. Last night it opened a crack, and a light went through." She let that land. "And the light didn't go into the ground with Susmithaa. It went to her brother's girl. The niece. The one who doesn't believe in anything." A beat. "Susmithaa worried about that one more than she worried about dying."

She sat back, watching him the way he watched hikers.

"So. You told me your thing. That's mine. Now you tell me what a champion does when his Lady is looking at a door."

For a while he said nothing.

"Twenty years." It came out flat. "Fasting on the ridge. Sitting in the fog till I couldn't feel my hands. You waking me at two to go ask the mountain if I could walk on her." He laughed once, through his nose, with nothing in it. "And she opens the door for somebody who doesn't believe in *anything*."

He heard himself. His mouth tightened. He pressed the heel of his hand against the birthmark, hard, as if he could push the cold out.

"*Perdón.* Your friend died. She drank coffee at your table for eleven years, and I'm sitting here sulking over her cup." Lower. "That's not what you raised."

He breathed the way she'd taught him: in for four, hold, out slow. When he looked up his eyes were wet, but his voice held.

"What a champion does." He turned the question over like a stone from a creek bed. "He doesn't stand in front of the door. He doesn't block it because he's jealous of what came through. He stands *beside* it. He watches who comes in, and he makes sure nothing else follows. Not the thing that's been eating the old ones."

He swallowed.

"Does she know? The niece? About the light, any of it?" His hand came off the birthmark and lay flat on the oilcloth, near the overturned cup but not touching it. "Does she know. No. Last night she knew her Amma was crying on the phone, and maybe she dreamed something she's already calling cheese." She lit the burner under the big pot. She was too kind to frighten anybody."

He didn't answer. He didn't need to.

By two o'clock the fog had come down over Daly City and stayed, and the Sowri house had filled the way houses fill when someone dies.

Shoes three deep in the entry. Folding chairs from nowhere. Aunties Arenya half-recognized sat on the sofa with their hands in their laps, and somebody's teenage son texted in the hallway with the volume of a boy being very quiet at a funeral. The kitchen smelled of coffee nobody was drinking. Appa had lit it an hour ago with a kitchen match. The flame was small and steady.

Arenya's right hand had stayed loose since Seton. It ached the way a hand aches after gripping something all night. Susmithaa's phone sat in the hospital bag by her feet. It hadn't buzzed again.

Then someone knocked, twice.

Amma's hands were in the sink, so Arenya got there first. She stepped over the third layer of shoes and opened the door.

An old woman in a pinned shawl. Behind her, a young man in a black shirt with a heavy pot held high against his chest. Fog behind them both, so thick the houses across the street were only porch lights.

She didn't need an introduction.

"You're Itzel." Her voice came out rougher than she meant. "She said you'd come with pozole. I'm Arenya. Susmithaa's niece. Please come in. My mother's going to want to see you."

She kept her right hand open and visible at her side. She didn't know why. It seemed like manners.

The old woman took both of Arenya's hands, pot or no pot, and held them. Her thumb passed over the right palm and stayed there half a second longer than courtesy. She didn't say what she'd found.

"Mija. Ella me habló de ti. *Mucho.*"

The young man toed off his boots without bending and set them in the pile, toes out. "Lo siento mucho," he said, to the height of a mother's face, and then he didn't look at Arenya again. He looked at the lamp.

Nana Itzel reached up and laid two dry fingers on Arenya's cheek. "Sí. You have her chin. She told me you'd be the one who opens the door." She stepped over the shoes without looking down. "Joaquín, the kitchen. Put it on the stove, low. Don't spill on her mother's floor."

He went down the hall with the pot. As he passed the alcove, the lamp flame lay down sideways.

It didn't flicker. Arenya had watched a lot of candles in the last day, and she knew what a flicker looked like. This was something else. The flame leaned toward the boy, flat and patient, like a dog's head turning at a footstep, and held the lean the whole length of the hall. No window was open. The furnace was off.

Her right hand answered it. A pulse ran up her wrist, warm, once, like a second heartbeat out of step with the first.

She said nothing. She closed the door on the fog, slid the bolt, and flexed her fingers slowly, so nobody would be watching her do it.

Amma came around the corner drying her hands on her kurta, saw the shawl, and stopped. "Itzel-amma," she said, and her voice broke on the second word. They met in the middle of the hallway, and Arenya looked at the floor to give them that. She picked up a pair of sneakers someone had kicked sideways. It was something to do with her hands.

In the kitchen, Kino had not stopped walking, because if he stopped the pot would tip. But his stride had shortened by half, the way it did on a trail when the brush went quiet all at once.

*If the flame leans when somebody walks past, you look at who walked past.*

It was him. He was the one who'd walked past.

The cold in his forearm climbed to the elbow. He set the pot on the back burner and turned the knob down to the lowest blue ring, and left his hand on it a second too long. It was the one thing in the room he understood. Then, over Amma's shoulder, he found Nana's eyes, and asked the only way he was allowed to in this house. *¿Soy yo?*

Nana turned the question over the way she turned tortillas, without hurrying. She shook her head. *No.* Then her gaze slid past him to the girl in the hall, who was flexing her right hand where she thought nobody could see. Nana watched the hand. She looked back at him, and the corner of her mouth moved.

*Not only you.*

*No* meant he wasn't the wrong thing in this house, and something in his chest he hadn't known was clenched let go. The second part took longer to land. When it did, it landed lower and colder than relief.

When he came back into the hall, Arenya was there, a foot away, holding somebody's sneakers. The lamp had eased upright, but it trembled toward the two of them, as if it couldn't decide.

"Her grandson," he said, and lowered his eyes to her collarbone, which was polite and gave away nothing. "Joaquín. Kino." The corner of his mouth almost moved. "I saw it. I don't know why it did that. I'm not going to guess in your mother's house." Then, because it was true and she'd just lost her aunt: "She sent my grandmother tea bags with too much sugar. Nana kept every one."

Something like a laugh caught in Arenya's throat. She put the sneakers down before she did something stupid with them. "She put sugar in rasam once. Said it was for balance. Nobody argued."

The flame wavered between them. She decided she was too tired to pretend she hadn't noticed.

"I'm not going to guess in my mother's house either," she said, low, under the murmur from the living room. "But I'd like to talk to you and your grandmother later. Somewhere with fewer aunties. About what she told Susmithaa, and what Susmithaa told me." She tipped her head at the white pressed against the front window. "And thank you, for whoever's out there. I'd rather someone watched that street than not."

He looked at her properly for the first time.

"He's called Ix. He doesn't like being thanked. He takes it as an insult and sulks for a week." Kino moved half a pace sideways, so they stood shoulder to shoulder facing down the hall, the way you stand next to someone at a trailhead to look at the same ridge. If the flame wanted to lean, it could lean at both of them at once. "Those go by the door, by the way. Toes out. In case anybody has to leave fast."

The flame sank back to a small upright bead, the way a dog lies down when the people it guards finally stop moving.

* * *

The house filled in around them. Amma settled Nana in the good chair. Two aunties from the Fremont side wanted to know if pozole was like sambar, and Nana told them gravely that it was exactly like sambar, completely different. Steel bowls came out, then paper ones when the steel ran out. Somebody's uncle asked for extra chili and was told, in a tone Susmithaa would have recognized, not to be rude about the chili.

Kino counted exits out of habit, then faces. At nineteen he stopped.

There were twenty.

The twentieth stood by the bookshelf near the alcove: mid-fifties, grey cardigan, a paper bowl of pozole he hadn't touched. He had the kind of face you forgot while you were still looking at it. Nobody was talking to him. The aunties' eyes went around him the way water goes around a stone. When Appa came by with the ladle, he filled the bowls on either side of the man and skipped his, without seeming to notice he had.

The man wasn't looking at the photograph, or the lamp, or Nana. He was looking at Arenya's right hand. He watched it the way an appraiser looks at a ring in a pawnshop window. Patient. Pricing.

"Arenya." Kino handed her a bowl, his voice level and a little warmer than before, a polite stranger remarking on the weather. "Quick, and don't look. The man by the bookshelf. Grey sweater. Whose cousin is he?"

"He's not Fremont side," she told the steam. "And he's not anybody's cousin. Everyone here has been fighting about seating since noon. I'd know." She held the bowl in her right hand on purpose, so the hand was busy and obviously just a hand. "Kino."

"Don't walk toward him. Don't walk away. Don't ask your father about him. Appa skipped his bowl and didn't notice, and that isn't rudeness. It's *made*." He picked up a second bowl he didn't want. "I'm going to stand by the alcove with my back to the lamp, where I can see the whole room. If he comes toward you, say my name. Just that. I'll be there before you finish it."

She nodded once. He went.

She found the black screen of the television and used it, the way she'd once used a laptop screen at Tartine. A grey cardigan, an untouched bowl, a face she already wanted to forget. She made herself keep it. *Mid-fifties. Left-handed. No ring.* Then she crossed to the sofa and sat between two aunties, who shifted without a word and patted her knee.

"Amma's going to say it's too spicy," she announced to the room. "And it isn't. Aunty would have said so."

And under her breath, to the one who could hear: *Abernathy. Look at him. Does he have light, or is he another hole in a fence? And before you answer, I haven't forgotten you've told me nothing since 3:19. We're having that talk later. Right now, look.*

*Not a hole, love,* he said, close and quick, the cockney sitting on him like a borrowed coat. *Dull. Middle-aged. Pilot-flame color. But he's wearin' something. Ring of frost round his head, like a hatband. His light bends round it, all smoothed over. That's why your eye skids, and the aunties' too. Nobody remembers invitin' him because the ring won't let 'em.* Half a syllable of something older, a voice that didn't say *love*. *Same cold as the corridor this morning. The coat at the hospital was a void. This one's a bloke carryin' a piece of the void on his head. Borrowed. Leashed, maybe. And he's pointed at your right hand, guv. Only that. Level as a spirit-bubble.*

*Eat your pozole. Keep the hand busy. Keep the lad close. And the talk later? Yeah. I know. I owe you one.* A pause. *Two, actually.*

*Eat something, though. Proper. You've had one coffee since yesterday.*

Across the room, Kino took the alcove with his back to the lamp and his shoulders loose. Every ten seconds he looked at something else, then came back. Behind him the flame stopped trembling and leaned hard toward the sofa, as if it were trying to see past him.

The man set his bowl on the shelf beside a row of Agatha Christies and crossed the living room through gaps that opened just before he reached them. An aunty shifted her chair. A child stepped back against his mother's knee. None of them looked up.

Three steps from the sofa, he stopped before the garlanded photograph, put his palms together and dipped his head, correctly, like someone who'd learned it from an Indian colleague a long time ago. When he straightened he was close enough that Arenya could smell him: wool, cold air, and faintly, impossibly, frost on a windshield.

"I'm sorry for your loss," he said kindly, in a flat Midwestern voice. "She was very good at keeping her hands still." His eyes dropped to the bowl in her right hand and came back up. "Eat. It's good pozole."

Kino was already there. He took the empty space at her shoulder where a mourner would stand, and the aunties' eyes slid off him. His eyes didn't slide off anyone. He looked straight at the man's face, the face he wanted to forget, and held it, and let himself be seen holding it. Behind him the lamp stood straight up, the way a guard dog stands when it's decided.

"She's eating," Kino said quietly, in the voice he'd use on a hiker off the trail. "Let her."

Arenya took a spoonful, chewed, and swallowed before she answered. She looked at the knot of the man's tie, not his eyes.

"Thank you. It's my aunt's friend's recipe. She'd be glad you liked it." She lifted the spoon a little toward the Christies. "You've got a good eye for her bookshelf. Did you know her, or did you just read the spines?" It was the voice she used on PR people who said *no comment* too fast. "Kino, this is the kind of man who sends a card. I'd like to get his name before he leaves. Wouldn't you?"

"Wouldn't mind it." Kino moved his bowl to his left hand and left the right empty and easy, the way he'd stand when a bobcat hadn't made up its mind. "Names are polite. It's a house of mourning. I'm Joaquín Balam. Friend of the family. And you?"

The man looked at Kino for the first time, a long, mild look, a man noting the make of a car in his driveway. Then he looked back at Arenya's tie-knot gaze and almost smiled, as if she'd passed something small.

"We never met," he said. "I read about her. For a long time. She was a careful woman." He considered the shelf. "*Curtain* is upside down. I don't think that was an accident." And to the question under the question: "Bell. People call me Mr. Bell. It's on nothing, so don't bother looking."

His head turned a degree toward Kino. Nothing else of him moved. "Your dog's very well trained, son. He watched the street all the way through." A pause. "I came in the back at eleven. Before any of you."

He reached into his cardigan pocket. Kino's whole body tightened. The man stopped with two fingers showing and waited, patient, until the boy breathed. Then he drew out a plain white sympathy card, already sealed, no name on the envelope, and laid it on the sofa arm beside Arenya's knee.

"For the family. Open it when the aunties go home."

He stepped back. The gaps opened for him again—an uncle turning, a child ducking—and he walked toward the kitchen and the back door, unhurried.

Kino didn't follow. A man who leaves by the back door after announcing he came in by it wants to be followed.

*Ix. Back door. Don't touch him. Where he goes, you go. Slow.*

Out in the fog, something low and heavy turned without a sound.

Ix took the scent at the back step: wool and windshield frost, slippery as a wet bar of soap held in the teeth. Two blocks downhill the man got into a beige Camry parked under a cypress, a rental barcode on the rear window. Then he did something Ix didn't understand and Kino, through the line, did. He reached up with both hands and *lifted something off his head*, like a man taking off a tight hat at the end of a shift. The frost smell dropped by half. What was left smelled like a tired man. And like blood. He sat with his forehead on the steering wheel a long time. A thin line of red ran from one nostril, and he wiped it on his cuff.

He made a phone call. Ix had no words for it, but the cadence came down the line: short, flat, a report. Then a longer pause, and something in the voice that on a person you'd call reluctance.

He drove toward 280. At the on-ramp, in the rear-view of a passing car, three characters of the plate: *7K*, and something that might have been an *R*.

*Enough,* Kino sent. *Come home. Slow, by the wet side of the street.*

He bent to pick up Arenya's empty bowl from the floor, a reason to be at her knee, and spoke to it.

"Beige Camry. Rental barcode. Plate starts 7K, maybe an R. He took something off his head in the car, like a hat, and his nose bled afterward. The cold on him is made. It costs him." He set the bowl on the sofa arm. "He called someone and sounded sorry about it. Write the plate on your hand, not your phone. Then let's feed your mother."

Her left hand had gone flat over the envelope as if she were smoothing her skirt. The paper was cold through her palm. She'd never had a sympathy card feel like a dead battery.

She pulled the cheap pen from under her notebook, turned her left wrist over, and wrote on the inside of it in small block letters: *7K_R? BEIGE CAMRY. RENTAL BARCODE.* Under that: *NOSEBLEED. CALLED SOMEONE. SORRY.* It was the first time she'd ever taken notes on her own skin, and her hand was steadier than she expected.

"That's the first useful thing anyone's said to me in two days," she said, and slid the card into her bag behind the notebook. "And tell Ix he's invited to the pozole. He can sulk about it."

* * *

The afternoon did what afternoons in a mourning house do. It went slowly, then all at once. The Fremont aunties left at four with Tupperware. The uncle who'd wanted more chili left at five, having had more chili. Appa fell asleep upright in his chair with the ladle still in his hand, and nobody took it from him.

By six the fog had come down to the porch rail and the windows were black mirrors. Four people were left in the living room. Amma sat on the floor by the alcove with her knees drawn up. Nana was in the good chair, her shawl unpinned. Kino, who hadn't yet decided he was allowed to sit, finally did, on the floor beside Amma with his back to the wall. He was the lowest person in the room, sitting next to the one who'd been crying all day. His sleeve was pushed back. The birthmark showed in the lamplight, pale and cold. He didn't hide it and he didn't point to it. It was there in case she wanted to ask.

And Arenya, on the sofa, had taken the card back out and kept her left hand flat on an envelope that hadn't warmed by one degree in four hours.

*You're not imagining it,* Abernathy said. *Not humming. Cold. Soaked through like damp in a cellar wall from ridin' too long next to that hatband. And there's a thread off the corner, guv. Fine as a spider's. Frost-light, runnin' off toward the back door. A tag. Like a ring on a pigeon's leg. Whoever's holdin' the other end knows where the card is, and while it's in your hand, they know where you are.* A pause. *Which is why he handed it over. Bit of a present. We could cut it, but it'd ring at the other end. Your call. I'm only the squire.*

On the shelf, *Curtain* was still upside down among the Christies.

"Bueno," Nana said, and breathed in like someone about to lift something heavy. "The aunties are gone. Now we talk."

Arenya lifted her hand off the envelope. Her palm was damp and numb. She held the card out flat on both hands, not yet handing it over.

"Before anything else. I think this is tagged. There's a thread off the corner, running toward the back door. Like a ring on a bird's leg." She heard how it sounded and didn't stop. "I'm not going to tell you tonight how I know that. Maybe soon. But if I carry it, whoever's on the other end knows where I am. Where we are. Can you put it somewhere that thread can't follow? Without telling them we noticed?"

Kino looked at the envelope, then a little to the left of it. "I can't see a thread. But the card's shadow doesn't sit right. It points at the hall." His voice was low. "It's a mark, not a bomb. That's why he left it and didn't take anything. Don't carry it. Don't burn it. If he's counting on us to notice, we shouldn't do the thing he can predict."

Nana didn't take the card. She looked at it the way she'd look at a scorpion on a tortilla: not afraid, just deciding where to put her hands. "Joaquín. The towel from the pot."

It was a thick cotton towel, still damp with steam, smelling of hominy and chile and forty years of her kitchen. She draped it over the envelope without touching the paper, folded the corners in like a tamal, and carried the bundle into Amma's kitchen. She opened the freezer and set it at the very back, behind a brick of frozen parathas and a bag of peas gone white with frost. She shut the door and stood with her palm flat against it.

"Cold hides in cold," she said, coming back and wiping her hands on her skirt. "And it smells of my kitchen now. Whoever holds the string will feel a house, and soup, and a girl who stayed home. Let them feel that." She sat down heavily. "We don't cut it. Cutting is a word, and they would hear it. We just bore them."

Arenya turned her right hand over in her lap and opened it, palm up, in the lamplight, where Amma could see it if she looked.

"Amma, I lied to you at the hospital. The wrist. It wasn't the steering wheel." She kept her voice quiet. "I'll tell you everything I can tonight, if you'll let me. I'm done doing this alone."

Amma was already looking. She got up on her knees, took the hand in both of hers, and turned it over the way she used to check for splinters when Arenya was six. Her thumb stopped on the heel of the palm.

"Your aunt's hands were warm like this," she said, very quietly. "Always. December, January, it didn't matter. I used to say it was the ghee. She used to laugh." Her grip tightened. "Your father knew. In March, he knew something, and he didn't tell me, and now *you*—" She stopped. Breathed. When she looked up her eyes were wet and furious and completely steady. "You don't lie to me again, kanna. Not about this. I don't care how strange."

"Sí. Everything," Nana said gently, the way you'd clear your throat before interrupting a prayer. She lifted her chin toward the bookshelf, toward the one spine standing on its head. "But first. Your aunt turned a book upside down, mija. That man saw it in one look. I have been sitting in this chair for four hours trying not to look at it." The faintest smile. "She always made me find the sugar myself, too."

Behind Kino's head the lamp flame didn't lean. It stretched a full inch taller, toward the shelf.

Kino got up quietly so Amma wouldn't startle. He checked the back door, the deadbolt and the chain, then put his palm to the cold front glass: nothing but fog, and under the porch one slow heartbeat that wasn't his. He came back and stood at the end of the shelf with his hands in his pockets.

"It's your aunt's book," he said. "Your hand should be the one that turns it. I'll watch the doors."

She squeezed Amma's hand and stood. "Two minutes. Then all of it, from the beginning, even the parts that make me sound insane."

At the shelf she could smell dust and old paper and, under it, the ghost of sandalwood. The Christies stood in their careful row, lined up by title. Except *Curtain*, spine to the wall.

Poirot's last case. Of course she'd picked that one.

Arenya laid two fingers of her left hand on the spine. Her right hovered an inch behind it, and she felt the heat leave her palm and gather in the book. She slid it out, turned it right side up, and held it against her chest with both arms.

"You're being very dramatic, Aunty," she whispered. "Fine. I'm reading."

The book fell open by itself where something had been pressed flat for a long time. Chapter seventeen, the spine cracked white. A temple receipt, thin and pink, the carbon-copy kind, from the Shiva-Vishnu Temple in Livermore. *Archana, 1 — $11.00.* Dated the second Sunday in March, three days after whatever she had told Appa. On the name line, in a volunteer's ballpoint: *S. Sowri.* Under *Nakshatra*, someone had written Arenya's birth star. Not Susmithaa's.

She had paid for a prayer in Arenya's name.

On the back, in fountain-pen ink gone slightly brown, small and upright:

> *Ari —*
> *The lamp knows who. Itzel knows the rest.*
> *Don't let them count you.*
> *Keep your hands still until you can't. Then don't.*
> *— S.*
> *(Not too much sugar. I know.)*

The last line was in fresher ink, as if she'd come back later and added it.

When Arenya's right hand closed on the receipt, the heat went out of her palm all at once. A heartbeat later it came back stronger. Behind Kino, the lamp's small flame split in two, side by side, like two people standing at a trailhead.

For one second.

Then it was one again.

In the good chair, Nana Itzel had her hand over her mouth.

Arenya read it twice standing up, because the first time the words didn't go in. *Ari.* Nobody had called her that since she was nine. She'd forgotten Aunty ever had.

"She paid for a prayer," she said, small and flat. "In March. In my name. With my star. Eleven dollars. And she kept it in a murder mystery for seven months."

She knelt in front of her mother and put the receipt in her hands, writing side up. She didn't explain. Amma read it three times. On the third, her lips moved on *Ari.* She folded it along its old crease and held it against her sternum with both hands. She didn't cry. That seemed to cost her more than crying would have.

Kino hadn't read it. It wasn't his. But he'd seen the flame split from the corner of his eye. Two flames, side by side. *One of them is her. The other one is a door.* He didn't let himself think about which of them he'd have wanted to be. He took half a step closer to the window, so that anyone coming for this room would have to come through him first.

"*Don't let them count you,*" Arenya said to Nana. "That's the net. That's what I saw in the dream. She knew it in March. Who are *they*? And what's the lamp supposed to know about me?"

Nana unpinned the brooch from her shawl and turned it over in her fingers: a small silver jaguar, worn nearly smooth.

"I know what she told me over tea. Eleven years of Thursdays. No more." She looked at the lamp, not at Arenya. "*They* have no one name. My grandmother called them *los que cuentan.* The ones who count. In her time they came with priests' ledgers. In my mother's time, with government clipboards, asking who in the village had dreams. Now they come in cardigans with rental cars. Different coats. Same hands. They write down people like your aunt. People who carry something. Then the people go quiet, and nobody can say quite when it started."

She set the brooch on her knee.

"Your aunt carried a light. Not a metaphor, mija. She said it came down her mother's side, a long way, and never to the eldest, never to the one you'd expect. She said that in March she felt it start to *look* for somewhere to go. She said it was looking at you." Nana's mouth went thin. "I told her to tell you. She said you'd laugh, and then you'd write an article about it."

"I'm not going to laugh." Arenya said it before Nana had finished, then made herself say the rest slower. "She was right that I'd have written the article. I'd have been wrong, but I'd have written it. I'm sorry I made her think she couldn't tell me."

"The lamp knows who the light belongs to now," Nana went on. "But it split. I have been on this earth seventy-eight years and never seen a house-flame do that. My Lady turned toward a door last night. Your aunt's lamp just showed me two people standing in one." She looked at Arenya steadily. "I don't know what that means. But I know it isn't nothing."

Arenya got the coffee-stained notebook out of her bag and set it on her knee without opening it. Tonight it was the wrong tool. Holding it was still better than holding nothing.

"I'm going to start with the part that sounds worst. Eight weeks ago people started dying. They froze mid-sentence, stared at someone, and dropped. I found nine. Last night I dreamed the whole thing. A net, four men, light pulled out of people like thread." She turned her right hand over on her knee. "And this afternoon a man in a cardigan walked into my mother's house and looked at this hand like a price tag."

She breathed out.

"Nana, you said two people in one door. If that's me and him, then the lamp's telling us something we'll have to work out together. Not tonight. But I want it said out loud, in front of my mother, so nobody finds out later that we hid it." She looked at Kino's back. "And Kino. Whatever's on your arm, you don't have to show me. But I'd like to know if it's cold."

He turned from the window. His face was plain and unguarded, the face he'd wear at a trailhead at four in the morning with nobody there to see. He crossed the carpet and stopped an arm's length from the sofa, where Amma could see him, and pushed the black sleeve to the elbow.

A pale rosette. A thumbprint pressed into brown skin. It looked colder than the room.

"Since the dawn watch," he said. "Since the mountain turned toward a door. I thought it meant she'd turned away from me. I've dreamed it for weeks: she rolls, and I slide off her shoulder. I didn't tell anyone until this morning." He didn't look at Nana. "It hurt. It still does, a little. I'm not going to pretend it doesn't in front of your mother." He lowered the sleeve. "But it's cold when you're near. It's cold when the lamp leans. Not *at* you. *Toward* something. I think we're both pointing at the same door, Arenya. I'd rather stand beside it than argue with it." His mouth moved, tired. "The training says I'm supposed to be the one who walks through. I'm trying to learn the other job."

She didn't say anything clever. She was out of clever. She unbuttoned her cuff and pushed the sleeve past the wrist and held the arm out under the lamp, so that Kino, Nana and her mother could all see. The coin-mark sat on the pulse point, small and round, the grey of an old nickel. Under it, in ballpoint, a plate number and *SORRY*.

"Same cold. Since the minute she died. I've been calling it a pinched nerve." She looked at Amma and kept her arm steady. "That's the lie. That's all of it. Everything else, tonight." And to Kino, quieter: "Beside, not through. I can do beside."

Amma looked at the boy's sleeve, at her daughter's open hand, at the receipt pressed to her chest. Then she got up off the floor the way mothers do, one hand on the sofa arm, all at once and with no help.

"Then nobody goes home tonight." It wasn't an offer. "Itzel-amma, you take Susmithaa's room. The sheets are clean. She changed them Sunday." Her voice caught on *Sunday* and she walked straight through it. "Joaquín, the sofa. There's a blanket in the cupboard. If your dog wants the porch, he can have the porch." She looked at Arenya last. "And you start from the beginning, kanna. The nine people. All of it. I'll make tea."

She went into the kitchen. The freezer hummed. The pozole ticked on its lowest blue ring.

Then, from Arenya's zipped bag on the floor, a sound none of them had heard all afternoon: a short, polite double buzz. Through the canvas, Susmithaa's phone lit up blue. The mesh pocket was thin enough to show the lock screen.

**14 missed calls — Unknown**

**1 new voicemail — 6:12 PM**

* * *

The kettle's tick turned into a thin whistle, and Amma lifted it off the flame before it could scream.

"Ravi's birthday," she said from the kitchen, without turning around. She was answering a question Arenya hadn't meant her to hear. "Day, month, year. She said it was the only date in this family she never forgot." The cups clinked. "Don't tell him. He'll cry again."

Nana held out one hand, palm up, the way you'd ask a child for something it shouldn't be holding. "Give it a minute, *mija*. The message is already inside. It came down with the ringing. Whoever calls like this is listening for the phone to *answer*, not for a voice. So we close the door before we open the letter. No signal. No little blue map."

On the shrine shelf the lamp flame stood perfectly straight. It wasn't leaning toward anyone. It was waiting.

Arenya thumbed on airplane mode and watched the little plane appear like a promise. "Then the door," she said to Kino. "Would you?"

He was already moving. He drew the front curtain, then the hall one, and the room went the color of weak tea. He shot the deadbolt, crouched to look at the gap under the door, and scuffed the mat across the thin grey tongue of fog working at the threshold.

"Check the icon yourself," he said quietly. "Don't take my word. Don't take anybody's."

She checked. She sat on the arm of the couch with the phone cupped in both hands and the notebook open on her knee. If she was going to be afraid, she was going to be afraid with a pen.

Four digits, then the year. She typed them slowly, the way you'd turn a key in a lock you suspected was wired.

The screen hesitated, and opened. In the doorway, Appa made a small sound, as if someone had stepped on his foot. The wallpaper was a photograph of the lamp, taken from below, so the flame looked enormous.

"Listen once," Nana said, pulling a chair up until her knees almost touched the couch. "Then you turn it off. All the way off."

Arenya pressed play.

The voice was pleasant and unhurried. American, with no region in it anywhere, the kind of voice that had been trained flat, like a lawn.

*"Good evening. This message is for the household of Ms. Susmithaa Sowri."* He said the name right. *Sow-ri*, the *r* tapped against the roof of the mouth. Nobody outside the family ever said it that way. *"My name is Daniel Hale, beneficiary services at Meridian Mutual Life. First, I'm so sorry for your loss. I understand Ms. Sowri passed early this morning, at 3:19 a.m."* A sympathetic pause. *"In March, Ms. Sowri took out a policy with us and named a single beneficiary. Policy terms require me to verify that beneficiary in person. It's a short meeting. It won't take more than a few minutes of her time. Your claim reference is M-M-zero-three-one-nine-four-four. Please call me back at your earliest convenience. We'd like to settle this quickly, for everyone's sake. Thank you."*

A soft click.

Amma's hands had gone tight around the kettle handle. "Three nineteen," she said. "The hospital told us *around* three thirty. Nobody told us three nineteen."

Arenya held the power button until the screen went black. Then she wrote, in block capitals, fast: *3:19. DANIEL HALE. MERIDIAN MUTUAL. MM-031944. POLICY IN MARCH. SINGLE BENEFICIARY. "IN PERSON."* She underlined *in person* twice, hard enough to dent the page beneath.

"Three-nineteen is the minute she died," she said. "Abern—" She stopped. Wrong room. Wrong audience. "Somebody I trust told me the same minute. A hospital doesn't have it. An insurance company has it by dinnertime?" It came out clipped, the scared kind of clipped. "Amma. Did she ever mention a policy? Ever?"

Amma shook her head slowly. "Insurance? Your aunty didn't trust the bank with her gold. She kept it in a Horlicks tin." She looked at Appa. Appa looked at the floor.

"Did either of you see that?" Arenya asked. "The lamp. It went down."

"It ducked." Kino hadn't looked away from the wick. It was climbing back now, slowly, as if it wasn't sure it was allowed. "Not on *beneficiary*. On *her*. 'A few minutes of *her* time.' It went small and blue at the root, like somebody opened a freezer door. The corner went dark too, by the shoes." He rubbed his thumb once over his left forearm. "And my arm answered it."

"He said her name right," Arenya said. "The tapped *r*."

"And *March*," Kino said, very quietly. "The receipt in your book. A prayer in your name. The same month she buys a policy for one person who has to show up in the flesh."

He didn't finish. She could do that math faster than he could, and he knew it, and she hated that he was right.

*Nobody calls that number back,* she thought. *Nobody meets anybody in person.*

"The back door," Appa said, too quickly, and went. They heard the bolt slide, then the chain, then a second chain nobody had known the house owned.

Nana took the dead phone out of Arenya's hands and folded it into a fresh towel, like a tamal, for the freezer. "She told me at the Safeway," she said. "By the onions. *Itzel, I bought a door, and I put Ari's name on it.* I thought she meant the temple." She tucked a corner tight. "Maybe she meant both. A door can be locked from either side, *mija*. Whose side is this man on? That's the question."

Kino had gone to the front window. He stood to one side of the curtain, hands loose, and let his eyes go soft. Arenya watched the stillness come over him, animal and patient, the way a deer stops being a deer-shaped thing and becomes part of the hedge. Somewhere out in the fog, she understood, Ix was lending him eyes.

He stood there a long time. When he let the curtain fall back to its finger's-width gap, she knew the dark hadn't given him anything.

"A door can be locked from either side," he said to Nana. "Then we don't open it from ours."

At the far end of the block, an engine turned over.

No headlights. Tires peeled softly off wet asphalt, and a car rolled away dark. By the time Arenya turned her head it was gone around the corner. Out front, something let go of a long breath: the sound an animal makes when it has decided not to bark *yet*.

In the kitchen, the landline rang.

It was the old beige phone on the wall by the fridge, the one Amma kept "for emergencies and Periamma in Chennai." Nobody called it. Amma half rose. Appa froze in the hallway with one hand still on the chain.

It rang again.

"Amma. Don't." Arenya was across the room before the third ring, palm out like she was stopping traffic. Her right hand was hot enough to feel in her teeth. "Let the machine take it. Please. Nobody says a word. Nobody says *hello*. Nobody says our name."

She stopped in the kitchen doorway, close enough to hear and not close enough to touch, a finger to her lips. Appa. Amma. Nana. She flipped to a clean page.

Kino arrived behind her shoulder on the balls of his feet. "Let it ring out," he breathed. "Everyone off the linoleum by the phone."

Fourth ring. The machine clicked, and Appa's voice from 2009 filled the kitchen, younger and cheerful, a TV going behind him. *"You have reached the Sowri residence, please leave—"*

The beep cut him off.

A pause. Then the same flat, friendly voice.

*"Good evening again. Daniel Hale, Meridian Mutual. I see the other handset has gone quiet. That's all right. People grieve differently."* Paper rustled, too deliberately, as if he wanted them to hear him reading. *"I believe this one is for Arenya."*

Amma's hand flew to her mouth.

*"The policy is real, Ms. Sowri. Your aunt was very careful. She paid in cash, in person, at our Colma office, on the nineteenth of March. It covers more than money. Wednesday at noon, the Serramonte Library, second floor, by the periodicals. Bring identification. If noon doesn't suit, I'm happy to come to you."* He let that sit. *"Though I'd hate to bring paperwork to your mother's door during the mourning period. And Ms. Sowri? Keep that hand warm. Goodnight."*

Click. Then the dial tone, droning into the tape until the machine gave up.

Arenya looked down. The page was nearly full, her handwriting steady all the way down. Nearly. In the margin, beside the line where he'd said *bring identification*, were two words she had no memory of writing.

*Eleven, twelve.*

She read them twice. Her handwriting. Her pen. She hadn't heard them—not consciously—and that was what turned her stomach over.

"He said *hand*." Very level, which was how she knew she was scared. "I never told anyone about my hand. Mr. Bell saw it. Mr. Bell *reported* it." She turned the notebook toward Kino. "Somebody was counting under him. I wrote it down without hearing it. Did you?"

"I heard it." His eyes had gone to the freezer. "Behind his breath. Someone else on the line. Slow, like counting steps in the dark. *Eleven. Twelve.* It stopped when he said *hand*."

She followed his look. Along the seam of the freezer door, where a phone and a card lay wrapped in towels, a thin white rime had grown. It hadn't been there a minute ago. Behind her the lamp climbed back to gold now that the line was dead, and leaned hard toward her, the whole flame bent, as if trying to put itself between her and the kitchen.

"Counting what?" Kino asked Nana. "Is it counting us? Is Arenya twelve?"

Arenya made herself look at Amma, at the fingers pressed to her mouth, and made her voice soft. "Amma. He's not coming here. I won't let him. He said *mourning period* on purpose, to make you afraid." She breathed. "But he gave me a time and a place. A public library, with a librarian and cameras. That's a tell. Men who want a body don't book the periodicals."

"It's a room on their terms," Kino said. "With a paper trail that says you came willingly. Nobody picks the ground but the one who's already stood on it." He flexed the cold forearm once. "If you go, you don't go as the one he booked. Not alone. Not Wednesday noon on his say-so. I'll walk Serramonte tonight and see it before he does. And Ix goes where I can't."

"Don't go alone." Sharper than she meant. "Not tonight. If they're counting us, a scout walking their library in the dark is just a bigger number." She shut the notebook on the pen.

And Colma, she thought. A cash counter. Some clerk who'd seen a woman's face on the nineteenth of March. That, at least, she knew how to do. Find the desk. Find the person behind it.

Nana got up, crossed to the freezer, and laid her flat palm on the white seam, the way you'd test a child's forehead. The rime softened under her hand. Then it stopped softening.

"Not your age, *mija*," she said at last. "Your number." She wiped her palm on her skirt. "Your aunty told me once, years ago, when we were both drunk on that terrible Christmas wine. They count the ones who carry. While they are still hunting, they don't say the number out loud. When they say it out loud, they are done looking. They have started collecting." Her eyes found the margin of the notebook.

"Eleven was somebody. Twelve is you."

No one said anything. The lamp leaned, gold and steady.

Then Nana pointed a finger at Kino, like at a nephew caught climbing the fence. "And no. You don't walk Serramonte tonight. Your shadow can go. Tell him to look and come home. You sleep on this floor where the flame can see you." She clapped her hands once. "Amma, the good blankets. Not the polyester."

Amma moved at last, because now there was something to do with her hands. Appa locked the back door a third time. Nobody told him he already had. Out in the fog, Ix slipped Kino's attention like a leash and was gone toward Serramonte, low and fast.

Arenya went back to the couch and sat by the lamp, and let it lean on her. It was the first thing in two days that had felt like a hand on her shoulder.

Near midnight the house was quiet. Five people, one lamp, a freezer humming a little too cold. On the coffee table, in the margin of a notebook, someone else's count sat in Arenya's handwriting, waiting for its next number.

* * *

At 2:47 in the morning the house had the particular silence of people pretending to sleep.

Amma's breathing from the armchair was too even. Appa's door was shut, but a line of light lay under it. Nana snored in Susmithaa's room with her shoes still on. The lamp burned low between the couch and the floor, where Kino lay on two folded blankets.

Then the flame straightened, all at once, like a dog lifting its head.

Arenya was sitting up before she knew why. Something cold came through the back screen door without opening it. Down the hall the kitchen tiles went grey with frost, tile by tile, in a line toward the front room. Then the line stopped, and the frost had a shape: low and heavy and longer than any dog, a thing her eyes kept wanting to call a shadow. It shook itself like an animal coming out of water and lay down across Kino's shins, trembling, and pushed its muzzle into his palm.

Kino closed his hand around it the slow way, the way you hold a hurt animal, and breathed with it. Four in, hold, four out. Frost melted off it and soaked into his jeans. He didn't seem to notice. His eyes had gone somewhere else, and she watched his face take the pictures one at a time.

"Don't pet him yet," she whispered, flat and low, so Amma's fake breathing could stay fake. "Tell me in order. What's cold, what's warm, what's new."

The notebook was under her pillow. She uncapped the pen with her teeth.

"Serramonte," Kino said, barely a sound. "Second floor. The periodical racks are strung. Frost thread, rack to rack, ankle height, like a snare for rabbits. There's a ring of chairs by the window. Too many to count. Every one is cold except one." He swallowed. "That one's warm. Like somebody just stood up."

She wrote it left-handed, ugly: *Serramonte, 2nd fl. Strung racks. Ring of chairs. One warm.*

"And Ix smelled something he's never smelled before. Wet stone. Ink. Something old that's still keeping its accounts."

"That's not Hale," she said. "Hale smells like a hotel lobby. I'd bet money. Somebody's keeping *books*."

The lamp flame bent hard toward Ix. Then, for the first time all night, it bent *away*—away from Ix, away from Kino—and leaned toward her open right hand.

Kino saw it. She watched him see it. It went into his face like a splinter under a nail, and he let it hurt for exactly as long as it took to breathe once. Then she watched him put it somewhere and shut the lid.

"Later," he said, to no one.

Her palm was tingling. Not burning. A fingertip, going slow, as if someone were writing on her to see whether she'd flinch.

*Brooke told me not to flinch once,* she thought. *Funny what sticks.*

She didn't close the hand. She turned it up to the lamp and watched it the way you watch a phone that's about to ring.

A stroke, straight down. A hook, a curve, a flat base. Another figure beside it. Then a pause, long enough to breathe in. And one last stroke dragged under the pair from heel to fingers, ruled and straight, like the line under a sum.

*12.*

"Twelve," she said. Clipped and small. "Underlined." The cold wasn't on her skin. It was in the lines of her palm, as if someone had cut them a little deeper. "It's not writing on me, Kino. It's *filing* me."

The lamp stood up tall, white at the tip. The warmth in her hand rose to meet the cold. It didn't wipe the number out. It pushed, and the two of them sat under her skin like ice in tea that hadn't decided yet.

In the kitchen, the freezer ticked. Ticked again. A thin rime crept out along the seal, an inch, as if whatever was inside wanted to be closer.

*Arenya. Love. Don't flinch, you're doin' ever so well.* Abernathy was close and low, the cockney pitched too high and too cheerful. *Twelve, guv. Grey ink, proper tidy, ruled underneath. And it's not from the freezer, and not from Serramonte. It's comin' up from* under. *Under the floor, under the bulk. Somewhere wet and stony. Never been there, me. Don't fancy it.*

*Keep the hand open. Don't clench. A nice neat closed fist is just what they'd want for the ledger.*

Then the patter went thin, and something older showed through for half a word. *And there's somethin' else comin' up under the twelve. Faint. Like a watermark in paper. A little circle with rays on it. A sun, like a kiddie draws. Gone quick.*

*I know that shape, love. Don't ask me from where. It's made my coat go cold. Write it down if you must. Just don't show the boy yet.*

*Also: somebody's opened your dad's door a crack. Not a spirit. A frightened man in socks. Mention it gently.*

She didn't turn her head. Kino's hand slid across the carpet and stopped an inch short of hers, palm open. She slid hers the rest of the way, until the cold from his mark and the warm from her palm had to argue it out in the gap. Then he closed the inch, two fingers over hers, brief, and let go.

"Thank you," he breathed. "For not taking it from me. It stung. Still does."

"I think it's pointing at what's been used," she said. "Not at who it likes."

He put his forehead against Ix's brow and asked the only way he knew, no words, just the memory of the frosted tiles held out like a plate. *¿Te siguieron?*

Nothing had walked behind Ix. Then Kino's mouth went tight, because there was a second picture, the one Ix was ashamed of. The edge of Colma on the way home: low wrought-iron gates in fog, a stone yard full of blank headstones waiting to be cut. As Ix ran past, something there had *noticed* him. It hadn't chased. It hadn't even turned. It had made a small tidy mark, the way a toll booth logs a plate, and let him go on.

"Not followed," Kino said. "*Logged.* Whoever keeps the books already knows our dog's route home."

Down the hall, the door opened the rest of the way.

"Appa," Arenya said, without turning around. "If you're going to stand there, come sit. There's room on the couch."

He came in barefoot, in the old Cal sweatshirt he slept in, reading glasses pushed up into his hair. He was holding a long white envelope against his chest with both hands, the way you'd hold a hymnbook. He looked at the lamp first, and his jaw worked. Then at her open palm. He sat down on the far end of the couch very carefully, as if it might break.

"Meridian Mutual," he said. It wasn't a question. "I heard the phone."

Across the flap, in Susmithaa's round handwriting: ***Ravi — only if they call.***

"She made me witness something in March. At a notary in Colma, by the flower shop. I signed where she pointed. She wouldn't let me read it. I told your Amma it was a will." His thumb rested on the seal and didn't break it. "Kanna, I lied to everyone in this house for seven months, and I am very bad at it." He looked up. "They called. So. Do I open it, or do you?"

From the armchair, Amma's breathing stopped being even.

"Appa." Arenya's voice had gone soft, and she hated how young it sounded. "You're not bad at lying. You're bad at lying to *us*. That's different." She looked at the seal. *Only if they call.* "They called. Twice. Amma, you're awake. Come sit. If this is a letter, it's for all of us."

Kino had come off the floor without a sound. He stood behind the couch now, between the hall and the kitchen, not guarding Appa so much as walling him in. "Mr. Sowri," he said, in the voice he used on old men on cold trails. "You did right to bring it out where the lamp can see. Open it slow. If the flame ducks, stop."

"I'll open it," Arenya said. "Not because it's mine. Because you've carried it seven months and your hands are shaking. Give me the weight."

She slid a thumb under the flap slowly, giving everyone in the room one more second to say stop.

Nobody did. The flap gave with a small dry sound, like a breath let out.

The lamp didn't duck. It leaned in a little, the way someone leans to read over your shoulder. Amma didn't take the couch. She sat on the carpet at Appa's feet with her back against his shins and one hand around his ankle, and said nothing at all, which was worse than anything she could have said.

There were three things inside.

The first was a letter on heavy cream stock, folded in thirds, with blue-and-silver letterhead: *MERIDIAN MUTUAL LIFE · Beneficiary Services.* Dated March 9. *Welcome to the Meridian family. We are pleased to confirm that your policy is in force.* Below that, a little grid in typewriter font:

> INSURED: SOWRI, SUSMITHAA ............ **11**
> BENEFICIARY: SOWRI, ARENYA (NIECE) .... **12**

No premium. No face value. One line at the bottom: *A representative will contact the beneficiary in person to verify.* And across the whole page, in Aunty's blue ballpoint, pressed so hard the paper had torn through at the O:

**NO.**

The second was two pages of legal paper with a raised notary seal she could feel with her thumb. *Declaration of Susmithaa Sowri.* Plain, almost dull. She had purchased no policy from Meridian Mutual Life or any affiliate. She named no beneficiary. Her niece was party to no contract made in her name. Signed by her, witnessed by *RAVI SOWRI* in Appa's careful engineer's capitals, stamped *Colma, California.*

Appa looked at his own signature as if someone else had forged it. "She told me not to read it," he whispered.

Arenya laid the two documents side by side on the coffee table, the way she'd lay out sources. Precision was the only thing she had that didn't shake.

"Okay. March ninth. *Welcome to the family.* That's ten days before Hale says she walked into Colma with cash. So his story's backwards. Nobody bought anything. They sent her a receipt for something she never ordered." Her finger slid to the grid. "And they numbered us. Eleven. Twelve. In typewriter font, like a utility bill." She touched the torn O, very lightly, as if it might still be hot. "She went through the paper."

"Printed in March," Kino said quietly. "Before anyone died. They had the numbers written down long before they said them out loud. And she tore the paper saying no. That's not a woman who got sold something, Mr. Sowri. That's a woman who got *counted*, and said no to its face."

"And then she went to a notary," Arenya said, "and swore under seal she never bought it. With you as her witness, Appa, because you're the one person on earth who'd sign where she pointed and not read." Her voice broke for exactly one word. "That's not you being bad at lying. That's her trusting you to be good at not knowing."

She wrote it, small: *3/9 letter, unsolicited. 3/19 Colma notary: a declaration, not a purchase. She didn't buy a door. She was trying to shut one.*

The third paper was folded small, torn from the cheap spiral pad that had lived by Aunty's kettle for as long as there'd been a kettle. The ragged edge was still on it. Her round handwriting showed backward through the page, crowding to the margins, and at the bottom, where a name should have been, the ink went heavy. Even folded, even reversed, Arenya could make out the shape.

A little circle. A crown of quick rays.

A sun. It was how Aunty had signed every birthday card Arenya had ever gotten from her, since before she could read. They were in a drawer in her apartment right now, every one. She had never once asked why a sun.

*Before you read, love,* Abernathy said, quick and quiet. *Keep your face still. That sun's not ink. It's lit, the color of the lamp, soakin' through the folds. Same mark that came up under the twelve. Your aunt didn't just sign with it. She* sent *it.*

*I don't think it's a trap. The cold in your hand aches when you touch it, but your light doesn't flinch. It leans in.*

*And I'll tell you somethin' true, since I said I'd never lie to you about danger. Lookin' at it, I smelled hot brass and lamp-smoke, and I heard a voice say,* Mark it so it can be found again. *Low. Patient. Mine, and not mine. I don't know who it was talkin' to. Don't ask me yet, guv. I've got a headache I haven't got a head for.*

*Read it. Slow. I'm here.*

She put her numbered hand flat on the back of Amma's, so they made a chain: her, Amma, Appa. "Kino. Come where you can see. She says Itzel knows the rest. I think you get to hear this too."

He came around the couch and knelt on the carpet beside Amma, Ix settling across his feet. He kept his eyes half-lidded on the freezer seam, reflected in the dark window. "It's hers," he said. "I'll listen to the room while you read."

She unfolded the page and read it aloud from the top, in Aunty's voice as best she could: the brisk one with a little lift at the end of every sentence, the way Aunty used to read her the newspaper when she was seven and pretending she already could.

*Ari kanna,*

*If Ravi has given you this, then they've called and I'm not there to pick up. I'm sorry. I wanted to be the one who picked up.*

*In March they sent me the letter you're holding. I never bought anything. That is how they say* we have found you. *Men who count don't knock. They send paperwork.*

*Listen to me.* **Writing is not keeping.** *They wrote me down in March, and it took them seven months to come with their net, and when they came I had already decided what they could have, which was nothing important. If your palm has been written on, don't scrub it and don't hide it. Just don't go and* sign. *Don't verify. Don't say your full name to them in a room they chose. A thing gets kept when you agree to it.*

*The paper Ravi signed is my no. Keep it near the lamp. Itzel knows what a no is worth to the one under Colma. Make her tell you all of it this time; she leaves out the parts that frighten her.*

*If Itzel's boy is there, the cold one, then the lamp has already decided something, and you can stop arguing with it. Feed him. He doesn't eat when he's worried.*

*Tell your Amma I'm sorry about the will. Tell your Appa he lied very well for once and I'm proud of him.*

*Not too much ghee in the lamp. It smokes.*

And then the sun.

Appa broke first. It was quiet. His shoulders went, and he put his big hand over his mouth and kept it there. Amma turned her hand over under Arenya's and gripped hard, numbered palm and all, and said to the carpet in Tamil, *Stubborn. Stubborn, stubborn woman,* in the voice people use for *I love you* when they can't manage the words.

In Arenya's palm, the warmth stopped pushing against the cold twelve. It *settled*, the way someone sits down next to you on a bench and makes it clear she isn't getting up. The number was still there. It just wasn't alone with her anymore. The lamp burned lower and steadier, and its flame tipped once toward Kino, like a nod.

A floorboard creaked in the hall.

Nana Itzel stood at the edge of the lamplight with her shoes still on and her braid undone, one hand on the wall. Nobody knew how long she'd been there. Her eyes were wet and very hard.

"*Ay, Susmita.*" Softly. "Telling on me from the grave." She looked at the notary seal, at Arenya's palm, at her grandson. "Bueno. The parts that frighten me." She pulled Aunty's reading chair toward the lamp and sat and folded her hands. "Somebody put the kettle on. It isn't a short story, and I'm not telling it twice."

Arenya folded the kettle-pad page along its soft creases and tucked it into the back of her notebook, against the spine, where she kept the things she couldn't afford to lose. She filled the kettle herself, because Aunty had said *feed him* and because her hands needed a job. She put the first mug into Kino's hands before he could refuse.

"Drink that. Aunty's orders."

He drank. Hot enough to hurt; he looked glad of it. Then he said something low to his grandmother in Spanish, and Arenya caught *todo* and *miedo* and understood the rest from his face.

Nana took the second mug and held it in her lap like a candle.

"In nineteen-twelve," she said, "San Francisco decided the dead were bad for property values. So they dug them up. A hundred and fifty thousand, maybe more, and carted them south over the hill to Colma, on wagons and on the streetcar at night. My grandmother's people cut stone down there. They said you can't move that many dead without someone *counting*. Every box written down: who, from where, to which plot. Clerks did it for years, in ledgers, by lamplight, in a shed by the stone yard that always smelled of wet granite and ink." She looked at Kino. "Ix smelled the shed."

"The clerks died. The counting didn't stop. The stonecutters called it *el Contador*. Not a devil. Not a saint. A clerk. It writes down who is dead in that valley, and who is going to be. Very careful. Very fair. In a hundred years it has never once made a mistake in its sums. Nobody prays to it. You just don't lie on paper near Colma." She took a breath. "And somebody has learned to *read its books*. They wear grey, and they say *beneficiary*. Whatever it writes, they come and collect."

Her hands tightened on the mug.

"Here is the part that frightens me. In March, Susmita came to my kitchen with that welcome letter and she was *shaking*. I had never seen her shake. We went down together. She did the notary, all very correct, and then after closing we walked to the stone yard, and she put a copy of her *no* under the shed door, with salt and a candle, the old way. I stood witness." Her mouth worked. "Something answered. Not a voice. The candle went out, and afterward the salt on top of the paper was arranged into a line beneath it. Like a sum."

"*A no buys time, not freedom.* My grandmother used to say that. I didn't say it to Susmita, because she had already decided. It bought her seven months. I think she spent every day of them getting ready to give you the light instead of giving it to them."

Her eyes went to Arenya's open palm, then to her grandson's cold forearm, and stayed.

"And there was one more thing in the salt. A second mark, beside the line. Two small strokes, close together, side by side. I told myself it was the wind. Then tonight the lamp split in two." She looked at both of them. "I think the next *no* takes two people to say it. Don't ask me how. That's the end of what I know. I swear it on her lamp."

Arenya wrote it down, because a story like that was a list of facts, and facts were what she had. *1912. 150,000 moved. Ledgers. Shed: wet granite + ink. El Contador. Doesn't err. Somebody reads its books. March: salt, candle, her NO under the shed door. Answered with a line. Second mark: two strokes, side by side.*

"A clerk," she said. "Not a monster. A very fair clerk." The pen stopped. "That's worse, you know. Monsters you can fight. Clerks you have to *dispute*."

She looked at the torn **NO**.

"So twelve isn't a threat. It's a line item. And the men in grey aren't deciding anything. They're just the ones who learned to read the ledger. Which means what Aunty wrote wasn't magic. It was a formal objection. And it got entered. The salt proves it." She turned. "Nana. The two strokes. Did they look like a tally? Two ones? Or two people standing next to each other?"

Then, more gently, eyes still on his grandmother: "And whatever the next no takes, Kino, nobody decides that tonight. I'm not signing you up for anything because a pile of salt looked like a pair." She breathed. "But I'm not saying it alone, either. If it comes to that, I'd want it to be you."

She let it sit. She didn't take it back.

Kino set the mug down on the carpet. For a moment he said nothing at all, and she understood, a beat too late, that she'd said something he had waited a very long time to hear. He'd probably imagined it coming from a mountain.

"Okay," he said. It was rough, and he left it rough. "Not tonight, like you said. But I heard you, and I'm not going to pretend I didn't." "Not ones. My grandmother had a ledger page from the shed, in the old clerks' hand. Standing next to each other."

She handed the pen back.

"Does it know your no is coming? It doesn't. It isn't God. It *left room*. A clerk leaves a space on the line when he's waiting for a signature. That's all those two strokes are. A space." That's why she never answered the phone."

Arenya copied the two strokes into her notebook exactly, and boxed them. *Dos testigos. Contested. Stays open until two stand.* Under it: *They can't unwrite a no. They can only make you say your name to stop the phone ringing.*

Appa nodded, grateful for a job. He cleared his throat, eyes still red, and said, a little too loudly, practicing being brave: "Then it's settled. Nobody verifies anything. Everybody eats breakfast."

Amma didn't laugh, but her shoulders came down a quarter-inch.

In Arenya's palm, beside the cold twelve, there was a faint new pressure, like the place where a second pen would rest. She let it be.

"Breakfast, then Colma. In daylight, with a plan," she said. "Nobody signs. And the shed, if it's still there, is *our* room to choose. Not theirs."

"I walk the stone yard first," Kino said. "With Ix, in daylight, and you at the gate. A witness doesn't have to be the first one through the door. She has to be the one still standing at the end."

She leaned her head back against the couch. The window had gone slate. Not dawn yet. The idea of it.

*Abernathy,* she said, where only one person could hear. *The voice you heard. "Mark it so it can be found again." I'm not going to push. But I'd like to know whose it was before somebody else tells me. And thank you for warning me about the sun. It helped to know first.*

She closed her eyes for one minute, palm open, her shoulder against Kino's, between her parents. It was the first time in two days she'd let herself.

The answer came after a long moment.

*I went lookin', love. Honest as I can be. Nothin'. Not fog—fog I'd know, fog's got somethin' behind it. It's a clean wall. Smooth as a page nobody wrote on. The smell's gone. The voice is gone. All I've got is that I* heard *it tonight, and now I can't.*

*Whose it was, I don't know. I won't make one up for you.*

A pause, and the cockney went thin as paper.

*Doesn't feel like forgettin', though. Feels like somebody tidied up after me. I don't care for that.*

The minute passed, and then a few more that nobody counted.

Appa got down on his knees behind the side table and pulled the landline cord out of the wall, gently, like taking a splinter out of a child. Amma fetched the good blanket and put it over both of them on the couch without asking whose knees went where. Nana stayed in Susmithaa's reading chair with her shoes on and didn't sleep, watching the lamp the way her grandmother had watched the stone yard. Kino kept the door. Ix's breathing slowed against his feet.

Outside, the slate went to pewter over the hill. Somewhere south, in a valley with more dead than living, a shed that smelled of wet granite and ink waited with a line left open, and two small strokes in the margin.

The frost on the freezer seal held. So did the lamp.

* * *

Colma, by daylight, was a town of fifteen hundred living people and a million and a half dead, and it had arranged itself accordingly. Cemetery gates alternated with florists. Every block had a place to buy a bucket of roses and a place to put them down.

Vengo a ver, no a tomar.*

With permission. I've come to see, not to take.

"Nana," she said, low. "If the line stops at the gate for me the way it did for Ix, tell me. I don't want to be the reason it opens."

"It will stop for you, mija. It stops for everyone at the gate. The gate is where they *ask*." Nana's thumb moved once on her elbow. "If it opens, I'll pull you back by the hair. I've done it before. Ask your tía."

Apron man: practiced nothing.* When the pen lifted, she saw that the faint printed rules of her cheap notebook had drifted. *Brass, love,* Abernathy said in her ear. *Just now. Half a second, hot and gone, same as last night. Sayin' it out loud like you asked, so you've got it on the record.* A pause. *And the gate's ruled a line by Ix's feet. Blank. Clerk dipped the pen, looked at him, set it down.* Not yet. *Puzzled, not hostile.*

*I'll take puzzled,* she told him. *Puzzled is a clerk who can't find your line, which is how I'd like to stay.*

*Puzzled's good. Puzzled's what we want. But a clerk that's puzzled by Ix is a clerk that'll be less puzzled by you. So keep your name behind your teeth, Ladyship.*

She kept it there. It was getting crowded back there.

Every blank in every row carried a faint pencil-grey line at exactly the same height, level from stone to stone, all the way back to the shed. The yard was ruled like a page. Kino walked it on the gravel, never where the line's shadow would cross his path, hands in his hoodie pocket. Halfway down he crouched to sight along it toward the shed door, the way you'd sight a trail, and stayed there a while. Arenya understood he was reading it.

"Twelve rows," he called back when he stood. Low and flat, a report from a ridge. "The shed's the heading. Where the thirteenth would be. There's a file of narrow stones along the left fence, one at the head of each row. That's the margin." He swallowed. "Row eleven has a salt stroke in the margin stone. Old. Chiseled. The line dips through it, like somebody argued there. That's Susmithaa. That's March."

She wrote it down. Her hand was steady, which surprised her.

He went a little farther and stopped where Ix stopped him, a few paces short of the last row. He didn't argue with the jaguar.

"Row twelve's clean. The margin stone's polished. The only finished face in the yard. One pencil stroke on it. Low. One, not two." He didn't say whose. "It's waiting for a second."

"Kino." She kept her voice level, the voice you'd use on someone on a ledge. "Row twelve is one stroke, and my palm just put its finger on the empty spot for the second. Nana made a sound that, in someone less formidable, would have been a laugh.

He went. Gravel, then weeds, then the cracked concrete pad the shed stood on. Behind him the polishing wheel cut off mid-whine, for no reason the shop would ever give, and the Tagalog radio went with it. * * *

Kino leaned toward the tin wall without touching it, so close his breath would have fogged it if it had been glass.

He heard a pen. A real one. Dip and scratch, unhurried, with the patience of something that had been doing this since 1912. And under it, the voice from Hale's voicemail, the one that had counted beneath the man's breathing. Dry as old paper. Not speaking to anyone. Reading back.

*"Once, entered. Once, contested. Sostenido por una."* A pause. *"Doce. Testigo: una. Falta una."*

Twelve. Witness: one. One missing.

Then the pen stopped.

The attention came through the tin like cold through a window and settled on the ache in his arm. It asked him, without sound, in the pause of a long counter, with the pen held over the line:

*¿Y tú?*

And you?

Twenty-six years of fasting and night-walks and dreaming on the mountain, waiting for something old to wake and turn and ask for *him*. And here it was, through a tin wall in Colma, in a clerk's patient silence. The want came up in his chest so fast and so clean that it shamed him. He could say *sí*. It would be the easiest word of his life.

He didn't nod. He didn't shake his head. Either one was an answer, and you don't lie near Colma.

So he gave the only true thing he had. Slowly, so the attention could follow it, he turned his head away from the shed and looked back down twelve rows of granite to the gate. To Arenya, with her closed hand and Nana's fist on her elbow.

He held the look there. *Not mine to give. Ask her. Not today.*

* * *

From forty yards off, Arenya saw his face change.

Her palm went warm in her fist. Under the cold of the 12, a sun-heat gathered in the place beside it, where the second stroke would go. Nana's grip on her elbow tightened until she felt it in her back teeth.

"Kino!" Sharper than she meant. "Don't answer it. Not a word, not a nod. Not *yes*, and not *no* either. Tell it you came to see, not to take. Then walk back to me."

He was already moving. He backed off the concrete pad without turning his back on the door, one step, two, three, into the weeds, the way you leave a lion's ground. Ix moved backward with him, shoulder to knee. Only when his heels hit gravel did he turn and walk the long way round by the fence. He didn't look at the clean stone in row twelve as he passed it. He didn't trust himself to.

She made herself breathe out. Quieter, though he was too far to hear: "It's asking you because you're the one standing closest. That isn't the same as you being the one it wants. We said not tonight. Say it with your feet."

He said it with his feet.

At the last row he lifted two fingers—*coming back, don't move*—and when his voice finally reached her, it was rough as the gravel.

"It's reading. *Doce, testigo una, falta una.* It asked me." A swallow. "I didn't answer. Get her off the gate, Nana. We talk in the car."

Then she felt it go. All three of them did. Something followed his look down twelve rows, along the pencil line, past the blanks and the careful gravel, to the gate post and the girl standing at it with her hand shut. It rested on her for the length of a held pen. It wasn't hostile. It felt like being *read*, a line at a time, by someone with all the time in the world.

Inside the shed the pen dipped once and made a small sound, too short to be a word.

A tick.

*Referred.*

The polishing wheel started up mid-whine, as if it had never stopped. The radio laughed at something.

Kino came up the last row with his face set and grey. Tito Boy Esposo stood aside to let him through the gate and didn't ask. He only said to Nana, with his eyes on the back of the lot, "Next time, Mama, call first. It's been busy this week." A pause long enough to be an opinion. "Lot of visitors."

In the Corolla, Arenya finally opened her hand.

The 12 was there, cold. The rule was under it. Beside the rule, where a second stroke would go, there was no stroke. There was a dot of pencil-grey, small and exact. The kind of mark a clerk leaves beside a line to say *come back to this*.

*Referred,* she thought. *Like a bad knee.*

Kino sat with his hood up and both hands under his thighs and said nothing, and Ix was a weight in the car she could feel more than see. She wanted to tell him it was fine. It wasn't. She wanted to tell him she'd seen his face and knew what he'd given up in that second, and she couldn't find a sentence that wouldn't make it smaller.

Nana pulled out onto Mission Road. Across the street, outside the florist with the bucket roses, a beige Camry was parked at the curb. Empty. The plate began **7K**. On the dash lay a parking-validation slip, and Arenya could read the logo from where she sat: **Colma Notary & Mail**, two doors down.

*Oi,* said Abernathy, very quietly, with none of the accent at all. *Ladyship. That's a lot of visitors.*

"Nana," Arenya said. "Slow down."

Nana slowed. She looked at her grandson's grey face in the mirror, and at the closed hand in Arenya's lap.

Then she drove on.

* * *

The Camry was there again on Wednesday morning, in the same spot across Mission Road, as if it had never left.

Nana Itzel would not drive past it. She went the long way: down El Camino, along the Italian cemetery's wall of cypress, then a U-turn at the gas station on Serramonte Boulevard that made the Corolla's suspension groan like a man getting out of a low chair.

"If he's watching the road," she said, "he watches an old lady who is lost. Very believable."

She turned the radio to a mariachi station and left it low, which in Nana's language meant *talk, I'm not listening.*

That gave them the back seat and about twelve minutes. Kino had asked for this talk. The pencil dot in Arenya's palm itched the way a scab does. The glovebox clock read 11:09. Hale's noon meeting at the Serramonte Library was three-quarters of a mile up the road they'd just used to turn around.

"Say it fast, Kino," Arenya said, "before I talk myself out of listening. The voice at the shed. Was it Hale?"

Kino sat with his eyes on the mirror, on the strip of Nana's face, and his hands flat on his knees as if he'd had to tell them to stay there. The weight of Ix leaned against his shin.

**TO THE BENEFICIARY**

Once.

"Nana, go. Not past the Camry. Whichever way you like, as long as it's slow and boring."

"Slow and boring," said Nana, and had the engine going before his door shut. "My specialty."

Arenya's hand wouldn't stop shaking, so she put it flat on her knee until it did.

"It's Bell," she said. "Grey sweater, cold hands. He filed a request for Aunty's notary entry from March nineteenth. *Verification of beneficiary.* And there's an envelope in a box in there with my name on it. My whole name, typed. It wasn't on the glass. It was on the photo." She swallowed. "So it's only my name if somebody looks at it through a lens. I don't know what that means yet."

She wrote it down, because writing was something that ended. *Rodel Panganiban. Box 112. Meridian Mutual Services, cash, March 19. Collected Fridays 7:40. Bell: PO box, Daly City.*

She looked up at the mirror, at Nana's eyes. "Hale's noon at the library is in twenty minutes. I'm not going. But Friday at 7:40, somebody comes to that box, and I want to know who. And I want to know it from a car, not a counter. Take us home."

Nana signaled—the most law-abiding signal ever made in San Mateo County—and pulled out away from the Camry, toward the cemeteries.

In the side mirror, the Camry wasn't empty anymore.

Mr. Bell sat behind the wheel in his grey cardigan with a small notebook open against the steering wheel, writing without looking down. He didn't turn his head as they passed. He didn't have to. Then he closed the notebook, got out, and crossed the street with the unhurried walk of a man with an appointment. At the florist's buckets he stopped long enough to choose a single white chrysanthemum, and paid in coins.

Then he went two doors down and opened the door of Colma Notary & Mail.

The bell over it was a real bell, brass, on a curl of spring. Arenya watched it move.

She didn't hear it ring.

"He went in," Nana said to the windshield. Her knuckles were pale on the wheel. "Rodel is a stubborn man. Stubborn men are good at paper." A long breath. "*Mija.* Whatever is on your phone, don't show me in the car. Show me in my kitchen, with the door shut and the pozole on."

"Pozole. Door shut. Yes, Nana." It came out smaller than she'd have liked. She pressed her closed hand between her knees. "He bought a flower for Rodel, didn't he? That's what you do before you ask a stubborn man for something."

Nobody answered.

Mission Road unrolled ahead of them, cemetery after cemetery, green lawns and white stones in their patient rows. She didn't open the notebook again. She counted cemeteries instead—one, two, three—and caught herself at it, and kept counting anyway.

"Kino." She kept her eyes on the lawns. "When we get to the kitchen, I want you to read what's on my phone first. Not me. Read it twice, like you said. If it says my name, I want one person who isn't scared of it to tell me what it says after."

Her closed hand was cold in her lap. Under her fingers, faint as a held breath, the pencil dot beside the rule was still there.

Waiting.

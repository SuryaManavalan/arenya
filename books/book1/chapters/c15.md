# Chapter Fifteen: The Cheese Dream

Two days of soft, buffering nonsense.

Then the buffering stopped.

She was standing in her parents' backyard in Daly City, and the backyard had too many directions. She could see the fence from the front and from behind at the same time. She could see the curry-leaf tree from inside its own growing. Fog poured along the ground, and it wasn't weather. It moved like a tide with an opinion.

At the bottom of the garden, where Susmithaa Aunty's cottage should have been, stood her brass lamp—the one from her window—grown to the size of a temple. Every wick was burning.

Her aunt stood barefoot at the center of it.

She was not her aunt. Or she was, the way a sunrise is a light bulb. A woman made of lamps. A thousand small flames in the shape of a woman, tiny silver charms ringing in her braids.

Far beneath her, the way you see the bottom of a pool, a small woman in a cardigan stood in the real garden, perfectly still, staring at the street.

Four men stood around them.

Arenya couldn't hold on to their faces. They slid off her attention like rain off glass, each man a smudge of grey static. Between them a net was drawing tight: humming filaments that pulled like magnets, dragging flame after flame toward the center and snuffing it.

One of the men wore a light grey jacket.

She had seen that jacket before.

It had been on her wall.

*This is a dream,* she told herself. *The cheese-before-bed dream. You took the wall down, and your brain is putting it back up in Amma's garden.*

It didn't help. Her feet were cold. You don't feel cold feet in dreams.

Susmithaa turned her head through an angle that wasn't an angle and looked straight at her.

"Kanna." It was the most frightened Arenya had ever heard her. "You came. Of course you came. *Listen to me.*"

"Aunty." It came out six years old, lost in the Serramonte mall. She swallowed. "Okay. I'm listening. Tell me what to do."

She didn't wait for the answer. She reached, the way you'd reach for someone slipping on a wet step, toward the nearest flame in her aunt's burning hand. And as she reached, she made herself look at the man in grey. Not his face. Her eyes wouldn't stay on it. The jacket. The collar. The seam at the shoulder.

*Details. You can always hold on to details.*

"I know you," she said, and her voice shook. "I've got you on a wall."

The flame came into her hand and did not burn. It felt like Aunty's palm on her forehead when she'd had a fever at eight: dry and warm, camphor and ghee. A silver charm rang against her knuckle, a bell no bigger than a grain of rice.

"Good girl," Susmithaa breathed. "Hold that. Don't let go of that."

Then the man in grey turned toward her.

Not his head. The static turned, the whole smear of him shifting like a satellite dish finding a signal. One filament peeled away from the lamp-woman and drifted across the fog toward Arenya.

It didn't hurry.

It was curious.

"—bleedin' *wire*, bleedin' *magnets*, who brings *magnets* to a—"

Someone else was there. A lanky figure in a shabby long coat, a light swinging on a chain like a lantern, swearing steadily in something that might have been cockney. One moment he was behind Susmithaa. The next he was between Arenya and the man in grey, slope-shouldered and wrong.

"*Oi.* Your Ladyship. Don't you *talk* to 'im." The patter was too fast and too thin, a man whistling past a graveyard. "You've never *met* 'im. You're a nice girl 'avin' a cheese dream, ain't that right, lads?" He dropped his voice for her alone. "They count what they can see, love. Don't let 'em count *you*."

He brought the lantern down overhand on the nearest filament. With his other hand—a hand that never quite had the right number of fingers—he cupped the air around her flame, like a man sheltering a match.

"Hold it. Don't let go of it, whatever she says." For half a word the cockney slipped, and the voice under it was low and old and very sure. *"—keep it lit, child—"*

He caught himself. The grin came back, wobbling.

The lantern struck the wire with the sound of a tuning fork. For an instant the filament glowed cherry-red, and three wicks in the knot leaned toward freedom.

Then the net did what magnets do. It took the heat and pulled it in. The glow ran down the wire like water down a drain, and the three wicks went out together. *Pff. Pff. Pff.* The lantern guttered to an ember on its chain. Two of the grey smudges turned toward the lanky man.

There was no anger in it.

Only inventory.

Arenya closed her fist around the flame, hard, the way you hold your phone in a crowd. Her other hand was already writing in the air, because it didn't know it wasn't holding her notebook. *Collar. Seam. Hangs long on the left. Something heavy in the left pocket.*

"Left pocket," she said out loud. "Left pocket. Left—"

The words were already going strange in her mouth, the way a word does when you say it forty times. The collar went first. Then the seam. She was holding a sentence with nothing left inside it.

At the center of the fire, Susmithaa let a whole side of herself go dark in one long exhale, so that she could turn toward them.

"I pick the kid," she said. "Always the kid, Abernathy. Stand there."

*Abernathy.* The name landed somewhere familiar, and somewhere not.

The curious filament found the edge of his cupped hand, slipped around it like a cat around a doorframe, and touched the inside of her wrist.

Cold. A small, precise *click*, like a turnstile. Like a barcode reader.

*Counted.*

"No," said Susmithaa. It was the voice from the engagement dinner, low and certain, sandalwood and silver rings. "Not her. You don't get her."

She stopped fighting.

Every flame left in her turned at once, the way a flock turns, and *poured*. Down her burning arm, across the impossible distance, into the one wick in Arenya's fist. It wasn't heat. It was weight. Every lamp lit in that window for every year Arenya had been alive.

Her hand was full of morning.

The net clenched shut on the place where the woman had been and caught nothing but ash, and the faint ring of silver charms falling.

What was left of her face was a few flames wide. It came close enough to kiss Arenya's forehead, and it did.

"You reached, kanna. That's all it ever was." The voice was fraying. "They aren't the war. They're people who think they can win it before it starts. Something old is waking. The doors are going to open, and every lamp will matter. Keep it lit." A ghost of the old mischief crossed what remained of her. "And look after that one. He'll say he's looking after you.

"*He's lying.*"

Then she was gone.

Far below, in the real garden, the small woman in the cardigan sat down on the wet grass beside the curry-leaf tree. Gently. Like someone stopping to rest.

Three of the four men were already turning away, the way a crew turns at the end of a shift.

The one in grey didn't. His static had gone very still.

The stillness of a man taking out a pen.

* * *

She woke in her own bed in the dark.

Her right hand was clenched so tight her nails had cut half-moons into the palm. The palm was warm, the way a mug stays warm after the coffee's gone. On the inside of her wrist was a cold spot the size of a coin.

The clock said 3:19.

At 3:20 her phone lit up. **Amma.**

And from the kitchen, through the half-open door, she heard her kettle being filled, and somebody swearing at it under his breath.

In cockney.

She didn't look. The kettle and the swearing went into the same drawer as the garden and the fog, and she slammed the drawer with her whole body. Her left hand found the phone. The right stayed a fist and wouldn't open.

"Amma? It's three in the morning."

She already knew.

In the kitchen doorway the swearing stopped. He stood with one shoulder against the frame, the coat hanging wrong even at rest. His lantern had burned down to a dull orange ember, and he held it the way you'd hold a sparrow.

*Let her get it out herself, love,* said his voice, thin and close, somewhere that wasn't quite her ear. *That's your aunt's light in your fist. Near enough all of it. Keep that hand shut till it settles.* He looked at her wrist, and the patter stopped. *Somethin' counted you. Don't scratch it.*

Then, not meeting her eyes: *She said somethin' at the end. About me. I don't know what she meant. I'm sayin' so now, before you start wonderin' alone.*

On the line there was only breathing, wet and ragged and far from the phone. Behind it a television played in an empty room, and Appa's voice said *okay, okay, okay* to nobody.

"Kanna." Amma was shaking. "It's Aunty. Appa went out because the dog next door would not stop. She was in the garden in only her cardigan, in this cold, sitting by the curry-leaf tree like she was waiting for somebody. He thought she was sleeping." A sound came out of her that wasn't a word. "They took her to Seton."

Then her voice dropped, the way Amma lowered it in temples and at the DMV.

"And her lamp went out. Thirty-one years, kanna, I never once saw it out. There was oil still in it." A breath. "And there is a man here at the hospital. American, very polite. He was here *before* us. He asked the nurse for Susmithaa Sowri by her full name. And he asked if she has a niece."

Arenya sat in the dark with a dead woman's morning shut inside her fist.

Behind her, the kettle ticked as it cooled.

* * *

The first thing Caleb knew was the smell: burnt hair and hot plastic, rising off his own skull.

The second was that he was sitting in the passenger seat of a grey sedan. A canister sat at his feet. Its little status light was green, which meant *full*. Behind the inspection glass lay a grey drift of ash.

Half a block up, a porch light had snapped on, and someone inside was screaming a word he didn't know. *Aunty*, maybe. It went into him like a nail.

*Awake.*

Dorsey sat behind the wheel with his chin on his chest. Pruitt and Hale were slumped in the back. All three had their eyes open. Nobody was blinking.

The Crown buzzed in his molars. Shepherd, cut to pieces: *"—canister reads full. Confirm col—"*

Fourteen months of habit knew exactly what his face should be doing. For the first time, it wasn't doing it. He had to build it by hand: jaw slack, eyes soft, breath slow over a heart that hammered like someone locked in a trunk.

"Canister reads full," he said. Flat. Flat was easy. "Collection confirmed. Team Four standing down."

*"—copy. Strand. Your filament's still out. Why is your filament still out?"*

It was. A cold thread ran up his forearm, south-east, to someone at the other end who was awake. A woman.

"Residual tether," he said, bored. "Lift was messy. Reeling it in."

He pinched the thread at his wrist and folded it, the way you'd fold a wet paper chain, and pushed the fold back down the line toward her. He didn't snap it. He didn't want to hurt her, and he didn't want Shepherd to feel a snap. He felt the knot travel away and close somewhere warm.

Not cut. Somewhere, a woman now wore a cold coin on her skin that only he could find.

*"Proceed to Drop Two. Canister goes hand to hand. No stops."*

He lifted the canister into his lap and held it like a sleeping child. It was warm.

At the empty red light by the on-ramp, he pressed the seatbelt release. In a car that quiet, the click sounded like a rifle bolt.

Three heads came up together.

He was out the door before the click finished echoing, canister clamped to his ribs. Over a low wall, through a side yard, into the black slot between two garages.

They didn't chase him. That was the worst of it. The light turned green and the sedan sat through it, three men waiting for instructions the way a television waits for a channel. Then it pulled away, unhurried. Going to report.

The Crown hummed. And for the first time in fourteen months, the voice in it wasn't asking for a status.

*"Caleb."*

Soft. Patient. A man calling a cat in off the road. Nobody had said that name since the day the Crown went in. He'd forgotten it had a sound.

*"That canister's empty, son. Come in on your own and this stays a malfunction. You won't even remember being scared."*

"My name's Caleb." Out loud, too loud. He no longer knew how to say anything inside his own head. "You don't get to say it again. And it's not empty. Her name was Susmithaa. I'm not coming in." His arms tightened. "I'd rather be scared."

*"Susmithaa,"* Shepherd said, tasting it like a wine he hadn't ordered. *"Noted. Be seeing you, Caleb."*

At the end of the slot a storm culvert opened onto a channel of black water. Ten steps into the rusted pipe, the hum in his teeth thinned to nothing.

Silence. The first real silence in fourteen months, with nobody in his skull.

Then his hands started to shake, and he remembered all of it—rooms, nets, names, the sounds people made—and he sat down in the water because his legs decided to.

"Tartine," he told the steel. "The old man in the scarf. That was us. Tonight. Susmithaa. Daly City."

His left hand found the two Vesper doses in his pocket. It closed on them, and then it let them go.

"I don't know who to give you to," he told the ash. "But I'm not handing you back. Not till somebody who isn't them tells me what you are."

He kept his hand away from the pocket, and counted drips.

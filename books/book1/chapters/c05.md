# Chapter Five: Leftovers and Larceny

“So,” Saiesh said, chewing a mouthful of cold naan, “how does it feel to have officially committed cybercrime for the sake of a moderately paid journalism job?”

Arenya sat cross-legged on the floor, her back against the couch, sipping lukewarm chai that had gone slightly bitter. “Technically, I committed fraud through the moderately paid journalism job. That’s called leveraging your position.”

He pointed a piece of naan at her. “Your honor, the defendant shows no remorse.”

“She shows resourcefulness.”

They were surrounded by the quiet chaos of a late night hang: laptop cords snaking across the rug, sticky notes stuck to the floor, her notes stacked in uneven piles, and two half-finished samosas on a paper plate between them.

Saiesh lay sprawled on his stomach, chin propped on one hand, clicking through tabs like he wasn’t definitely aiding a federal offense.

“You realize if this gets traced back to Teddy, you’re not just fired,” he said.

“I’m aware.”

“Like, lawsuits. Blacklisted. Possibly interrogated by a man in a trench coat.”

Arenya raised an eyebrow. “You’re enjoying this too much.”

“Absolutely,” he said. “The only thing better than being a genius coder is being a genius accessory.”

She snorted and tossed a pillow at him. It bounced off his arm without fanfare.

A moment passed.

She reached for her notebook, flipped it open, and stared at the latest interview notes. Names. Timestamps. That same line over and over again: froze for thirty seconds. Looked scared. Collapsed.

She didn’t say anything for a while.

“I’m not wrong about this,” she muttered.

“No,” Saiesh said, rolling onto his back. “You’re not.”

She glanced at him.

He was picking at a tear in the rug, his fingers twitchy the way they got when his brain was going faster than his mouth. He’d always been like that—nervous hands, quick thoughts, big energy in a soft shell.

“You still do that thing,” she said absently. “With your fingers.”

He looked up. “What thing?”

“Like you’re unraveling a wire. You did it during finals week sophomore year. Also during that fire drill when you were trying to decide whether to tell me you’d built that entire VR ethics module by yourself.”

He blinked, then gave a lopsided shrug. “I didn’t want you to be mad.”

“I wasn’t mad. I was impressed. I also took full credit.”

He smiled at the ceiling. “Yeah. I remember.”

It was quiet again, but not empty. Just paused. Like something hovering in a room, waiting for permission to land.

Arenya closed the notebook and leaned her head back.

“You ever think we were more brilliant when we were broke and stupid?” she asked.

“Constantly,” he said. “I peaked at twenty-one.”

“That’s sad.”

“I mean, I’m still great. Just—great in a different way now. Less fire, more… sustained flicker.”

She smirked.

He glanced at her. “You don’t talk about what you want anymore.”

She didn’t respond immediately. Just watched the steam curling up from her mug like it was spelling something out.

“I’m still figuring it out,” she said finally.

“Thought you already knew. Truth. Justice. Hard-hitting exposés.”

“Yeah, but I thought those things would come with… a sense of purpose. Not a list of login credentials and a fake database entry.”

He looked at her for a second like he wanted to say something, but didn’t.

Instead, he reached for the samosa and took a bite. “Could be worse.”

“How?”

“You could be writing another article about therapy turtles.”

She snorted.

They let the silence settle in again.

“You ever think about going back to it?” she asked. “Building things that matter?”

Saiesh shrugged. “Sometimes. But I get paid too well for shit I don’t believe in. Golden handcuffs. You know how it goes.”

“Yeah,” she said. “I do.”

A beat passed.

“I didn’t want to drag you into this.”

“You didn’t,” he said, a little too quickly. “I volunteered.”

They didn’t look at each other.

He cleared his throat. “Anyway. When the footage comes in, I’ll help you decrypt it. I’ve got some scripts I can run to clean up the compression artifacts.”

She nodded. “Thanks.”

He stood, stretched, and grabbed his jacket from the hook. “I should go. Meeting tomorrow. Something about predictive text and empathy scores. Very important, deeply impactful.”

She rolled her eyes. “Don’t let the burrito algorithm down.”

He hesitated at the door, keys jingling in his hand. “You’re doing something real, Arenya. Whatever this turns out to be.”

Her face didn’t move. “I know.”

He gave her a small nod, then left.

She stood there for a while after, staring at the closed door.

There were a lot of things they didn’t say.

Maybe that’s why they worked.

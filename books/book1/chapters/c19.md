# Chapter Nineteen: To the Beneficiary

Colma had fifteen hundred living residents and a million and a half dead, and it had arranged itself accordingly. Cemetery gates alternated with florists. Every block had somewhere to buy a bucket of roses and somewhere to put them down.

**ESPOSO MONUMENT CO. — SINCE 1961** was a chain-link lot of uncut granite blanks standing in rows, a waiting room for people who hadn't arrived yet. At the back, past the last row, a corrugated-tin shed turned its door away from the street as if it were embarrassed to be seen.

The man in the dust-white apron followed Nana's eyes to it, and his face did a small, practiced nothing. "That. City says it's on our lot. We don't go in. You go, you go. Don't touch my blanks."

Kino stepped through the gate first, as agreed, and stopped two paces in. Under his breath, the old words Nana had taught him for entering a place that wasn't his. *Con permiso. Vengo a ver, no a tomar.*

With permission. I've come to see, not to take.

Something low and heavy went with him, at heel.

Arenya stayed at the gate with Nana's hand on her elbow. The 12 in her palm didn't hurt. It *itched*, the way a word you can't remember itches.

"If it starts to open for me," she said, "pull me back."

"By the hair," Nana said. "I've done it before. Ask your tía."

Every blank in every row carried a faint pencil-grey line at exactly the same height, level from stone to stone, all the way back to the shed. The yard was ruled like a page. When Arenya opened her notebook to write the time, the printed lines on her cheap paper had drifted to run parallel with it.

"Facts are fine," Nana murmured. "Facts it already has. Just don't sign the bottom."

Halfway down, Kino crouched to sight along the line, the way you'd sight a trail. When he stood, his voice came back low and flat, a report from a ridge.

"Twelve rows. The shed's the heading. There's a file of narrow stones along the left fence, one at the head of each row. That's the margin." He swallowed. "Row eleven has a salt stroke chiseled in its margin stone. The line dips through it, like somebody argued there. That's Susmithaa. That's March."

A few paces farther, the jaguar stopped him. He didn't argue.

"Row twelve's clean. The margin stone's polished. One pencil stroke on it. One, not two." He didn't say whose. "It's waiting for a second."

Under the 12 in her palm, the itch gathered to a single point, like a finger set down on a map.

"Kino," she said. "That stone is *mine*. Nobody gets to be brave on it before I say so."

He circled wide to the back of the shed. Behind him, the polishing wheel in the shop cut off mid-whine for no reason, and the Tagalog radio went with it.

* * *

Kino leaned toward the tin without touching it.

He heard a pen. Dip and scratch, unhurried, with the patience of something that had been doing this since 1912. And under it, the voice that had counted beneath Hale's breathing, dry as old paper. Not speaking to anyone. Reading back.

*"Once, entered. Once, contested."* A pause. *"Doce. Testigo: una. Falta una."*

Eleven, entered and contested. Twelve: one witness. One missing.

The pen stopped.

The attention came through the tin like cold through a window and settled on the ache in his arm. It asked, without sound, pen held over the line:

*¿Y tú?*

And you?

Twenty years of fasting and night-walks and dreaming on the mountain, waiting for something old to turn and ask for *him*. Here it was, through a tin wall in Colma. The want came up in his chest so fast and so clean that it shamed him. *Sí* would have been the easiest word of his life.

He didn't nod. He didn't shake his head. Either one was an answer, and you don't lie near Colma.

So he gave the only true thing he had. Slowly, so the attention could follow, he turned his head and looked back down twelve rows of granite to the gate. To Arenya, with her hand shut and Nana's fist on her elbow.

*Not mine to give. Ask her. Not today.*

* * *

From forty yards off, Arenya saw his face change. Her palm went warm in her fist, a sun-heat gathering beside the cold of the 12, in the place where a second stroke would go.

"Kino! Don't answer it. Not *yes*, not *no*. Walk back to me."

He was already moving, backing off the concrete pad without turning his back on the door, the way you leave a lion's ground. He didn't look at the clean stone in row twelve as he passed it.

Then all three of them felt it. Something followed his look down the rows, along the pencil line, to the gate post and the girl standing at it. It rested on her for the length of a held pen. It wasn't hostile. It felt like being *read*, a line at a time, by someone with all the time in the world.

Inside the shed, the pen made one small sound, too short to be a word.

A tick.

The polishing wheel started up again mid-whine. The radio laughed at something.

"Next time, Mama, call first," the apron man told Nana, his eyes on the back of the lot. "It's been busy this week. Lot of visitors."

In the Corolla, Arenya opened her hand.

The 12 was there, cold, with its rule beneath it. Beside the rule, where a second stroke would go, there was no stroke. There was a dot of pencil-grey, small and exact. The mark a clerk leaves beside a line to say *come back to this*.

*Referred,* she thought. *Like a bad knee.*

Kino sat with his hood up and his hands under his thighs and said nothing.

As Nana pulled out onto Mission Road, Arenya saw a beige Camry parked outside the florist across the street. Empty. The plate began **7K**. On the dash lay a parking slip from **Colma Notary & Mail**, two doors down.

*That's a lot of visitors,* said Abernathy, with no accent at all.

* * *

The Camry was there again on Wednesday morning, in the same spot, as if it had never left.

At 11:09, three-quarters of a mile from the Serramonte Library and Hale's noon meeting, Kino said what he'd been holding since the shed.

"I wanted to say yes. It's the first thing I've ever wanted that badly that wasn't the mountain. That's what scares me. Not the clerk. Me." He breathed out. "If I'd been alone, I don't know."

Arenya didn't have a joke for that.

"In there," he said, before she could find one, "we don't sign. We don't say your whole name. If anybody offers you a form, you hand it to me, and I read every line twice."

Nana parked nose out, like a getaway she was pretending not to plan. "If the grey man comes back, I honk twice. If I honk once, a lady cut me off. Don't confuse them."

**COLMA NOTARY & MAIL · LIVE SCAN · APOSTILLE · SE HABLA ESPAÑOL · TAGALOG.** The bell over the door was real brass on a curl of spring, and it gave one flat note.

One long room. A wall of brass PO boxes with little glass windows. Behind the counter, a man in a pressed guayabera eating sinigang from a styrofoam cup: sixty or so, reading glasses pushed up into grey hair, a notary seal on a lanyard like a saint's medal. **R. PANGANIBAN.**

Halfway down the wall, Box 112 hung open half an inch. It was the only door in the room that wasn't flush.

*Brass,* Abernathy said, low. *Hot brass, out loud like you asked. Comin' from 112. And ink under it, wet. I* nearly *know it, love. I nearly—*

"Mailbox, fingerprints, or notary?" The man set his spoon down at a precise right angle to the cup. "If notary, you sign my journal and you give me your thumb. Everybody signs for me."

Then he looked at her properly, and stopped.

"Ah," he said quietly. "You have her chin."

He slid the fat green journal away from them and laid his palm flat on it. "That one is closed today. Twice already this morning people want to look in my book." He nodded at a clipboard. A *Request for Copy of Notary Journal Entry*, filled out in neat block capitals. Date requested: **03/19**.

"I'm curious about the lady with my chin," Arenya said. "I'm not here to sign anything. Did she come in here?"

"Three scarves," Rodel said. "In March, three scarves, and rings on every finger like she's going to punch somebody. She brought me champorado in a jar because she said my soup was sad. She was not wrong." He wiped his fingers one at a time. "A declaration. Her brother witnessed, very nervous man. She read every line out loud before she signed. Nobody does that. She said, *Rodel, if anybody comes asking about this day, you make them write it down.*" He tapped the clipboard. "So I make them write it down."

"Has anyone besides you had a hand in that box today?" Kino asked.

Rodel lowered his voice, though there was nobody else in the room.

"Same day, one hour after the lady, a man comes in. Nice suit. Very polite. He rents one-twelve. *Meridian Mutual Services.* A year in advance, cash, all new twenties. Every Friday, seven-forty, before I open, he comes with a key. Always he closes it." A pause. "Today is Wednesday. I came in at eight and it was like that. A finger's width. Like somebody wants me to see."

Through the little glass window of 112 stood one cream envelope, upright, the same heavy stock as the letter in her bag.

**TO THE BENEFICIARY**

*To be collected in person. Identification required.*

"Hija." Rodel took his glasses down from his hair. "I don't ask your name. Don't tell me. But if you take that out of the box, you are the one who took it. That is how the post works. And that is how *they* work too, I think."

She didn't reach for it. She stepped back from it, which felt like the smartest thing she'd done all week, and raised her phone. No flash. Then she lowered it.

"I'm not taking it. That's a door with my name already on the other side." She breathed. "The man who filled out your request this morning. Did he put down a name?"

Rodel turned the clipboard around with one finger. **W. BELL.** *Purpose of request: Verification of beneficiary.* The signature trailed off into a long flat stroke, ruler-straight, like a line under a sum.

"Grey sweater," he said, rubbing his palm slowly on his trousers. "Cold hands. I shook them because I'm polite. Still cold."

The phone in Arenya's hand had finished loading the photo. The brass door, the gap, the envelope behind the glass. And in its printed window, where the glass in front of her said TO THE BENEFICIARY, the photograph said something else.

Her whole name. Typed. Centered. The one Susmithaa had told her never to say in a room they chose.

Her stomach went down a stairwell without her.

Outside, the Corolla honked once. Then, after a deliberate count of two, again.

"Thank you, Mr. Panganiban. Please don't let anyone sign for that envelope." At the door she stopped, not looking back. "Leave the box open. Let it wait."

Kino took the street side without being asked. Nana had the engine going before his door shut.

"Hale's noon is in twenty minutes," Arenya said, her shaking hand flat on her knee. "I'm not going. But on Friday at seven-forty, somebody comes for that box, and I want to know who. From a car. Not a counter."

In the side mirror, the Camry wasn't empty anymore.

Mr. Bell sat behind the wheel in his grey cardigan, writing in a small notebook without looking down. He didn't turn his head as they passed. Then he got out and crossed the street with the unhurried walk of a man with an appointment. At the florist's buckets he chose a single white chrysanthemum and paid in coins.

Then he opened the door of Colma Notary & Mail.

Arenya watched the brass bell move on its spring.

She didn't hear it ring.

In her lap, under her closed fingers, faint as a held breath, the pencil dot beside the rule was still there.

Waiting.

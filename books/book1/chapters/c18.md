# Chapter Eighteen: Eleven, Twelve

"Ravi's birthday," Amma said from the kitchen, without turning around. "Day, month, year. The only date in this family she never forgot." Cups clinked. "Don't tell him. He'll cry again."

"Airplane mode first," Nana said. "Whoever calls like that is listening for the phone to *answer*, not for a voice. We close the door before we open the letter."

Arenya thumbed it on and watched the little plane appear like a promise. Kino drew the curtains and shot the deadbolt. She typed the code slowly, the way you'd turn a key in a lock you suspected was wired.

The wallpaper was a photo of the lamp, taken from below, so the flame looked enormous.

She pressed play.

The voice was pleasant and unhurried, American with no region in it anywhere, trained flat like a lawn.

*"Good evening. This message is for the household of Ms. Susmithaa Sowri."* He said it right. *Sow-ri*, the *r* tapped against the roof of the mouth. Nobody outside the family ever said it that way. *"My name is Daniel Hale, beneficiary services at Meridian Mutual Life. I'm so sorry for your loss. I understand Ms. Sowri passed early this morning, at 3:19 a.m. In March, Ms. Sowri took out a policy with us and named a single beneficiary. Policy terms require me to verify that beneficiary in person. It won't take more than a few minutes of her time. Please call me back. We'd like to settle this quickly, for everyone's sake."*

Click.

"Three nineteen," Amma said. "The hospital said *around* three thirty. Nobody told us three nineteen."

Arenya held the power button until the screen went black. "Three nineteen is the minute she died," she said. She knew, because it was the minute she woke. "A hospital doesn't have that. An insurance company has it by dinnertime?" She looked up. "Amma. Did she ever mention a policy?"

"Your aunty didn't trust the bank with her gold. She kept it in a Horlicks tin."

Amma looked at Appa. Appa looked at the floor.

"The lamp ducked," Kino said. He hadn't taken his eyes off the wick. "Not on *beneficiary*. On *her*. 'A few minutes of *her* time.' It went small and blue at the root, like somebody opened a freezer door."

*Nobody calls that number back,* Arenya thought. *Nobody meets anybody in person.*

Nana folded the dead phone into a fresh towel, for the freezer. "She told me once at the Safeway, by the onions. *Itzel, I bought a door, and I put Ari's name on it.* I thought she meant the temple." She tucked a corner tight. "A door can be locked from either side, mija."

In the kitchen, the landline rang.

The old beige phone by the fridge, kept "for emergencies and Periamma in Chennai." Nobody called it. Amma half rose.

"Don't." Arenya was across the room before the third ring, palm out like she was stopping traffic. Her right hand was hot enough to feel in her teeth. "Let the machine take it. Nobody says *hello*. Nobody says our name."

Fourth ring. Appa's voice from 2009, younger and cheerful: *"You have reached the Sowri residence—"*

The beep cut him off.

*"Good evening again. Daniel Hale, Meridian Mutual. I see the other handset has gone quiet. That's all right. People grieve differently."* Paper rustled, too deliberately. *"I believe this one is for Arenya."*

Amma's hand flew to her mouth.

*"The policy is real, Ms. Sowri. Your aunt was very careful. She paid in cash, in person, at our Colma office, on the nineteenth of March. Wednesday at noon, the Serramonte Library, second floor, by the periodicals. Bring identification. If noon doesn't suit, I'm happy to come to you."* He let that sit. *"Though I'd hate to bring paperwork to your mother's door during the mourning period. And Ms. Sowri? Keep that hand warm. Goodnight."*

Dial tone.

Arenya looked down. She'd taken it all down, steady, in block capitals. Nearly all. In the margin, beside *bring identification*, were two words she had no memory of writing.

*Eleven, twelve.*

Her handwriting. Her pen. She hadn't heard them, not consciously, and that was what turned her stomach over.

"I heard it," Kino said. His eyes had gone to the freezer. "Behind his breath. Someone else on the line, slow, like counting steps in the dark. It stopped when he said *hand*."

Along the freezer seal, a thin white rime had grown. It hadn't been there a minute ago.

Nana laid her palm flat on it. The rime softened under her hand, and then stopped softening.

"Your aunty told me once, when we were both drunk on terrible Christmas wine. They count the ones who carry. While they are still hunting, they don't say the number out loud. When they say it out loud, they are done looking." Her eyes found the margin of the notebook.

"Eleven was somebody. Twelve is you."

* * *

At 2:47 the house had the particular silence of people pretending to sleep.

The lamp straightened all at once, like a dog lifting its head.

Something cold came through the back screen without opening it. The kitchen tiles went grey with frost, one by one, in a line toward the front room, and the line had a shape: low and heavy and longer than any dog, something her eyes kept wanting to call a shadow. It lay down across Kino's shins and pushed its muzzle into his palm.

He breathed with it, eyes gone somewhere else.

"Serramonte," he whispered. "Second floor. The periodical racks are strung with frost, ankle height, like a rabbit snare. A ring of chairs by the window, all cold except one. That one's warm, like somebody just stood up." He swallowed. "And on the way home, at a stone yard in Colma, something noticed him. It didn't chase. It made a tidy little mark, the way a toll booth logs a plate."

Arenya's right palm began to tingle. A fingertip, going slow, as if someone were writing on her to see whether she'd flinch.

She didn't close the hand. She turned it up to the lamp.

A stroke straight down. A hook, a curve, a flat base. A pause, long enough to breathe in. Then one last stroke dragged under the pair from heel to fingers, ruled and straight, like the line under a sum.

*12.*

"It's not writing on me," she said, clipped and small. "It's *filing* me."

*Don't flinch, love,* said Abernathy, too cheerful. *Grey ink, proper tidy. And it's comin' up from* under. *Somewhere wet and stony.* The patter went thin. *And there's somethin' under the twelve. Faint, like a watermark. A little circle with rays on it. A sun, like a kiddie draws.* A pause. *I know that shape. Don't ask me from where. It's made my coat go cold.*

Down the hall, a door opened.

Appa came in barefoot, in the old Cal sweatshirt he slept in, holding a long white envelope against his chest like a hymnbook. Across the flap, in Susmithaa's round handwriting: ***Ravi — only if they call.***

"She made me witness something in March," he said. "At a notary in Colma. I signed where she pointed. She wouldn't let me read it. I told your Amma it was a will." His thumb rested on the seal. "I lied to everyone in this house for seven months, and I am very bad at it."

From the armchair, Amma's breathing stopped being even.

"They called, Appa," Arenya said. "Twice. Give me the weight."

There were three things inside.

A letter on heavy cream stock, blue-and-silver letterhead. *MERIDIAN MUTUAL LIFE · Beneficiary Services.* Dated March 9. *Welcome to the Meridian family.* Below that, a little grid in typewriter font:

> INSURED: SOWRI, SUSMITHAA ............ **11**
> BENEFICIARY: SOWRI, ARENYA (NIECE) .... **12**

*A representative will contact the beneficiary in person to verify.* And across the whole page, in Aunty's blue ballpoint, pressed so hard the pen had torn through the paper at the O:

**NO.**

Two pages of legal paper with a raised seal. *Declaration of Susmithaa Sowri.* She had purchased no policy from Meridian Mutual Life. She named no beneficiary. Her niece was party to no contract made in her name. Witnessed in Appa's careful engineer's capitals. *Colma, California.* March 19.

"March ninth, *welcome to the family*," Arenya said. "Ten days before Hale says she walked in with cash. His story's backwards. Nobody bought anything. They sent her a receipt for something she never ordered." Her finger touched the torn O, lightly, as if it might still be hot. "And she went to a notary and swore she never bought it. With you as her witness, Appa. Because you'd sign where she pointed and not read."

The third thing was a page torn from the spiral pad that had lived by Aunty's kettle forever. At the bottom, where a name should have been, a little circle with a crown of quick rays.

A sun. It was how Aunty had signed every birthday card Arenya had ever gotten from her. She'd never once asked why.

*That's not ink,* Abernathy said quietly. *It's lit. Same mark as under the twelve.* A pause. *Lookin' at it, I heard a voice say,* Mark it so it can be found again. *Low. Patient. Mine, and not mine.*

She read it aloud, in Aunty's brisk voice as best she could.

*Ari kanna,*

*If Ravi has given you this, they've called and I'm not there to pick up. I'm sorry. I wanted to be the one who picked up.*

*In March they sent me that letter. That is how they say* we have found you. *Men who count don't knock. They send paperwork.*

*Writing is not keeping. If your palm has been written on, don't scrub it and don't hide it. Just don't sign. Don't verify. Don't say your full name to them in a room they chose. A thing gets kept when you agree to it.*

*The paper Ravi signed is my no. Itzel knows what a no is worth to the one under Colma. Make her tell you all of it; she leaves out the parts that frighten her.*

*If Itzel's boy is there, the cold one, the lamp has already decided something. Feed him. He doesn't eat when he's worried.*

*Tell your Appa he lied very well for once, and I'm proud of him.*

Appa broke first. Quietly. He put his big hand over his mouth and kept it there. Amma said to the carpet in Tamil, *Stubborn, stubborn woman,* the way people say *I love you* when they can't manage the words.

In Arenya's palm, the warmth stopped pushing against the cold twelve and *settled*, like someone sitting down beside her on a bench. The number was still there. It just wasn't alone with her anymore.

A floorboard creaked. Nana stood at the edge of the lamplight, braid undone, shoes still on.

"*Ay, Susmita.* Telling on me from the grave." She pulled Aunty's reading chair to the lamp. "Somebody put the kettle on."

Arenya put the first mug into Kino's hands. "Aunty's orders."

"In nineteen-twelve," Nana said, "San Francisco decided the dead were bad for property values. They dug up a hundred and fifty thousand, maybe more, and carted them south to Colma. You can't move that many dead without someone *counting*. Clerks wrote every box down in ledgers, by lamplight, in a shed by the stone yard that smelled of wet granite and ink." She looked at Kino. "Ix smelled the shed."

"The clerks died. The counting didn't. The stonecutters called it *el Contador*. Not a devil. A clerk. It writes down who is dead in that valley, and who is going to be. In a hundred years it has never once made a mistake in its sums." Her hands tightened on the mug. "And somebody has learned to read its books. Whatever it writes, they come and collect."

"In March, Susmita brought that letter to my kitchen, *shaking*. After the notary we walked to the stone yard, and she put a copy of her no under the shed door, with salt and a candle, the old way. I stood witness. The candle went out. Afterward the salt on the paper was arranged into a line underneath. Like a sum." Her mouth worked. "*A no buys time, not freedom.* It bought her seven months."

"And there was a second mark beside the line. Two small strokes, close together. My grandmother had a ledger page from that shed. When somebody disputed a name, the clerk didn't cross it out. They don't cross out. He drew two strokes in the margin. *Dos testigos.* Two witnesses. It means *this entry is contested, and it stays open until two stand for it*."

"A very fair clerk," Arenya said. "That's worse, you know. Monsters you can fight. Clerks you have to *dispute*." She looked at the torn NO. "Nobody decides anything tonight. I'm not signing Kino up for something because some salt looked like a pair." She breathed. "But I'm not saying it alone either. If it comes to that, I'd want it to be you."

She didn't take it back.

Kino set the mug down. She understood a beat too late that he'd waited a very long time to hear something like that. He'd probably imagined it coming from a mountain.

"Okay," he said, and left it rough. "Not tonight. But I heard you."

*Abernathy,* she said, where only one person could hear. *That voice. Whose was it?*

The answer came after a long moment.

*I went lookin', love. Nothin'. Not fog. Fog I'd know. It's a clean wall, smooth as a page nobody wrote on.* A pause, and the cockney went thin as paper. *Doesn't feel like forgettin'. Feels like somebody tidied up after me. I don't care for that.*

Appa got down on his knees and pulled the landline cord out of the wall, gently, like taking a splinter out of a child.

The frost on the freezer seal held.

So did the lamp.

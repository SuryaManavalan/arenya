Friday.

The fog over Mission Road had come apart into strips, and the low sun lit them from underneath, gold, like steam off a pot. Across the road the Esposo yard was still chained, its granite blanks grey with dew. Two doors down, Flores de la Paz had its buckets out already, chrysanthemums and marigolds, and a woman in a fleece was hosing the sidewalk.

Appa parked the Corolla nose-out, the way he always parked, and left the engine running.

"I am here," he told the windshield. "Seven-forty, I come in. Before, if you wave." His hands stayed at ten and two.

The roll-down gate of Colma Notary & Mail was shut. Behind its slats the shop lights were on. Arenya could see the counter, the wall of brass post-office boxes, and Rodel Panganiban in a cardigan, sitting very straight on his stool. A big green clothbound register lay open in front of him. He wasn't reading it. He was watching the door.

When he saw them he got up and went around to the side, where a steel door opened onto the alley between his shop and the florist. He held it with his foot.

"Come in, come in. Don't stand in the street." He glanced up at the strip-mall sign, then at the sky, like a man checking for rain. "Fifteen minutes. At seven-forty I open, because I always open at seven-forty, and today I am doing nothing I don't always do."

The box wall was warmer than the room. She felt it as she passed, like standing near a stove that had just been switched off. Box 12 sat at shoulder height, closed and ordinary. Box 112 was low, by the floor, and its little brass door was clean. Every other door on the wall wore a film of dust and fingerprints.

That one had been wiped.

Rodel laid his palm flat on the open register and didn't sit.

"Ask me," he said. "I was waiting for her niece to ask me. Ask me right, and I'll tell you right."

*Brass again, love,* said Abernathy, somewhere near her ear. Ix was a pressure low against her shin, keeping away from the wall. *Not hot. Warm. Like somebody's just took their hand off it.*

She opened the notebook on the counter, pen already moving, and kept her other hand well clear of the brass.

"Mr. Panganiban. I'm Susmithaa's niece, and I'm not here to scare you. I'm here because you said a sentence to my father, and I'd like it in order. Who is *he*? You said he asked for one-twelve before it was empty. Who asked, when, and how? A call, a note, in person? A number, a name, a hand you recognized? Start there. And whose number is on the 2207 line? Because it's yours."

She glanced at Kino. Back to Rodel. Softened a notch.

"Take your time. Just not fifteen minutes' worth."

Kino hadn't moved from the side door. Shoulder to the frame, he could see the gate, the alley and the brass at once. His eyes were on Rodel's hands, not his face.

"She sat nine nights with your mother," he said, low. "Whoever *he* is, he used that. Tell it how it happened. Not how you'd want it to have happened."

One hand went to his pocket and the twist of salt in it. Nothing else.

Rodel looked at that hand, then at Arenya's chin, the way he had on Wednesday. Then he bent under the counter and came up with a different book. Not the green notary journal. A fat blue post-office ledger, BOX RENTALS 2014– on a strip of masking tape, the spine mended twice with packing tape.

"The journal's the State's," he said. "This one's mine."

He opened it near the back and turned it to face them. Every box had a row. Row 112, in his slanted ballpoint: **S. SOWRI — opened 04/2015 — cash, yearly.** Eleven renewals were initialled down the column. The last entry read **CLOSED 03/06/26, key returned, box emptied by renter.**

Above that line, squeezed into the margin in pencil, was one more sentence.

*Hold 112 for Mr. Dalal on vacancy.*

"That's my pencil," Rodel said. "That's my book. I wrote it while he stood where you're standing." He put a fingertip beside the line without touching it. "Look at it, anak."

The line was perfectly straight. Every other word on the page leaned right, tired, a man who wrote standing up. These letters sat on an invisible ruler. The last *y* ended in a small hook, like a bracket.

Arenya knew that hook. She had been staring at it for two days, on the back of a check.

"End of February," Rodel said. "A Friday, around this time of day. He came in person. Nice shirt, from the temple in Fremont, he said. He knew her star. He knew she'd had a box here eleven years." He swallowed. "I can't tell you his face. I've tried, at night. I get the shirt and I get his hands. Cold, like Mr. Bell's. He didn't sign anything. He didn't pay anything. He asked me to write it for him, so I did, and I thought it was *my* idea to write it so neat."

He tapped the counter twice, hard.

"Then he asked if the family could use my number 'for the paperwork,' since she trusted me. I said yes. I said yes, Mrs. Sowri's niece. That's how my number got on your line."

His eyes went to the wall. "Box twelve wrote to box one-twelve every March for eleven years. Long envelopes, no stamp, just put in. She took them home and never opened them here. One week after that man came, she emptied her box and gave me her key, and she was angry. Not at me. She said, *'Rodel, they're moving in next door to me.'*"

His voice caught.

"I didn't understand. Thirteen days later she was back with your father's friend, notarizing her no. And an hour after her, the polite one walked in and rented one-twelve with new twenties. He asked for it by number."

The clock over the stamp machine said 7:29. Out on Mission Road a car door shut. It wasn't Appa's.

*The pencil smells like the box did,* Abernathy murmured. His forced cockney had thinned to something close and careful. *Hot brass, but old. A cold stamp, months cold. Someone wrote* through *his hand, little light. And that car door...* A pause. *I can't smell it. That's what bothers me. Everything on this street smells of something.*

Arenya copied the pencil line into her notebook. She stopped at the final *y*. She didn't touch the ledger. She took out her phone, slowly, so he'd see it coming, and held it over the page.

"I want a photograph of this page and of her card. Not a copy, not the book. I'm taking nothing. It's evidence that your pencil is yours and his hand isn't." She tilted the notebook so he could see the bracket-hooked *y* she'd traced from check 2207. "Same hand that endorsed her check 'R. Sowri.' You wrote his words and thought they were yours. That isn't a crime. It's what he does, and you're the first person who's told me so."

She looked at the clock. At the door.

"Who just pulled up out there? And before seven-forty, did he give you instructions for today? What to say, what to do, when someone asks for one-twelve?"

Kino slid along the wall to the slats, never turning his back on the room. He read the street: plate, driver, hands. His right hand stayed near the salt.

"Arenya. Keep the phone low," he said. "Photograph it and step back from the brass. If anyone comes through that door, you're nobody's niece and nobody's signer. You're a customer."

Rodel looked at the hooked *y* for a long second. Then he gave one short nod.

"Take your picture. Pictures aren't signatures."

The phone clicked twice. On the screen the ledger looked the way it did on the counter, with one difference. Under *Hold 112 for Mr. Dalal on vacancy* ran a faint grey rule that wasn't on the paper. It crossed the full width of the page and off the edge, like the line drawn under a column of figures before the total gets written.

The card in the back said *S. Sowri, box surrendered, 03/06/26.* Her aunt's signature was firm and slanted, the S like a wave. In the photograph, and only in the photograph, something followed it.

A small round sun. Short rays. Drawn the way a child draws one.

Her throat did something she didn't authorize.

*Ghee and pencil shavings,* said Abernathy, very softly. *That sun smells like her. She signed that card twice, little light. Once for him to see. Once for you.*

Through the slats, Kino saw a dark grey Nissan at the curb two doors down, by the florist, nose pointed toward Colma. Not Bell's car. The driver's door was shut, the engine off. Nobody in the seat. Nobody on the sidewalk. Nobody walking away. The windows were clean and dry, and on a cold morning they weren't fogged at all. Above the right rear wheel, a thin line of dew on the paint ran perfectly straight.

"Instructions." Rodel wiped his palm on his guayabera as if it were still cold from Wednesday. "Mr. Hale called last night at nine-twelve, on the shop line. He said, *'If Ms. Sowri comes before I do, please tell her the box is open to her until seven-forty. She needn't sign anything. Only show identification.'*" He said *identification* exactly the way Hale would have, carefully, and seemed to hear himself do it. "And I should offer her coffee."

There was a fresh pot behind him. He didn't remember making it.

"I don't drink coffee," he said quietly. "I have tea."

7:31. Along the wall, the door of 112 stayed shut and clean. A thin warm shimmer rose from its keyhole, like heat over asphalt.

*Don't drink the coffee, little light,* Abernathy said. *It smells of nothing too. Same as that car.*

"Don't pour that, Mr. Panganiban." She didn't look at the carafe. "Not for me. Not for you. Pouring is how it gets accepted."

She turned the phone toward Kino, thumb over the grey rule and the sun.

"That sun isn't on the paper. Aunty's signature, and then a sun. Either she left it for me, or something is signing after her, and I don't know which." She breathed. "Hold that thought."

Back to Rodel, quieter. "Here's what I want. Not the box. Not Hale's identification. He says I needn't sign, only show ID, but showing ID is still walking in. So I won't. Write one more line in your own book, in your own slant, standing up. *Ms. Sowri came. Declined. Nothing accepted.* At seven-forty, open like always. If Hale comes, the box is empty of me. Can you do that?"

She took one full stride back from the brass.

"And his call, in your words, as exactly as you can. I'm betting he said it flat."

Kino stayed on the Nissan one breath longer, then stepped back from the glass.

"Nobody's in that car," he said, low. "And it isn't fogged. Whatever's parked there doesn't breathe. Is that the florist's lot, Rodel? Do you know it?"

He moved to Arenya's shoulder, between her and the gate, where he could see the alley door and the wall. From his pocket he took a pinch of Nana's salt and laid a thin line along the inside of the alley sill. He didn't make a show of it.

"Seven-thirty-one. We say our piece, write our line, walk out the side. Appa's nose-out. We don't wave early."

Rodel lifted the carafe off the warmer and poured the entire pot into the bucket under the counter, without looking at it. He didn't spill a drop. It didn't steam as it went down.

"Not the florist's," he told Kino, wiping his hands. "It's been there since before six. I saw it when I unlocked. Nobody got out."

He took a cheap blue ballpoint, and standing, leaning right, he wrote under the ruled pencil line in row 112:

*Fri 10/30 7:25 — Ms. Sowri came. Declined. Nothing accepted. — R.P.*

The ink went on wet and ordinary. As he lifted the pen, the shimmer over the keyhole thinned and stopped, like a stove somebody had turned off. Behind the brass door something small and stiff slid down. An envelope tipping over, maybe.

"The call." Rodel shut his eyes so he could get it right. "Nine-twelve. No hello. He said, *'Mr. Panganiban, this is Daniel Hale of Meridian Mutual. If Ms.'*" He paused. "And then your name, anak. Your whole name, every part, the way it's written on things. *'Comes before I do, please tell her the box is open to her until seven-forty. She needn't sign anything. Only identification. Offer her coffee. Thank you, that will be all.'*"

He opened his eyes.

"Flat. It wasn't polite like Wednesday. It was polite like a recording of polite. When he hung up, my phone said forty-one seconds."

He tapped the fresh ink twice, then stopped.

"Thirty-one years, and every line in this book has two hands on it. Mine and the renter's, or mine and a witness's. This one has only mine." He didn't push the pen toward anyone. He left it lying across the page. "I can let it stand alone. I don't know what that's worth, to whatever reads here."

At the alley door, the salt along the sill sat white and dry.

Through the front glass, the Nissan hadn't moved. But its passenger window was fogged now, on the inside, one slow breath's worth, from the bottom up.

7:34.

Arenya looked at the pen and didn't pick it up. Neither did Kino. She caught his eye so he'd know not to.

"Let it stand alone," she said. "A *no* doesn't need a second hand. It needs to be true, and it needs to stay where the book can see it. Aunty's no went in with one signature and a notary, and it's cut into a stone now. Yours is the same kind of thing. Nobody adds a name to it. Not me, not Kino. A witness gets entered with her household, and we can't pay that."

She closed the notebook. The extra weight in it was still there.

"Leave the pen where it is. Don't cap it. At seven-forty you open the gate, you say the box is open to no one, and you pour nothing for anyone. If Hale comes with that recording voice, give him your tea."

She backed toward the alley door, toward Kino's line, phone low.

"One last question. When a man writes through your hand, can you tell? A taste, a chill, anything? Because if you can, Wednesday's you might have wanted to know."

She glanced once at the fogged window. Not twice.

Kino's gaze stayed on it a moment longer. Then he turned his back on the glass and planted himself at the alley door, boots on either side of the salt.

"Rodel. Leave the pen. If anything asks for a second hand on that line, you say, 'Not today.' Out loud, in your own voice, and then you go make tea." He tipped his head at the window. "It's breathing now. That means it's listening. We go out the side. Arenya first, over the salt, not on it. I go last. Nobody looks back at the car, and nobody says a full name."

He pushed the steel door open with his foot, the way Rodel had, and waited.

Rodel considered her question honestly, the way he'd consider a fee.

"It feels like being helpful," he said at last. "That's how it gets in. On Wednesday I felt *useful.* I haven't felt useful since my mother." He looked down at the line in his own slant. "Next time I feel that useful, I'll make tea first."

He said *Not today* once, under his breath, practicing. Then he went to fill the kettle.

* * *

The alley was cold and smelled of wet cardboard and the florist's bins. Arenya stepped over the salt, not on it. Kino came last and let the steel door fall shut on its closer.

Appa's grey Corolla was where he'd promised, nose out, engine running, hazards off. A thermos of Amma's filter coffee sat on the passenger seat. He didn't offer it to anyone. He looked at Arenya's face, saw that her hands were empty, and let out a breath he had probably been holding since 7:25.

From the back seat, through the gap between the florist's van and a light pole, Mission Road was visible in a narrow strip.

At 7:40 exactly, by the dashboard clock, the driver's door of the grey Nissan opened.

Daniel Hale stepped out in his navy overcoat as if he'd only been sitting there. Nobody had been sitting there. He buttoned the coat. He walked into Colma Notary & Mail.

Ninety seconds later he came out holding a cream envelope by one corner. He didn't look at it.

On the sidewalk he stopped. He turned his pleasant face toward the gap between the van and the pole, toward the back seat of a car he shouldn't have been able to see, and nodded once. A courteous nod, the kind you give across a lobby.

Then he got in the Nissan and drove toward Colma. The fog on the passenger window did not clear.

Nobody in the Corolla said anything. Arenya found she was holding her own wrist.

Appa's phone buzzed in the cupholder. A text from the shop line, Rodel in capitals like a man who had learned texting late: **HE ASKED FOR TEA. I SAID NOT TODAY. BOX 112 CLOSED. NEW LOCK ORDERED.**

A second one followed. **PEN STILL THERE. NOBODY TOUCHED IT.**

"In order," Appa said, to nobody, and put the car in gear.

The sun was up properly now, low over the cemeteries.

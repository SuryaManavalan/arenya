By noon Appa was asleep sitting up on Nana's couch.

His right hand lay open on his knee. The grey stain on his thumb faced the ceiling, like he was showing it to someone. Nobody woke him.

Amma wouldn't put a shoe on her left foot.

She sat at the kitchen table with that foot propped on a folded dish towel, and every so often she pressed her sole flat against the linoleum, testing for a step that wasn't there.

"It's cold," she told Arenya once. "Not the floor. *Me*. From the ankle."

Nana fed everyone caldo and asked nothing. It was the most frightening thing she had done all week.

Arenya sat down on the kitchen floor in front of Amma's chair. The chair was the only thing in the room that wasn't shaking. She put both hands around the foot.

It was like holding a glass of water somebody had left out overnight.

"Tell me when it's warmer," she said. "Or when it's not. Either is data."

Amma almost laughed. "You and your data."

"It's all I've got, Amma."

She kept the notebook shut in her bag, and counted anyway. One: the landline in the hall, which rang once at 3:12 and stopped. Nobody moved. The pestle stayed where it was, on the form. Two: the pink transfer, which she only knew about because Kino texted it from a bus shelter in San Bruno. Three: Ava's text at 4:40. `croissant.` Then, a minute later: `oven light leaned south all afternoon. not toward the street. south. like it was watching something down the peninsula. probably nothing. probably not nothing.`

South of the Mission was the peninsula. The peninsula was *them*.

She typed back with her thumbs, her hands unwilling to leave the foot for long. `Thank you. Lock up early. Don't answer anything that rings. Salt your doorway if you have any. Table salt counts.`

Then she looked at Appa's open hand and made herself say it, quietly, to the room.

"Pink transfer. He's carrying their paper onto the bus. Whatever the 110 is, he's not walking on his own."

Nobody answered. Amma pressed the foot against her palms, as if to check that Arenya was still there.

*Oi, love,* said Abernathy, from the far end of the bond. He had spent the afternoon on the counter by the stove with his coat not quite moving. *Cold from the ankle, she said. Her, not the floor. I've been lookin' at that foot all day. Thread's slack, so it ain't pullin'. But slack ain't gone. Somethin' left a bit of itself in there, like a coat on a hook.*

She didn't look up. *Is that bad?*

*It's a fact. Keep your hands on it. And keep count of the rings. One at 3:12. They're checkin' the line's still answered by nobody. Polite lot.*

*The pink transfer?*

*Don't like it. Paper that don't exist no more, handed over anyway. Rules bendin' just for him.* A pause. *Somebody up the line wants him ridin'.*

She watched his edges flicker against the window over the sink. For half a second there were two of him, and the second one was standing very still.

*And tell your friend thanks. Tell her to eat. Have* you *eaten? Eat.*

*Abernathy.*

*...I keep thinkin' the 110 sounds familiar,* he said. *Don't ask me why, guv. I'd rather you didn't.*

She didn't.

* * *

The shoe rack at the San Bruno satsang was nearly empty when Kino crouched in front of it.

The hall smelled of tamarind rice and floor cleaner. Prasadam was over. Where the black oxfords had stood toes out, the bottom shelf held a clean rectangle in its grey dust. Two shoes' worth. Crisp-edged. As if they'd been lifted straight up.

He didn't touch it. He laid two fingers an inch above the dust and held them there, listening, the way Nana had taught him on the mountain, when listening meant keeping his mouth shut for an hour.

No scuff. No drag. Nobody walked out of a place like that.

"He didn't leave," he said. "He was called. Somebody up the peninsula wants their runner home before Sunday."

Malachi stood at the lamp table by the shrine door. Dalal's reading glasses lay folded there, the cord coiled around them like a small neat snake. Malachi picked them up by the cord without unwinding it and wrapped them in a clean handkerchief. Not his to keep. Not safe to leave where the next hand would be Dalal's.

"Keep those wrapped," Kino said. "Away from salt until Nana says. And don't put them on."

"I wasn't going to."

"Everybody says that."

Out on El Camino, an older woman with a wire shopping trolley waited under the bus shelter and was glad to talk to Malachi's face. People generally were, which Kino found interesting and had decided not to say.

"The polite one? Saturdays. The 110, down to Linda Mar and back. Always sits behind the driver." She lowered her voice as if it were gossip. "And he asks for a *transfer*. A paper one. They don't do paper anymore, I've told him. The driver gives him one anyway. Pink."

Kino took the handle of her trolley for a moment while she dug for her pass, a small courtesy, and read the number off the stop pole. The nearest one to the temple lot.

Malachi checked the timetable pane, then the street in both directions. "We don't board," he said, low. "We don't follow a man who counts exits onto a bus. We learn where he gets off, and we let Sunday come to us on Nana's side of the salt." To the woman, with something close to warmth: "Thank you, ma'am. If he comes tomorrow, don't talk to him about transfers."

"Why not?"

"He'll tell you it's the only kind that works."

She laughed. It wasn't a happy laugh, but she had the sense not to ask.

In the truck, Kino said, "Daly City. One pass on the way back. Slow. I want to see where the 110 turns around."

"Then Nana's."

"Before the porch light gets lonely."

He drove. Ix would have been on the seat between them if she'd come. He missed the weight of her, and kept his eyes on the road.

The 110 gave up where El Camino did, and became BART. Bay C sat under a concrete overhang that smelled of brake dust and wet cardboard. The bench was empty. In front of it, on the swept concrete, were two prints in grey dust, side by side, toes out, the way a man stands when he means to be first through the door.

Nobody else's feet had scuffed them. Somebody had stood in that exact spot often enough that the dust had learned it.

Malachi found the pencil mark behind the bench, on the posted timetable, without touching the glass. A single tick in faint grey graphite beside the first Sunday run toward the peninsula. And beside it: *8:12*.

He did the arithmetic out loud, because Kino had the wheel and needed to hear it. "Eight-twelve at Daly City. A Hillcrest stop by eight-forty. A walk up the hill to a gas meter, with time to spare before nine."

"He's not hiding it."

"No," Malachi said. "He's expected."

The handkerchief in his coat pocket weighed about what a pair of reading glasses weighs. He kept a hand on it the rest of the way up the hill.

* * *

Just after five, the foot gave.

It was small, like a knot slipping. Amma said "*Oh*," not frightened, surprised, the way you say it when a stair turns out to be there after all.

The cold went into Arenya's hands.

It came up through her palms to the wrists. It was patient and complete and not unkind, and it lasted three heartbeats. She counted them, because that was what she did. *One.* Her mother's eyes on her face. *Two.* Abernathy, very quietly, taking his hat off, or the idea of one. *Three.*

Then it was in neither of them.

The foot was just a foot. A little chilly. Amma flexed the toes, and then did it again, and then put her sock on, and then her shoe, and then looked at the shoe on her own foot for a long moment as if it belonged to a stranger who had been very kind to her.

"Kanna," she said. "It's gone."

"I know."

Across the room, Appa slept on with his grey thumb toward the ceiling.

At the stove, Nana didn't turn around. "Wash your hands with salt before you eat, mija."

Arenya did. The salt was coarse and grey and it stung a little in a hangnail she hadn't known she had. Under the tap, she looked at her right palm: the frost mark, the rule, the two dark strokes. Cool, no pull. Her left was the one she watched now.

It stayed quiet. That was suspicious too.

* * *

The porch belonged to Caleb, and he ran it like a loading dock.

Who came. Who left. Who stopped too long. Around four the block filled with costumes and he counted them from the glider with his back to the wall: a dinosaur, two Elsas, a boy dressed as a mailman, satchel and all.

He didn't like the mailman.

Then he did, because the boy's mother was right behind him and the shoes were sneakers. He let his shoulders drop one inch for the kid. It surprised him that they went.

The scar under his hair stayed cool all afternoon. He noticed and didn't trust it. Quiet was how Shepherd had waited last night.

When the hall phone rang once, at 3:12, he didn't move, and neither did anyone behind the screen. Afterward he said, to the street and to Appa asleep inside on the couch, "He rides the bus, Appa. Pink transfer. I never saw that on my side." He thought about it. "Whoever he is, he's lower than us. They don't send the good ones to stand at curbs."

It didn't make him feel better. It made him feel accurate. He was getting used to the difference.

Near dusk he got up and walked the salt once on the inside without crossing it, running the flat of his hand along each sill. Then he sat back down facing the street.

"Porch is mine tonight," he told the empty walk. "Anyone wants sleep, take it. I'll say out loud if the scar moves."

Nobody answered, which he'd expected. Inside, someone turned a tap off.

* * *

At Tartine, Ava closed the till at 4:40 and counted it twice, because her hands wanted a job.

The oven light was leaning south. She wrote it down anyway. *4:38 p.m., Sat., light bias S, ~15°, no draft, no door open.* She put next to it the question mark she put next to everything lately.

Then she did what a person with a chemistry-lab-grade fear of her own perception does. She took the salt shaker off the counter, and poured a thin line across the back-room threshold, and another across the front-door sill. Table salt. Embarrassing. She laid her left palm flat over the oven glass for a count of ten. Clear. Ordinary-warm. She didn't trust that either.

She texted Arenya: `salted. not answering anything that rings. if the oven points at anything other than south I'm calling you, and I'm sorry in advance for the call.`

She turned the sign to CLOSED, pulled the blind, and sat on an upturned milk crate facing the oven with her phone on the counter and a pen behind her ear.

"Okay," she told the oven, in her case-report voice. "Observation continues. You first."

At 5:58 the streetlights came on along Guerrero.

In the oven glass, the small light that had leaned south all afternoon swung once. Southwest. Toward the coast. Toward Pacifica.

Ava stopped breathing. She could tell, in the clinical corner of her mind, that she'd stopped. *Subject holds breath, 3–4 sec.*

It stayed there for exactly the length of the held breath.

Then it settled back to south.

She picked up the phone. Put it down. Wrote *5:58, SW, ~1 breath, then S*, and underlined *SW* twice, and did not call, because it hadn't pointed at anything other than south for long enough to be a call.

Probably.

* * *

By dusk the porch light came on by itself, on its timer, and the trick-or-treaters thinned out to a few stragglers dragging half-empty pillowcases.

At 6:20 Kino's truck came up the hill and parked at the curb, carefully clear of the meter.

Arenya was at the front window with her hands still smelling of salt. She watched Malachi get out of the passenger side. He didn't come up the walk. He stopped at Nana's gate, outside the line, his hat held in both hands, and stood there.

He looked like a man at a door he knew the rules for.

Behind her, her mother's chair scraped. Amma got up, on two warm feet.

"He is waiting to be asked," she said.

"Yes."

"Then we should not leave him standing."

Arenya looked at the dark shape at the gate. *A man standing outside is still standing somewhere,* Nana had said. She hadn't known then whose line that was going to be.

On the porch, Caleb stood up from the glider without being asked, and held the screen door open.

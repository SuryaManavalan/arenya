Appa took the exit without asking anyone.

At the house the porch light was still on from the night before, and Amma's chappals were still lined up by the door. He emptied the thermos into the sink, rinsed it twice, and set it upside down on the rack beside her steel tumbler. He said nothing about either one. By noon he was asleep in his chair with the TV muted on a cricket replay and his reading glasses pushed up on his forehead.

Kino took the couch for an hour and didn't sleep. Around two he said he should check on Nana. At the door he stopped long enough to look at Arenya's left hand, the warm one.

"Tonight," he said. "Before someone with a pen."

Then he was gone, boots laced, toward the bus.

At 4:12 her work email received a message she wasn't copied on. It reached her anyway. Teddy forwarded it with no comment except his corgi-gif signature.

*From: Records Compliance, SF Dept. of Public Health. Re: Access verification, "Urban Mortality Patterns – SF 2025." Our annual audit flagged a footage request under this project. Please confirm the requesting party's authorization and the project's IRB status by Monday, Nov 2, 5:00 p.m. Unconfirmed requests are referred.*

Two minutes later, a text. **renny do u know what this is?? im at the office til 8, deadline hell. not a big deal prob 👉👉**

She read it standing in the hallway between Amma's chappals and the muted cricket. Palm warm. Nobody had written anything yet.

*Yet* was doing a lot of work.

She left Appa his glasses and put a note on the thermos rack: *Going to the city to fix something. Home by tomorrow. Eat the rasam. — A.* She texted Teddy back with steady thumbs. **Not a big deal, but I need to tell you in person. Don't answer the DPH email. Don't forward it. Be there by 6:30. Please stay.**

On the 5:00 BART she opened the notebook on her knee and wrote the sentence she owed him before she could talk herself out of it.

*I cloned your keycard. I made up the project. Footage request is mine. His name, my hand.*

The line came out dark and pressed. The palm stayed warm.

*Oi,* said the bond, from the seat beside her, where a shape in a shabby coat flickered like a bad bulb. *Wrote it first. Proud of you, love. Don't tell anyone I said so. When you get to "Teddy," then "I," don't dress it up. Angry's allowed. Anger's just a man being honest at you.* A pause, and the voice slid half a word toward something older. *And eat something.*

She didn't eat. At Saiesh's stop she almost got off. She texted him instead, **I'm telling Teddy tonight. Whatever he does to me, you were never on the paper. Talk after?** Then she rode on with Nana's salt in her pocket, practicing the first word, which was *Teddy*, and the second, which was *I*.

* * *

Serramonte Auto at 3:40 smelled of carpet shampoo and burnt coffee.

Malachi had bought a prepaid phone for cash at a pharmacy on Mission and put nothing of his own in it. Now he stood in a rumpled coat in front of a desk, a man shopping for a used Camry. He didn't show a badge. He asked the sales manager one dull question: who had bought a beige '09 with the frame on it, plate beginning 7K, and would he like a finder's fee for a name he'd give a stranger anyway.

The sales manager was Benny, with a gold chain and a lanyard. He took the twenty before the question was finished.

"Beige '09? Cash buyer. A lady. Asian. Didn't get a name."

Malachi watched his hands. The right one went flat on the desk phone as he said *lady* and stayed there. It didn't lift until Malachi was back out on the lot. Through the showroom glass he watched Benny dial without looking at the keypad, a number his thumb already knew. Whoever it was answered before the second ring. Benny said three words and hung up.

He didn't look out the window. That told Malachi more than if he had.

He took nothing from the desk. He took the way the hand had gone flat, and the three words he couldn't hear, and carried them north.

* * *

By dusk, the Excelsior kitchen smelled of copal and onions.

Kino washed his hands at Nana's sink before he touched anything, the way she'd taught him, and checked the salt line under the door with two fingers. Unbroken. Ix lay across the threshold with his eyes half open, watching Kino and not the street. Kino kissed Nana's forehead and sat across from her.

He told her in Spanish, low, with his hands flat on the oilcloth where she could see them. Whose Spanish did Bell count in? And the Lady had had her back to him for weeks. He should have said so sooner.

"Don't tell me it's nothing," he said. "Tell me what you'd do."

Nana listened with both hands around a cup of canela and didn't interrupt. When he finished she was quiet long enough for the pot to tick.

"He counts in whatever language the paper is written in, mijo. Here the paper is English. Mine wasn't, so he had to come to my kitchen to read it." She reached across and turned his hands palm up. "The Lady. When a woman turns her back on you in the night, you ask what she is facing. You never looked past her. Tonight you sleep here, and you look."

He ate the rice and black beans she put in front of him, because refusing would have been an insult. Then he took her blanket to the floor of the front room, boots off, head toward the salt. Ix settled against his back, warm and heavy.

He didn't try to sleep. He did what he'd done on the mountain since he was twelve: slowed the breath, let the ceiling go, and asked. Not *why did you leave me*. He asked, *what are you looking at, Lady?*

Somewhere across the city Arenya was saying the hard thing to someone. He thought of her warm left hand and held his place.

* * *

The floor was mostly dark. Teddy sat at his desk with his coat on and his corgi mug full of cold tea, the DPH email open and unanswered on his screen.

He'd stayed.

He turned his chair when the elevator dinged and gave her a crooked half-smile that didn't quite get where he wanted it. "You said 'not a big deal' and 'please stay' in the same text. That's the scariest text anyone's ever sent me." He pushed the second chair toward her with his foot. "Okay. I'm here."

She sat down without taking her coat off. They looked like two people waiting for a bus that wasn't coming. She put the notebook on the desk, open to the page, and her left hand flat beside it.

"Teddy." First word. "I did it."

That was the second word, and then the rest came before she could rephrase it into a listicle.

"The DPH email is mine. There's no Urban Mortality Patterns project. I made it up. In September I cloned your keycard, got into the vendor portal as you, and requested the Tartine footage from October under your credentials. Your name, my hand." She turned the notebook so he could read it. The line was still darker than the ink should have been. "I wrote it on the train so I couldn't make it sound better here. I was going to make it sound better. I'm really good at that. It's literally my job, which you know, because you pay me for it."

She made herself look at him and not the mug. "I did it because people are dying. Nine of them since August, the same way, and nobody was looking, and I wanted to be the one who looked. That's the reason. It's not an excuse. It was your name on it. If this gets referred, it lands on you first, and you didn't know. So it shouldn't land on you."

Her voice went clipped. She let it.

"I'll write to DPH myself, tonight or Monday, whatever you and legal want. No authorization, no IRB, the project's fake, you had no knowledge. I'll sign that one. It's the only thing I'm signing." She stopped. Then, because it was true: "Someone did this exact thing to me this month. My name, their hand. I found out what it feels like from the other side. I didn't want you finding out from an auditor."

She took her hands off the desk and put them in her lap.

"That's all of it. Except that I'm sorry. Which comes last, because it's the cheapest part."

Teddy said nothing for a long time. He read the line twice, his lips moving slightly on *his name, my hand*, then set the notebook down square with the desk edge, the way he did with contracts. When he looked up the crooked smile was gone.

"You used my *badge*." It came out quiet, which was worse than loud. "I defended that email. I told legal it had to be clerical, because Teddy doesn't do that, because I'm the guy who reads the terms of service." He breathed through his nose. "Nine people?"

He didn't wait. He held out his hand, palm up. "Your keycard. Not forever. Until Monday. You don't come into this building on anything with a magnetic stripe until legal and I are sitting next to you. And you don't write that letter tonight, alone, at midnight. That's how you got here." His jaw worked. "We write it together. My name's on it too, as the guy who didn't know. That's the truth, so it goes in."

She slid the real keycard out of her wallet, the one HR had given her, and laid it in his palm. "Monday. Fair. And together. Yes. I don't deserve the 'together,' but I'll take it."

He closed his hand around it and put it in his shirt pocket behind the pen he always kept there. He saw her see the pen. He took that out too and laid it on the far side of the desk.

Then he turned his monitor toward her. Under the DPH email sat a pink while-you-were-out slip, photographed and forwarded from reception at 4:12.

*Caller for Theodore re: footage request. Asked if he had "signed for it yet." Said he'd "wait for the signature." No name.*

The callback was a 650 number. She knew it. It was Rodel's shop line, borrowed again.

"I didn't call it back," Teddy said. "It felt like the kind of number you don't call back." He looked at her lap, at her hand. "So. Who's waiting for my signature, Arenya?"

Her palm stayed warm. It didn't tick, which was somehow worse.

"That's Rodel's line," she said. "A notary's shop in Colma. A man borrowed it to put on a form with my name on it, and he's been borrowing it since." She tapped the screen, not the number, the words. "'Signed for it yet.' He's not an auditor. He wants you to sign something saying the request was authorized. Your name, his hand. Same trick, only now he's after yours. Don't sign anything. Not the DPH confirmation, not a 'verification' form, not a receipt, not a courtesy acknowledgement. Not for me, not for him, not for legal until legal's read it twice. If someone hands you a pen, you say you'll get back to them."

"Who's waiting?"

"I don't have a face. I have a name that isn't his, a shirt from a temple in Fremont, and cold hands." She tried the half-smile. It landed about as well as his had. "I can't explain the rest without sounding insane, and I'd rather not lose you tonight for sounding insane. Can I tell you the true parts and let you decide how much to believe? Starting with: it's not a prank, and I'm not in danger from you."

Teddy leaned back. "True parts. I'll decide how much I believe. That's a fair deal." He gestured. "Start wherever it starts."

She got as far as *My cousin Susmithaa died on Monday.*

The desk phone lit.

* * *

Across the city, in a doorway, Caleb Strand stood with his back to a closed laundromat and his hands out of his pockets.

He'd gone to the motel's edge of the bed that evening and said it out loud to the room. *My name is Caleb Strand. I was in the grey jacket.* It came out flat. He tried again and it came out worse, which meant it was real. Both Vesper packs stayed on the nightstand, unopened. The canister rode against his ribs, ticking, and the folded thread on his forearm pulled northwest, steady as a needle.

At 6:28 it had yanked once, hard, like someone at the far end taking up slack. He'd been on his feet before he decided anything. *Not like this,* he'd said to the empty room, and gone anyway: a ball cap from the lost-and-found box, no grey, Muni instead of the car, off six blocks short. He walked the last of it the way he'd been trained. Parallel street. Fogged glass on the parked cars. Rooflines. Never the same side twice.

Now he put his eyes on two people's hands, as he'd been taught.

A man on a bench across from the café front, holding a full paper cup so still it could have been set in concrete. A professional's hands. And in the café door, a woman with her left hand pinned flat to her thigh like she was guarding a burn.

At 6:52 the thread on his forearm went slack.

Not faded. Slack, the way a line goes when whoever's pulling has arrived.

He stopped breathing for a second. Slack meant the other end was *here*, or that something had cut it. He couldn't tell which, and hated that he couldn't. He ran the street again, looking for what was wrong with the picture. Neither face was the one off the wall. Neither was Shepherd's. Nobody else on the street was looking at them, and that was wrong too.

He took the canister out just far enough to read by streetlight. Still ticking, still green. He tucked it away. Then he did what he'd told the motel room he wouldn't, and stepped to the edge of the doorway where the light touched his boots, and kept both hands open at his sides.

* * *

Malachi didn't turn his head. He used the Tartine window: a ball cap in the laundromat doorway across the way, a man standing the way Malachi had been taught to stand, hands out of his pockets and shaking where they could be seen.

Unarmed, then. Or wanting to look it.

"Ava." He spoke low, eyes on the street. "Go in now, and don't look behind you. If he crosses to this side, you don't serve him and you don't speak first." He set the coffee down untouched. She had brought it out at ten past six, black, no lid, along with a speech about house policy, a salt twist in her apron, and the correct description of a cardigan. He had let her have the last word on the cardigan.

She didn't go in.

"Counter-proposal," Ava said, from the café door, not loud. "I stay here, on camera, phone in hand, manager forty feet away. You two do the face thing. If that man starts a sentence with my name, I'm going inside and calling the police. I know how that sounds from a woman who's watched you refuse to use a phone." She laid a short crooked line of Kino's salt across the threshold and stood behind it. "Nobody's pouring anybody anything tonight. But he's shaking, Malachi. Bell doesn't shake. Write that down for whatever you write things down for."

Malachi stood slowly. He crossed to the middle of the sidewalk and no farther, and lifted his chin once toward the dark. *Come out, or I come to you.*

"Whoever you are," he said, at the pitch he'd use across a dinner table, "you've been standing there longer than I've been sitting here. I'm not here to hurt anyone. I'd like to see your face before the next person does."

The man stepped into the light.

Young. A lost-and-found cap, a jacket zipped to the chin. Malachi had never seen the face. The stance he knew.

* * *

The canister sped up.

*Tick-tick-tick.* It wasn't aimed at the man standing in the middle of the sidewalk. It was aimed past him. At the café door. At the barista behind her crooked salt, and at the hand she was holding to her thigh.

Ava felt it come across the street. The cold spot in her palm, two centimetres, unchanged for days, went from cold to *aching* in about four seconds, as if something over there had just called it by name without saying a word.

On the whole block, nobody else looked up.

"It's not ticking at you," she said, flat, case-report voice. "It's ticking at me. Palm: cold to aching, four seconds, no external stimulus except him. Whatever he's carrying, it's reading my hand like a name tag." She stepped back one pace, behind the door's lip, and held up her left palm, spread. "For the record, I'm not afraid of you. I'm afraid of the thing in your jacket. Name first, then the canister. Please. I've had a very long week."

Malachi took two steps sideways until he stood in the line between them, coat hanging loose to show there was nothing under it. "Stop there. Who taught you to walk a parallel street? And who gave you the thing in your jacket?" He did not look at Ava. "If that ache gets worse, tell me out loud."

"Caleb."

It came out too loud, like a word he hadn't used in a while, and he winced and brought it down. "That's my name. You wanted the face first. It isn't much of one."

He stopped at the curb and didn't step off it. His right hand went flat to his ribs, pressing, the way you press a wound. "I'm not here for you." He said it to Malachi, but his eyes had already gone past him. "Ma'am. Your left hand. It just got worse, didn't it? Something called it."

A pause, too long. Fourteen months of nobody needing him to finish sentences.

"I'm carrying something that belongs to a dead woman. It started counting the second I came into the light, and it's counting at *you*. So don't come any closer. And don't let anybody touch that hand tonight who asks nice."

His eyes slid once, without his meaning them to, to the corner table inside the glass. He dragged them off it.

Ava saw. So did Malachi. It was the table she had wiped twice a shift since Wednesday, the one nobody sat at. The camera over the register pointed straight at it, the same camera whose October footage someone had wanted badly enough to clone a keycard.

* * *

**Teddy's desk phone ringing.** It hadn't rung, at first. The line-one light went amber, and the little screen filled in by itself: **650**, then Rodel's number, one digit at a time, like someone dialing slowly. Then it rang, the old office ring, two short.

Teddy didn't move.

On the second ring, Arenya's warm palm **pulsed**. A single beat, in time with the bell.

She stopped mid-word. Abernathy had told her to, so she did, and she said it out loud instead of swallowing it. "My hand just ticked. On the second ring. Teddy, that's the number I was telling you about, and it dialed itself."

She didn't reach for the receiver. She put her left palm flat on the desk between them, warm and counting, and kept her right hand off anything that held a pen. "Don't pick it up. Don't say your name into it. Don't say 'yes,' don't say 'speaking,' and if it goes to voicemail, don't play it back tonight." She pulled the notebook over and wrote while it rang.

*6:58. Rodel's number dialed itself on Teddy's line. Nobody here is signing.*

The line came out dark and pressed, pushed up from the other side of the page.

"Let it ring out," she said, quieter. "I'm right here. You're not alone with it. If you want to hold something, hold the pen. Just don't give it to anyone."

Teddy held his pen in his fist like a kid holding a crayon and didn't blink. The phone rang eleven times. On the twelfth it stopped, and the amber light went red.

*1 new message.* The duration filled in on the little screen: **0:41**.

She knew that number. It was the length of the call on the flip phone in the temple hall.

Teddy looked at the red light for a long moment. Then he leaned down and pulled the cord out of the wall jack, neatly, the way he'd unplug a kettle.

"I'm not playing that," he said. "Not tonight, and not alone." His voice wobbled once and steadied. "Phones don't dial themselves, Arenya. I'm an analyst. I know that." He set the pen down in front of *her*, not himself. "So keep talking. I'm deciding."

She looked at it and didn't touch it. She slid it back across the desk with one finger until it sat square with the edge, his way. "Not mine to hold tonight. Thanks for the vote of confidence, though."

In her bag, her cell lit. *Kino.* She let it ring out. "That's a friend. The only person who's told me the truth all week. He'd tell you to keep the phone unplugged."

Then she gave it to him in order, plain, like a timeline for an editor. Monday, Susmithaa, a car, a lot, "aneurysm." Nine since August: a freeze of under a minute, staring at someone, then the fall. In March, someone had used her name to buy a paid prayer at a temple in Livermore, in her star and her family's line, and that put her in a book as the one who was insured.

"Susmithaa was the one on the policy. I think the prayer is what made her go." Her voice stayed level, which was a miracle. "There's a man who writes in people's names. Ruler-straight handwriting. He borrows numbers. He doesn't threaten. He asks you to *confirm*, and then you've said yes to something you didn't read." She pointed at the dead phone and its dangling cord. "That was him asking you."

Teddy listened to the whole thing the way he listened to a quarterly deck he suspected was cooked. When she finished, he opened his drawer, put the pen inside, and locked it.

"I believe you're scared. I believe that phone. I believe you'd have let me take the fall for a week if you thought it kept me out of this, and I'm angrier about that than about the badge." He rubbed his eyes under his glasses. "We listen to the voicemail Monday, with legal, and somebody who isn't either of us. Tonight I'm walking you to BART, and you're calling your friend back." A pause. "And Arenya. I'm not signing anything. For anyone. I want that in your notebook too."

She opened the notebook and wrote it under the 6:58 line, where he could watch the pen move. *Teddy: not signing anything. For anyone.* The ink came up dark.

"Done," she said. "In the book."

"Angrier about the fall than the badge. Fair. I'll earn that back. Slowly, and badly, probably."

She played Kino's voicemail with the phone against her ear and her hand cupped over it. Nineteen seconds. *The Lady never turned from me. She's standing between me and a line that counts my steps. Something at the far end felt me look. Don't sign anything tonight. I'm coming to the city.*

Her stomach dropped. She texted with her thumbs. **Heard you. Teddy didn't sign, I didn't. Mission & 18th if you can. I think something's happening at Tartine. Don't go alone.**

* * *

Kino had woken on Nana's floor at 7:04 with Ix's teeth bared at the front door and the cold already draining out of his sternum.

He remembered all of it. The mountain path in the hour before light. The Lady with her back to him, as she'd been for weeks. This time he hadn't looked at her shoulders. He'd looked past them.

She was facing down the path, the way he'd come up. Behind his own footprints ran a thin grey line, perfectly straight and ruled, following every step he'd taken. At each print there was a tiny tick mark. It was counting. It stopped a few paces short of her heels, because she was standing on it.

She had never turned her back on him. She'd been standing between him and the line.

His sternum, where the patch was, had gone cold for the space of one breath, as if something at the far end had felt him look. He hadn't flinched. He'd held still the way you hold still for a deer, and counted the ticks behind his footprints, and said silently in Nana's Spanish: *Entonces estamos de pie en la misma línea.* Then we are standing on the same line.

He didn't step over it. He didn't try to scuff it out. He'd only have been writing on it. He laid his open palm in the dirt beside her foot, the way you put a hand down for a dog to smell. *I see it. I'm here. I won't walk it for him.*

And the Lady had not turned. But her right hand had moved behind her back and opened, palm down, a handspan above his. She didn't touch him. She held it there the way a mother keeps a hand back without looking while she watches a road. Down the path, the tick marks stopped multiplying. The line wasn't erased. It was paused, like a pen lifted off a page by someone who has realized he's being watched.

His hand was still warm, as if it had really been held over.

Nana stood in the kitchen doorway with the salt jar already in her hand. "You looked," she said. It wasn't a question.

"I looked." He sat up. "She's facing the walk, Nana. She's standing on it."

Nana nodded once and began pouring salt along the sill a second time.

He called Arenya and it rang through, and he left the message short, no names that could be written. Then he put on his boots and his black hoodie, took the salt jar and a pinch of copal in a twist of paper, and told Ix to stay with her. Ix didn't like it.

He stayed.

* * *

Caleb stood on his curb.

"Army taught me the walk," he said. "Two tours, logistics. After that a contract outfit taught me everything else. I'm not proud of it."

He took his hand off the zip very slowly, the way you draw down on nobody, and opened the jacket two inches to show there was no holster. Then he lifted out the canister by its base with two fingers. A green steel thing the size of a thermos cap, ticking like a pulse that had lost its patience. He held it low and away from the café.

"I'm not setting it on the pavement, and I'm not kicking it. Sorry, ma'am. I mean that. It's ash. It's a woman's ash, and she's been put on enough floors." His voice cracked on *floors*, and he waited it out. "It's not a weapon. I think it's *her*. And it's reading your hand because the people who built it were reading yours. I don't know why yet."

Malachi's voice stayed level. "The table. Why did you look at it? Did you sit there? Were you the grey jacket?"

"I was on the team that did Tartine. I looked at that table through that glass in the light grey jacket I'm not wearing." He made himself finish. "And Monday, I was on the team that took Susmithaa Sowri. I want to say that to her niece with my own mouth before anyone else says it for me. I'm not asking for mercy. I'm asking for ten minutes before you decide what to do with me."

He looked at Ava. "That table. Nobody sits there because it's wrong, isn't it? It's still counting."

Ava put her right hand on the doorframe, because her knees had done something clinical.

"Yes," she said. Her voice was very even, which was how she knew it wasn't. "Nobody sits there. I've wiped it twice a shift and I couldn't tell you why. Observation, not theory: the oven light leans away from it." She breathed. "The camera over the register is recording. Motion-triggered, twelve-hour overwrite, the manager checks it Mondays. If anyone wants October off it, someone already asked. A man in a grey cardigan, though not out loud."

She looked at the shaking hands. The ash held low like a sleeping animal. She wanted to be angry. What came out was smaller.

"I watched a man die five feet from my steam wand because of that table. You were looking at it through this glass. I'm not going to tell you that's fine. Ten minutes. Not on this sidewalk, not near the window, and not in front of me alone. Malachi stays. Arenya's family is going to want your mouth, not mine. But I want to know what you saw before the paramedics came. Start with the four men's faces."

Malachi walked them out of the window's eye: around the corner, into the alley beside the bins, behind the café's back door, away from the register camera. Ava kept the doorway, the salt, and one coworker in earshot until the last moment, then followed. Caleb held the curb's edge all the way, like the ground was a tightrope.

Once the corner table was out of sight, the canister didn't stop. But it slowed. It went back to a patient tick, its own rhythm and not Ava's. The ache in her palm dropped to plain cold. Two centimetres. Unchanged.

Under the one sodium light, Caleb put his back to the brick so nobody had to ask him to stay put. He left his hands open and let them shake.

"The table first," he said. "Yes. I was the grey jacket. I sat there with a coffee I didn't drink and a phone I didn't use. I don't remember choosing to sit. I remember my hands doing it." To Ava: "You were behind the steam wand. I remember the noise. That's all I can give you of you, and I'm sorry."

Malachi had stopped at the mouth of the alley with his back to the brick, leaving Ava the better light and Caleb the worse. He did not fold his arms. "Four faces," he said. "In the order they sat. What each did with his hands. Not what you were told to see. What you saw. If you reach a part you don't remember, say *I don't remember.* Don't fill it. A gap I can work with. An invention I can't."

Caleb took a breath that didn't go all the way down.

"Four of us. Callsigns, not names. I was One. Lead. I'm not going to hide behind a number. Two, Dorsey: heavy, fair, a lazy left eye he used to cover with a ball cap. Three, Pruitt: skinny, nervous, chews the inside of his cheek. Four, Hale." He shook his head once. "Not the Hale at the notary. That's a name he rents. The real one has a neck that turns *wrong*, all at once, like a camera on a mount. If you see that, don't count his steps and don't say yours."

His throat worked. "We weren't told who he was. We were given a face and a window. Shepherd said a time. Yuri Melnikov was a name on a list, and I didn't know his name until the news. I knew his *coat*."

Ava, thumbs steady, was typing it into her phone as he said it. *Dorsey, left eye, ball cap. Pruitt, cheek. Four, the neck. One, the lead, who sat down without choosing.* "I'm writing it so it exists somewhere that isn't your head or mine," she said. "Timestamp 7:14 p.m." She didn't look up. "I'm not going to forgive you in an alley. I want that clear. But you said the coat. A navy scarf. I know it too. That's the first true thing anyone from your side has said to me, and I'm going to hold onto it while I stay angry."

Caleb lifted his hand, palm out, to show he wasn't touching the canister. "The Crown in my head went dark Monday night. I'm awake for the first time in fourteen months, and the first thing I did awake was run with her ash. I've got the Vesper that would put me back under, on a nightstand. I haven't taken it." He looked at Malachi. "Take me to her niece. Tonight, tomorrow, wherever she'll stand. I'll say it all again, in order. I'll give you Drop Two and what I know of Shepherd in the same breath. All I ask is that nobody lies to her about what I am."

"She's on her way here," Ava said. "Not in the café. My shift ends at nine. There's a back room with a door that locks and a camera that doesn't point in. Hot tea. No pouring for anyone who isn't us." And quieter, to Caleb: "You don't touch the Vesper tonight. If your hands shake, that means you're awake. I study people who'd give anything to be awake."

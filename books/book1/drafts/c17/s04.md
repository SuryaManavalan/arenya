Friday, 8:58 p.m., and Tartine was a different animal with the lights off.

The chairs were up on the tables, legs in the air like a field of dead insects. One bulb burned behind the pass. Ava let them in through the alley with the steel door propped on a sack of flour, and she didn't say hello.

The back room smelled of bleach and old starter. A mop sink. Four milk crates. A steel shelf of cambro tubs. A camera over the walk-in with a red light that did not blink.

Malachi leaned on the walk-in door with his arms folded and his eyes on the alley. Ava sat on a crate with her notebook open to a page headed **7:14**, her left hand flat on her knee, palm up, as if it might report something.

Caleb Strand sat on the crate farthest from the door, back to the wall, jacket zipped to the throat. The duffel stood between his boots. Through the canvas, small and dry and regular, something ticked.

When Arenya stepped over the threshold, the ticking skipped once.

Then it carried on.

"You're the daughter," Caleb said. It wasn't a question. "I'll say it once, start to finish. Then you can do what you want with me." He nodded at the empty crates. "Sit or don't."

She took the crate nearest the door, not the one nearest him. Kino took the one beside hers without being asked, a jar of salt set on the concrete between his boots, lid loose. She laid the notebook on her knee, closed, and her hand on the cover.

"Arenya," she said. "Not 'the daughter.' She's the one you carried a canister for." She opened to a clean page and uncapped her pen. "Nobody signs, nobody swears, nobody says a name that isn't theirs. If my palm stops mid-sentence, I'll say so out loud, and you stop too. Deal?"

"Deal." He said it to the wall first. Then he made himself say it to her, and made himself keep looking.

"Start with Monday," she said. "Where she was, and what she was holding."

* * *

"Daly City. A little before three. She was at the sink with her back to the window, holding a mug. Blue. Chipped at the rim. She wasn't afraid yet. She was thinking about something else."

His hands were shaking on his knees. He pressed them flat.

"I remember the mug. I don't remember deciding. I remember *doing*. Three of us went in and I was the lead. And I folded her tether, so they couldn't follow it to you. That's the one thing I did on purpose, and I did it while the rest of it was happening. It isn't an excuse."

Arenya's pen had stopped on the word *tether*. She wrote it down anyway and underlined it once.

Her palm had been warm through *Daly City* and *the sink* and *her back to the window*. On *blue, chipped at the rim* it gave one slow pulse, the same as on Rodel's second ring. Not cold. Not a stop. Something leaning closer to listen. On *I folded her tether* it went hot for a breath, sharp as a stove grate, and settled.

"Stop a second," she said. "I said I'd tell you. Slow pulse on the mug, like it was leaning in. Hot on 'tether,' one breath. Not a warning. More like you touched a stove I'm standing next to." She wrote both down, with the times. "Was the mug hers, or did somebody put it in her hand? And when you folded the tether, where did it go?"

"Hot on *tether*," Kino said, low, his eyes on Caleb's hands. "That's the one that matters. Folding a line takes knowing where it ends. So you knew, Caleb. Even without the name."

Caleb let the pause finish.

"Hers. There was a sticker on the bottom, half peeled. A cartoon bird. I saw it when it fell. Nobody put it in her hand." The duffel gave one beat against his boot. "I knew where it ran. It ran to you. I didn't fold it *to* anything. I folded it away, back on itself, like creasing paper until it won't open. In training they call it a knot. Nothing went down it after." He looked at her, not the page. "Nobody ordered it. That's mine. Shepherd would have my head, and he's looking."

Arenya wrote *cartoon bird, half-peeled* and her handwriting was worse than she'd have liked.

"The sticker was mine," she said. "College. She borrowed that mug every visit." She stopped. Breathed. "That goes on the page, and I'm not doing anything else with it tonight."

She didn't say what the other thing would have been.

"Finish the kitchen. Don't skip what happened to her."

He shut his eyes for exactly one second. Then he opened them, because she'd asked.

His voice went flat, the old way. He let it. It was the only way he was going to get through, and it wasn't hiding.

"Dorsey and Pruitt held the door. I went to the kitchen. She turned around and saw me. She said, 'Can I help you?' Like I was lost. Like I'd come to the wrong house."

His jaw worked.

"You don't need to touch the body for a lift. I reached for the astral. It comes loose like a coat off a hook, and the Crown does the pulling. She was warm all the way through. She knew, then. She looked at me and said a name. It wasn't mine." The canister had gone dead still. "The body went down. The mug dropped. Dorsey said *clean*. The Crown said *yield confirmed*. And I felt good."

The word came out of him like something he'd choked on.

"I felt *good*, Arenya. Fourteen months of nothing, and for four seconds I felt like a man who'd done his job. That's the part I'll never get out. Then I knotted the line before the feeling could tell me not to. That was the last thing I chose. The Crown took the rest, and I let it, and I'd have done it again Tuesday."

His hands were shaking so hard he pushed them under his thighs. He asked for nothing. He waited.

Arenya set the pen down on the notebook very carefully, like it might spill.

"Okay," she said. It came out small, and she let it. Kino's shoulder was close enough that she didn't have to fall into it, and that was enough.

"I'm not forgiving you. I want that on the page next to the bird." She swallowed. "But you said 'felt good' when you didn't have to. That's the second true thing from your side tonight. Ava has the first."

She lifted her eyes.

"The name. Exactly as she said it. If you don't have it, say so, and I'll take the gap."

He opened his mouth to give it to her, and nothing was there.

The fluorescent tube hummed over the mop sink. He stayed inside the silence rather than fill it.

"I don't have it," he said finally. "I'd be making it up. Malachi said don't fill a gap, so I'm not." He pulled one hand out and looked at it as if it might know. "She didn't say it to me. She looked past me. At the window, toward the city. Soft, the way you'd call someone in from another room. Two sounds, maybe. Not a question. She was telling someone something."

His throat moved.

"And the Crown heard it. I didn't, not properly. It did. There was a tag, right after *yield confirmed*. *Proximal reference logged.*" His voice cracked loud in the small room, too loud, and he hauled it down. "I didn't care Monday. I think I know now. If she said your name, it isn't in my head. It's in theirs. I knotted the line, and the machine wrote down where it went anyway."

Arenya wrote: *two sounds. Not a question. Telling someone.* Then *proximal reference logged,* and she underlined *logged* until the nib nearly went through.

"Thank you for not filling it." It cost her. "Aunty used to call me *kanna*. That's two sounds." She shook her head once. "Or it's Appa. Or Amma. I'm not deciding that in a bakery storeroom. Gap noted."

"*Kanna*," Caleb said quietly, once, to see if it fit the shape in his head. It might. He didn't tell her that it did.

She turned the page and her voice went to the one she used for evidence.

"Three things. Who is Shepherd and how do you reach him. What is Drop Two. And where did the ash come from, because a man told to close a file doesn't carry the file out in a duffel."

"Shepherd I've never seen. A voice, through the Crown. Level. Polite. No accent. He gives times and targets. He says *lost property* when something goes missing. I never reached him, he reached me. Mine's been dark since Monday, and I'd keep it that way."

He laid his hand over the duffel.

"Drop Two is a pickup. Somewhere on the Peninsula. An industrial lot, a roll-up door. A white van takes the canister and a man I never saw clearly signs for it. After that I don't know. I never asked. That's on me too." His thumb moved on the canvas. "The ash was supposed to go in the van. In the car it was ticking in my lap and I could feel her in it. Not a spirit. Her leftovers. I couldn't hand a woman who said a name like that to a man with a clipboard. So I ran. That's why they're hunting me. I stole it and I didn't even know what I was stealing."

He looked at Arenya.

"It's hers. It was always hers. I'm only the one who carried it."

* * *

Out front a chair scraped. Someone laughed. Then the bolt on the front door went home with a heavy clack.

On *it's hers*, her palm didn't pulse or burn. It went still, warm and attentive, the way a room goes quiet when someone you love walks into it. Under Caleb's hand the duffel ticked once, and again a full beat later. Not slow now. For the first time tonight it matched hers exactly.

"Mine's not doing it," Ava said from the walk-in, staring at her palm. "It always ticks with it. It's not following me now."

Malachi hadn't moved from the doorframe, but his eyes had left Caleb at *Drop Two* and stayed on the bag since. "Roll-up door on the Peninsula," he said, to nobody. "Industrial lot." He didn't say what he was thinking. His jaw said it for him.

On the concrete, the grains along one side of Kino's open jar had crept toward the duffel, a crescent a few millimetres wide, like iron filings near a magnet. Kino looked at it a long moment. Then he put the lid on, slowly, and left it where it was.

"It's not a trap," he said to Arenya, quiet. "The salt leans like that toward something that's *owed*. That's not hunger. But I don't know what happens if you pick it up, and I'd rather you weren't alone with the guess." His crate moved half a foot closer. "Your call. If you reach, my hand's on the jar."

"If she takes it," he added, to Caleb, "you don't ask for it back."

Caleb took his hand off the bag and let it rest on the concrete between his boots and her crate. He didn't push it toward her. He just stopped holding on.

"It's matching me," Arenya said out loud, because she'd promised. Her voice didn't sound like hers. "Not pulsing. In time. Like when you sit next to someone on a couch and your breathing does that thing. Tell me if I shouldn't."

She didn't wait long enough for the answer.

She got off the crate and knelt on the concrete. It was cold through her jeans and smelled of bleach. She didn't unzip the bag. She put her left hand flat on the canvas, over the place where the ticking lived, and left it there.

"Hi, Aunty." One stupid wet laugh, because it was that or the other thing. "You hated my driving. So you got a guy with two tours and a duffel bag instead. That's an upgrade, I guess." Her thumb moved on the canvas. "I'm not taking you as a delivery. Nobody's signing for you. I'm just here. You don't have to go anywhere you don't want to."

The canvas was cold, and then it wasn't.

The tick went on, in time with her, five beats, six. Then it slowed, and she felt her own pulse slow with it, though she hadn't asked. The bleach smell thinned. For the space of one breath the back room smelled of filter coffee boiled too long, and the cold cream Aunty kept by her sink, faint and unmistakable.

Then it was bleach again.

The ticking stopped. It didn't cut off. It *settled*, like a kettle taken off the flame. Her palm lay warm against the canvas, and it felt exactly like a hand resting on the back of hers, light, the way you'd stop a child fidgeting in temple.

*Love.* Abernathy, in the bond, with the accent worn thin as a coin. *Don't move your hand. There's somebody sat on the bench with us. Warm. Not nasty. You know 'er, it went through you like a kettle clicking off. I don't think she's clocked me, and I'd as soon keep it that way. Don't ask me why.* A pause. *Whatever she's resting on, it's you. Eat something after. I mean it.*

Ava let out a breath she'd held a long time. She turned her left hand over under the tube light. "It's gone," she said. "The cold. All of it." She closed her fist and opened it, as if unsure she was allowed to have it back.

Caleb had put both hands over his face. He didn't make a sound.

"You carried her," Arenya said to him, still on her knees, her eyes doing something she was ignoring. "That's in the notebook too. It doesn't cancel the kitchen and it doesn't have to. Both lines stay." Then, without looking away: "Malachi. Whatever your jaw is thinking about a roll-up door, say it. I'm done with people knowing things in rooms I'm standing in."

* * *

Malachi waited until Caleb's shoulders stopped moving. Then he came off the jamb, crossed the room, and crouched so his eyes were level with hers. The duffel sat between them. He didn't touch it.

"Eleven years ago, in a port city I'm not going to name, a customs clerk sat down across from a man in a café and stopped breathing forty seconds later. Eyes open, fixed. Cerebral event." He kept his voice level, because it was the only way he could say it. "Three days after, we traced the man's car to an industrial lot outside the city. Roll-up door. A white van came in at night and a man with a clipboard signed for something small enough to carry in one hand. I wrote it up. I was told the clerk had an aneurysm, the lot was a refrigeration contractor, and the file no longer existed. I was told to forget it."

A beat.

"I did what I was told. That's what my jaw was thinking."

He looked at Caleb, then back at her.

"And you're owed more. I'm not with the Department of Energy. That's a card in my wallet. The people who trained his team and wrote his callsigns and put a voice in his head may be the same people who sign my paychecks. I don't know that yet. Tonight I've stopped being able to tell myself I don't suspect it."

He held her eyes and let it cost what it cost.

"The name of who I work for, I'll give you once I know saying it in a room won't get anyone in this one killed. I'm not hiding. I'm telling you exactly where the line is, so you never catch me standing behind it. But Caleb doesn't go to my office, and that file doesn't go to anyone with a clipboard. His people or mine. Not tonight."

Her palm stayed warm through all of it. *Department of Energy. A card in my wallet. May be the same people.* Whatever was resting on the back of her hand, it wasn't troubled by him.

"Thank you for drawing the line out loud," Arenya said. "That's what I asked for. I'm writing it down as *where the line is*, not as a lie." She looked at him. "And I'll hold you to the name."

Caleb had lowered his hands. He was staring at Malachi as if trying to place a face from a briefing. "The van," he said hoarsely. "White. No fleet number. Rear windows blacked out?" He didn't wait. He could read the answer on Malachi's face.

On the wall, the clock ticked toward nine-twenty.

"Lupe does the final lock at nine-forty," Ava said. "Back door alarms after that, and she checks this room. You've got about twenty minutes. I'm not saying leave. I'm saying somebody has to decide where *that*" — the duffel, Caleb — "sleeps tonight. Because it isn't here."

Four people, a bag of ash, and nowhere obvious to take it.

They worked it out fast, in low voices, the way people do when there's a clock. Not Arenya's apartment: her notes were findable, her name was on a lease, and the lock could be opened with a hairpin. Not her parents'. Appa was asleep in his chair and didn't need this in the house tonight.

"Nana Itzel's," Arenya said. "Salt, a witness line, and a grandson who dreams with one eye open. Aunty fed your family. She said *feed Itzel's boy*." She looked at Kino. "But it's Itzel's house and Itzel's call."

Kino nodded slowly. Then he said the part he disagreed with, to her face, as she'd asked. The ash, maybe. Nana would know what to do with that. But Caleb didn't cross the line. He'd been the hand in a kitchen like hers on Monday, and Kino wasn't walking that into the house he'd sworn to keep shut. The porch, or the truck, with Malachi parked behind.

"That's not a punishment," he told Caleb. "It's the rules of the house. You can say no."

"I don't disagree with any of it." Caleb's voice was rough. "I'll sleep where Malachi can see my door. The Vesper stays in my pocket, and I decide. If I reach for it, I'll say so first." He looked at the bag, then at Arenya, and he didn't try to make his face do anything. "I won't ask for it back. If I'm in the car, it's because I'm carried. Not because I'm carrying."

"One more thing, since twenty minutes is short. The backwards lanyard. Every pickup, the man's badge was turned to his own chest. And Shepherd said *Friday*. Not just to me, to the team. Something is scheduled for Friday." He swallowed. "Tonight is Friday. I was off the board by then. But whoever replaced me knows the date. I'd rather you hear it from me now than find out at a trailhead."

Arenya wrote **FRIDAY. Tonight. Replacement knows the date** and boxed it twice.

"Call Nana first," she said. "If she says yes, she sets the terms and Caleb follows them. If no, the mountain, and Aunty goes in the cab with me. Not in the trunk. Not in a car with a man holding the strap." She stood, knees cracking, and lifted the duffel by both handles the way you'd lift a sleeping kid. It weighed about what a hand weighs. "Nobody signs, nobody receives. I'm moving her. That's the whole sentence."

Malachi held out his palm for Caleb's phone. "Battery out. On the table. Then you walk a pace behind my left shoulder. If anything on this block looks wrong, you say 'wrong' and nothing else." He didn't ask twice.

Kino pressed a pinch of salt along the inside edge of the threshold, thin as a pencil line, pulled up his hood, and went out under the sodium light with his phone. Through the propped door they heard him begin low, in Spanish. *Nana. Soy yo. Tengo una pregunta, y no la voy a escribir.*

Malachi gave Arenya the prepaid. She stood a few paces from Kino with the duffel on her arm and her back to the brick, and dialed Amma's mobile from memory.

She didn't give Amma time to start.

"Amma. Naan dhaan. I'm fine. Lock the door tonight, both locks and the chain. Go look at Appa and turn the heater down, he runs hot when he sleeps. Don't open it for anyone you didn't call, even if they know your name." She breathed. "Rasam was good. He drank it. I love you. I'll explain at the table. In person. Okay?"

She held the phone out flat to Malachi with the line still open, so he could hear whatever there was to hear and she wouldn't have to decide what she was hearing. A breath. A word in Tamil. The sound of a chain being slid home.

"Both locks," he said, taking the phone. "She did it while you were still talking." He popped the battery and put the pieces in separate pockets.

Kino came back. "She said yes." His voice was low and flat. "To Aunty. Not to him inside. Porch, her salt, and he doesn't cross." Then he repeated the rest, the way she'd said it: the cat had been sitting at her door for an hour, looking west, and hadn't eaten. *Come by Mission Street, not the freeway.* She'd hung up without goodbye. She never said it.

Which was when Arenya's palm went cool.

* * *

Not cold. A quarter-degree. The way a room changes when someone opens a window two floors up.

She said it out loud as the rest of her went still. "Palm went cool. Quarter degree." She turned her head to the alley's far end. "Grey Nissan at the 18th Street end. Driver's seat empty. Passenger window fogged from inside."

It sat at the curb, engine off. The fog on the glass was even, edge to edge, as if someone had been breathing on it for a very long time.

"The florist's, or its twin."

She didn't look at it. She watched its mirrors, and shifted the duffel from her ribs to her chest, both arms crossed over it, so it was the last thing anyone got to. Kino moved into the space between her and the street and laid a thumb-wide line of salt on the asphalt before her boots. "Nobody touch the glass," he said. "Fogged from inside with nobody in it. That's not a man waiting. That's something breathing on a window so we'll look."

"I'll check it." Malachi was already walking, along the near wall, an ordinary pace, one man taking a shortcut to a bus. "Stay on the salt," he said to Caleb without turning. "Next to Arenya."

He stopped a body's length short of the car, outside the line of the fog. Wet tires. No drip under the tailpipe. A dark dash. A folded newspaper on the back seat. He held his bare palm a few inches off the driver's door and felt nothing. Cold. It had been parked a while.

Plate: 7KBQ4R2. The last character clear this time, nothing hiding it. From the mirror hung a lanyard, the badge turned to the windshield, so all he could see was the white plastic back of the card.

At the kitchen door, Kino counted ten seconds with his breath, the way Nana had taught him to count a night-walk. The copal was in his pocket, the lid already loose.

"Wrong," Caleb said, very low. It was the one word he'd been given. "That's not a team car. We drove grey Corollas, and Pruitt wiped the glass with his sleeve every time. Nobody on my team ever breathed on a window." His hand had gone toward his left pocket. He caught it halfway. "My hand just went for the dose. I'm putting it in my jacket and keeping it there. That's not need. That's what happens when that fog shows up. It means he's close enough to know I'm dark, and he wants me to take it." He stepped away from the car. "If it moves toward the bag, drop me. Not her. I'm the one he wants back."

Then the fog on the passenger window moved.

It didn't drift. From the inside, slowly, the way a fingertip would, a short vertical line was drawn through the white, and clear glass showed behind it. Then a second beside it. A third. A fourth, evenly spaced, like a ruled ledger.

Kino's sternum drew tight on the third stroke, as it did when the grey line in his dream counted a step. He pressed a fist to it through the hoodie and kept moving. Arenya's palm dropped another quarter-degree. Caleb put two fingers to the scar at the crown of his skull and took them away. "Warm," he said. "No signal in it. It remembers one. I'm not answering."

Four strokes. Four people in the alley.

The fingertip paused over the glass, as if it were considering a fifth.

"Four strokes," Arenya said under her breath, flat, clipped. "Don't count them. Don't answer it." She turned her back on the glass before a fifth could come. "Malachi, back. Leave the plate, you have it. Caleb, a pace behind him and you say nothing."

From the steel door, fast and low, Ava: "Front's on a deadbolt. No alarm till Lupe arms it. Through the kitchen, *now*. I'll lock behind you."

"Ava. Lock it and stay off the window." Arenya tugged Kino's sleeve, once. "You're not on its list. Keep it that way."

They went through the kitchen at a fast, ordinary pace, past the mop sink and the milk crates, eyes on the floor tile and not on the camera. Malachi fell in beside Caleb instead of ahead of him. "Don't run," he murmured. "Running tells it we know it's a door."

At the deadbolt Arenya waited for Ava's hand. It was cold and steady. The door opened on Guerrero, the sidewalk washed orange under the streetlights, and shut behind them with a click and a turn. Through the glass, Ava didn't wave. She stepped back into the dark of the shop and was gone from the window, as she'd been told.

Nobody looked toward 18th.

Kino laid salt behind his heel at the curb and again at the corner, white commas on the dark. On 20th the truck was cold inside and smelled of pine and old coffee. He unlocked the passenger side first. Arenya climbed in, shut the door with her hip, and set the duffel upright in her lap with both arms around it. She buckled the belt under it, not over. The copal sat unlit on the dash.

*Abernathy,* she said into the bond, walking, before the door closed. *Four lines and a pause over a fifth. What does it want with another stroke? Is the one on the bench still with us?*

*Short, then,* he answered. *A fifth means it's counting who's left. Four's the tally of who it's got. The fifth is the one it hasn't pinned yet, and it's wondering if that's you or me.* The cockney had gone out of him entirely. *Don't turn round, love. Don't give it the number. And the one on the bench: still there. Quieter, but sat right against your palm. Didn't flinch at the car. Didn't like it, mind. I'd say it was listening to it.*

"Aunty's still with us," she told Kino, quietly. "Against my palm. She wasn't scared of the car. She was listening to it." She breathed out. "And the fifth is whoever it hasn't pinned. Might be me. Might be something with me. So nobody gives it a number."

She opened the notebook without looking at the street and wrote only the plate, **7KBQ4R2**, the backwards lanyard, and *Friday*. Then she closed it.

"If the mirror goes white, say 'wrong' and don't explain." She let her head fall back against the seat. "And somewhere on that mountain, or in that kitchen, I'm going to eat whatever Nana has. I've been told."

A block back, Malachi's headlights came on and stayed a steady distance behind, never closer.

They took Mission south, slow, past taquerias pulling down their shutters and bus shelters lit like aquariums, and nobody took the first turn they were offered. The canister lay quiet against her chest, not ticking, warm as a sleeping child. Her palm was warm too, the quarter-degrees climbing back one by one as the alley fell behind.

The fifth stroke didn't come.

Or it came where none of them was looking.

Twenty minutes ahead, in the Excelsior, a cat sat at a door and looked west.

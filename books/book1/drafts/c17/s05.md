Mission Street at ten on a Friday was taquerías with their grates half down, a pupusería still steaming, and a church marquee that said ALL SOULS — NOV 2 — BRING THEIR NAMES.

Arenya read it twice as they passed. Nobody in the truck mentioned it.

Kino didn't slow through the Excelsior, but his eyes went left at Brazil Avenue, where the old house had been, and came back. In the side mirror Malachi's headlights sat a block behind, steady as a metronome.

Near the county line the copal on the dash began to smell.

Nobody had lit it. Nobody had touched it. The resin hung in the cab anyway, thin and sweet, like a sentence somebody had started in the next room. Against Arenya's ribs the canister gave one slow tick.

She looked down at it. "She's clearing her throat," she said.

"Yeah," said Kino. "I heard."

*Don't say 'I told you so,'* she thought at the bond.

Abernathy, for once, said nothing at all.

* * *

Top of the Hill: a gas station, a liquor store, the WELCOME TO DALY CITY sign half-swallowed by a eucalyptus. Then the turn up into Hillcrest, where the fog had come in early and sat low on the stucco like it had paid for the lot.

Halfway up on the right, Aunty's blue house was dark. The cottage behind it was darker.

Nobody said anything as they passed. Arenya found she was holding her breath, and let it go slowly, so the truck wouldn't hear.

Nana Itzel was on her porch in a man's cardigan, a clay bowl against her hip. The porch light was off. A candle in a jar burned by the step instead. Along the foot of the porch ran a line of salt, white and unbroken, except for a gap about a body wide at the far end, by an old glider bench made up with blankets.

Ix sat at the front door. Facing west. It did not turn when the trucks pulled in.

Nana watched Malachi park. She watched the pale boy in his back seat for a long moment.

Then she looked only at her grandson.

"Mijo," she said. "The last of it, you lay. Not me." She held out the bowl. "And before you do, tell me where she's standing. Your Señora. I want to know which side of my line she is on tonight."

To Arenya, more gently, and without taking her eyes off Kino: "There's caldo on the stove. Bring Susmithaa inside. She always used the kitchen door."

Arenya got out slowly, the canister held against her sternum with both arms. She stopped at the white line and did not step over it. She looked at Nana, not at the gap.

"Buenas noches, Nana. I'm on the street side of your line. Outside it. I'll stay here until you say where I stand." She breathed. "She's quiet. She ticked once at the county line. That's all. I didn't sign for her, and I'm not asking anyone to sign for me."

She glanced back at the boy in Malachi's car. Then at the kitchen door.

"Can I bring her in the way she'd come? I'd like to carry her myself."

* * *

Kino cut the engine and sat a second longer, letting the copal settle in his chest the way you let a dog sniff your hand.

Then he got out, and he took the bowl from his grandmother with both hands. It was heavier than salt had any right to be. She had put something in it. He didn't ask.

"Nana." He kept his eyes on her, not on the cat. "Hace semanas que la sueño de espaldas. I thought she'd turned away from me. I didn't tell you. I was ashamed."

It cost him. He said the rest fast.

"Pero estaba mal. On your floor I saw it right. She has her back to me because she's *facing* something. There's a grey line that follows my steps and puts a tick on each one, and she stands between it and me." He turned and looked west down the hill, the way the cat was looking, past the dark house and into the fog. "Tonight she's on the outside of your line, Nana. Out there. Not in here with us. Holding the door."

Nana said nothing for a while. The candle in its jar leaned once, though there was no wind, and stood straight again.

She came down off the porch and put her hand flat on top of his head, the way you would with a boy of six.

"Ashamed," she said, tasting it. She didn't like it. "Of her back. Ay, mijo. A woman who turns her back on the street is guarding the house. Who taught you to read it the other way? Not me." She took her hand away. "Good. She's outside. Then we don't need to be brave out there. Only careful in here."

She turned to Arenya, who was still standing where a guest stands.

"That was right. Where you stood." Nana bent, took a pinch of salt from the bowl and let it fall at Arenya's feet, a small white star on the pavement. "Now you're invited. Kitchen door. Carry her yourself, she'd want that. Mind the third step." Something crossed her face, not quite a smile. "She tripped on it in 2009 with a whole box of mangoes and didn't drop one."

Arenya's throat did something she didn't authorize.

* * *

She put her heel on the right side of the star. Then she stepped exactly where Kino's boot stepped, one foot and then the other, through the hand's width of salt he'd scuffed aside. Elbows tight. She did not brush the jamb.

At the kitchen door the third step moved under her. She shifted the canister into one arm and didn't fall.

"Susmithaa Sowri," she said, quietly, to the house and not to anyone in it. (Rajagopalan was her mother's mother's family. It was Sowri here; it would be Sowri on the paper.) "It's Arenya. I've brought her home. Not home. Somewhere safe. You know the difference. Kanna's here."

Her voice cracked on the last word. She hated that. She added, "Sorry about the mug, Aunty. I'll tell you later."

Behind her Kino bent and poured a thin white thread across the gap. He pressed the edge flat with two fingers. It felt, he thought, like closing a book on a finger.

The kitchen smelled of chicken, cumin and cilantro stems. A pot on a low flame, a radio turned down to nothing. Above the sink a shelf ran the width of the window, and Nana's dead were already set out for Monday: a man in a sombrero in a tin frame, a girl in a First Communion dress, a dish of salt, a glass of water.

At the end, a space had been cleared and wiped. Still damp.

*"The end of the shelf, mija,"* Nana called from the porch, not coming in. *"By the water. She liked to be near the window."*

Arenya set her down. She took her time. She turned the canister a quarter-turn so the green steel faced the glass.

"There you go, Aunty. Window seat."

When she'd said *Kanna*, the steel had warmed. Not a tick. Just the plain steady warmth of something set down on a sunny sill. It was in her palm now, and she left the palm where it was.

Outside, her notebook sat shut in her bag. Malachi had said it from the curb, quietly: *Whatever you carry in there, don't write it down tonight.* She hadn't needed telling. Everything she wrote could be found. People had said that to her for a week and she'd finally started to believe them.

"Nobody signs," she said to the canister. "Nobody receives. You're ours, and you're not for sale or delivery."

*Is the warm thing here?* she thought, at the bond, with her mouth closed. *Tell me if it moves. Not out loud.*

* * *

On the porch, Malachi had walked Caleb up the path a pace behind his shoulder and stopped short of the salt. He looked at the line first. Then at the old woman.

"Señora Lucero. Malachi. I'm the one who followed the truck. I owe you for the porch, and I'll pay it however you say." A beat. "The fog at the bottom of the hill stopped moving a minute ago. I'd count it as watching. I'll watch it from outside your line until the sun's up."

Nana regarded him for the length of a breath. "My line isn't a fence, señor, and the car is the street. You watch from my bottom step, on the outside, where I can see you. There's coffee. You'll drink it."

Her chin tipped toward Caleb. "And him, on my word: *pase.*"

Caleb went through the gap like a man stepping onto ice. He sat on the glider, took the blanket, and did not lie down. Both hands stayed on his knees, in plain sight. Kino tipped the bowl, and the white closed behind Caleb's heels with a small dry sound, like a match being struck and not catching. Whatever Nana had mixed in glinted grey for a second in the candlelight.

Then it was only salt.

Kino took the paper twist he'd carried from the truck down to the curb and folded it into Malachi's palm. "Pour it behind your wheels. A line on the road, ankle-wide. If the fog moves, you drive. You don't fight it." He looked past him at the still grey at the bottom of the hill. "It's counting. Let it count us wrong."

At the bottom of the hill a streetlight went out.

It didn't flicker first. A moment later the next one up did the same, and the next. Three dark lamps, one at a time, in no hurry, coming toward the blue house.

Caleb said, very quietly, "My scar."

Then louder, the way he'd been told: "Señora. My scar is warm."

* * *

Kino was off the porch before the third lamp died. He crouched in front of the glider, an arm's length from Caleb, and set the bowl on the boards between them.

"Hands on your knees. Don't touch it. Don't answer it, not even to say no." The voice for a spooked horse. "What's coming up the hill is coming for the thing you used to wear. It'll go looking for the door that fits. You talk to me. Count in Spanish if you have to. Uno, dos. Anything that isn't a tally."

He laid his left forearm along his knee, birthmark up, where the candle could find it. His fist came away from his sternum, where it had been all night.

The fourth lamp went. The fifth.

The sixth stood in front of the blue house, and it didn't go out. It dimmed to the color of weak tea and held, as if someone had rested a thumb on it.

Under it, with no headlights and no engine, a grey Nissan rolled up the hill on what sounded like gravity.

It stopped at the curb. The driver's seat was empty. The lanyard on the mirror turned once on its cord. From Nana's bottom step Malachi could read the plate without trying: **7KBQ4R2**.

The passenger window began to fog from the inside. Four strokes were already on the glass, old ones, smeared. The glass waited.

Then, behind the dark house, in the cottage at the back where Susmithaa had lived eleven years, the kitchen light came on.

A small yellow square. A curtain half drawn. A shape moved past it, a woman's height, and water ran in a sink. Over the fog and the dead lamps a voice came up the hill, clear enough to carry. Mild. A little tired. A voice that had answered a lot of doors.

*"Can I help you?"*

Caleb made a sound as if he'd been hit in the stomach. His hands stayed on his knees only because Kino was right there. He shook hard enough to rattle the chain at his neck.

"That's what she said," he told Kino, through his teeth. "That's exactly how she said it."

Ix's ears went flat. He still did not turn.

In the kitchen, under Arenya's palm, the canister went cold. Not the cold of steel. The stillness of someone holding her breath behind a door.

* * *

"That's not her," Kino said, flat, one hand hard on Caleb's shoulder. "That's her *voice.* A voice is a thing you can pick up off the floor. It's bait wearing her coat." His jaw was tight. "Eyes on me. You don't answer it. You don't say her name. Uno, dos. Say it."

Toward the kitchen, not loud, because he knew she could hear: "Arenya. Aunty's cold because she's listening, not because she's leaving. Keep your hand on her. *Don't go to the back door.*"

He didn't look at the lit window. If he looked, he'd walk.

Malachi didn't stand. He kept his left hand flat on the bottom step, below the line, and watched the cottage. The curtain. The shape. How long the water ran. The sink never changed its note, and a real tap would.

"Nobody goes down that hill," he said. "It was built from what Caleb saw Monday. It's a door cut to fit him, and it wants one of us to knock." Then, not turning: "Caleb. Look at me. Her saying it was Monday. Tell me what color the mug was."

In the kitchen Arenya stood with her back to the window over the sink. Both hands on the lid.

"I know, Aunty," she said into the steel. "I hear it too. It's not you. You're here. Under my hand."

Loud enough for the porch, clipped and flat, the scared way: "Malachi, the mug was blue. It's mine. Cartoon bird sticker, half peeled, from college. If that voice asks about the mug, it's reading Caleb. It said 'Can I help you' and left a gap. It wants somebody to fill the gap with a name. *Nobody fill it.*"

Cumin. Cilantro. Chicken. And under them, the ghost of cold cream and filter coffee, close and real, coming from the shelf and not the hill.

*Right, love, quick and quiet,* said Abernathy in the bond, finally. *The warm one's still sat on that shelf with you. It ain't moved. It's gone still, like someone holdin' their breath behind a door, and I don't reckon it's scared. I reckon it's listenin' harder than the Nissan is. Them lamps ain't for her, guv. They're walkin' toward the blue house, where he stood Monday. That voice is bait cut to fit him. Your Aunty never said "Can I help you" to you in her life. That's a stranger's door-voice.*

A pause, with the accent slipping a quarter inch.

*But the cottage light. Whoever built that knows where she lived. Somebody's read the house, not just the boy.*

*Hand on the lid,* he said. *Stay off the back door. And eat the caldo when it's quiet. Union rules.*

"Salt holds what walks, señor," Nana said, from the porch, to Malachi's question. She had not taken her eyes off the hill. "What talks, salt can't hold. Only your mouth holds against that." She lifted her chin at the cottage. "And you're right about the tap. Listen."

Malachi listened. Now he couldn't stop. The same splash. The same small gurgle. One second of water, looping. The shape behind the curtain passed the light, and passed it again, with exactly the same sway at the shoulder.

On the Nissan's glass, the four strokes held. Nothing drew a fifth.

The voice waited for an answer. Nobody gave it one. The tap went on looping into the gap.

Then the voice changed.

It wasn't louder. It was closer, the way a voice gets closer when someone leans in to a phone. Softer, and warmer, the end of the word rising, the way you call someone in from another room.

*"Kanna?"*

* * *

It went through her like the Daly City kitchen used to.

Thirteen years old. Shoes off at the door. Her voice from the stove before Arenya had even said hello, rising at the end, because she already knew it was her.

Arenya's heels lifted a quarter inch off Nana's linoleum. Her mouth opened on the *y* of *yes.*

She closed it.

She closed it so hard her teeth clicked.

She didn't turn around. She put both palms flat on the cold green steel and leaned her forehead down until it almost touched the lid. Then she gave the word somewhere to go that wasn't the window. She didn't speak it. She breathed it, at a volume nothing outside that kitchen could hear, into the shelf, into her:

"You never called me from the yard, Aunty. You called from the stove. With your back to me. Because you didn't have to look."

Her eyes were wet. She let them be. She didn't wipe them. Wiping was a movement, and she was not giving the hill a single movement.

She stood at the end of Nana's shelf with a man in a sombrero and a girl in a Communion dress, and counted nothing, and answered nothing. Outside, the tap looped its one second of water into the gap where her name was supposed to go.

Three seconds.

Then, under both her palms, the canister ticked.

Not the throat-clearing from the county line. Two ticks, close together, the second a shade lower than the first, the way a word falls at its end when you say it toward a stove and don't need to turn around. Warmth came back into the steel all at once, like a hand coming out of a coat pocket.

Down the hill the cottage light went out. There was no click. It simply wasn't on anymore. The tap stopped in the middle of its loop.

On the Nissan's passenger glass something began to draw the fifth stroke. Top to bottom. Slowly.

Halfway, it stopped.

The line hung there, unfinished. A bead of water ran down out of it and blurred it into a smear. There had been nobody to witness it, so the tally could not close.

The car rolled backward down the hill, no lights, no engine. As it passed each dark lamp, the lamp came back on behind it. One, two, three, all the way to the bottom, until Hillcrest was fog and sodium light again and a dog two streets over started barking as if it had just woken up.

On the glider Caleb let out a breath that seemed to have been in him since Monday.

"Blue," he said, to nobody. "It was blue."

Ix turned. He didn't look west anymore. He looked through the screen door, down the hall, at the kitchen and the girl at the end of the shelf with her forehead nearly on the lid. He blinked, slowly, once.

Then he lay down across the threshold with his back to the street, the way a cat lies down when a watch is over.

* * *

"Two ticks," Arenya told the shelf, hoarse, when she could. "I heard you. Yes. Okay."

She wiped her face with the heel of her hand. Nobody was watching now except a girl in a Communion dress, and the girl had seen worse.

She found a bowl. She ladled the caldo herself, and her hands shook a little, and she let them. She carried the first bowl to the screen door and set it on the boards by Caleb's knee without a word. Then back for a second, for Malachi, who had sat on a step and watched a looping tap for an hour so no one else had to. Tonight that counted.

Kino touched Caleb's shoulder again, lighter. "Blue. Good. You held."

He didn't say more. Caleb wouldn't have believed it yet.

Then he went and walked the line once, heel to toe, pressing every inch flat with his thumb. It was the only way he knew to say thank you to salt.

Malachi washed his hands at Nana's sink under running water, slowly, the way you do when you want the minute to be long. The kettle was already on. He sat at the end of the table, across from the shelf.

"Arenya. I asked you for an hour on check 2207. Not tonight. Tonight you eat." He folded his hands. "Someone forged 'R. Sowri' on that endorsement. I want the ink, the pressure, the pen. A forger leaves a habit. The lanyard man has one too. He wears it backwards. Habits are how I find people."

"Tomorrow," Arenya said. "All of it. Tonight all I've got is 'it slants right and the y has a bracket.'" She rubbed her eyes. "Whoever built that voice didn't hear it from her. Caleb said she said it at her sink, looking past him. The Crown caught it. *Proximal reference.* They played it back at me like a hostage call." She looked at the green steel in its stripe of light. "Somewhere there's a recording of my aunt's last word being mine. I want it. Not tonight. But I want it."

*It was a copy,* Abernathy said, when she'd sat and the spoon was in her hand. *Both voices. The "Can I help you," hollow, nobody behind it. The "Kanna" cut finer. Somebody heard it said proper. Kino's right.*

*The two ticks, though. That weren't a copy. That was the warm one, and it weren't wearin' her. It was answerin'. Which means it knows the difference, and it picked you.*

Another pause, longer.

*One more thing, and I owe you it. When the fifth stroke started and stopped, somethin' in me went quiet. Like I'd been counted and the count got dropped. Don't ask me what that means. I don't know. I'm just not pretendin' it didn't happen.*

*Finish the bowl,* he said. *All of it.*

She finished it.

Nana came in last. She stepped over Ix the way you step over a sleeping grandchild and lowered herself into the chair at the head of the table with a small grunt she would have denied. She didn't take caldo. She took the coffee Malachi hadn't finished.

"How many more, mijo? However many it takes for them to learn." To Malachi: "And he's right. Tonight they sent a voice. A voice is cheap. The second one is when they stop asking and send someone with hands. Hands have to come up Mission Street like everybody else. And hands, I can see."

To Arenya, gentler: "Before Monday? Nothing. She stays on my shelf with my people. Ernesto"—a tilt of her head at the man in the sombrero—"will talk her ear off. Monday is Día de los Muertos. We take her name to the church, like the sign says. After that she's not mine to decide. She's your family's. Yours, and your father's." A pause. "He's going to have to say something besides *it's handled,* eh?"

Behind her, the kettle began to tick toward a boil. Water ran in the sink: a real tap, its note changing as the basin filled.

Then something buzzed in Malachi's coat.

* * *

Inside left pocket. Where he'd put Caleb's prepaid, and its battery, separately. He could feel the battery against his ribs, a flat cold slab in its own fold of lining.

The phone buzzed anyway. Once, long. The glow of its little screen came through the wool.

On the porch Caleb went very still. Through the screen door, not loud: "That's the Shepherd line. He only texts when he's already somewhere."

Arenya was on her feet before she'd decided to be. She put both hands flat on Nana's table instead of reaching for anything. "Don't take it out. Don't read it. Don't answer."

Malachi didn't take it out. He put his hand over the pocket. "The battery is out. So it isn't a phone buzzing. It's something that wants me to think it is." He stood, took off the coat, carried it to the porch and laid it on the boards beyond the salt, pocket up. He didn't open it.

"Caleb. Near the road, or near you?"

"Metal talks," Kino said, at the screen door, eyes on the coat. "Nana's got a clay comal in the shed, and the grey salt. Wet dishtowel, under the comal, outside the line."

Caleb took his time. His hand rose halfway to his neck, stopped, and went back to his knee.

"Scar's cool. Cooler than it's been all night." Then: "Near. Not near me. Near the *thing.* Monday at 2:51 the line buzzed and said ON SITE. He was already parked at the bottom of this street, close enough to see her door. We didn't know till we came out and the Nissan was there, empty." He swallowed. "He doesn't text to tell you where he's going. He texts so you know he's already watching what you do next."

Nana went out and came back from the shed with the comal under one arm and a jar of salt. "Clay keeps its mouth shut." She soaked a dishtowel, wrung it once, and gave it to her grandson. Kino wrapped the coat's pocket, phone and all, without opening it. He set it on the bottom step under the upturned comal and rang it with grey salt.

The glow under the clay didn't fade. It was just gone.

After that the house settled the way old houses do. The heater ticked. Ernesto kept his watch from the shelf. Ix slept across the door. At the bottom of the hill the fog moved again, drifting as fog is supposed to, and the dog two streets over gave up.

Midnight came and went. Somewhere past one, Hillcrest was quiet enough to hear the freeway they hadn't taken.

* * *

The couch was too short and smelled of cedar and someone's old perfume. Arenya lay on her side under Nana's wool blanket, shoes still on, facing the kitchen. From here she could see the shelf: Ernesto's tin frame, the girl in white, the water glass, a green steel edge catching the stove pilot.

Somewhere past one she took out her phone and held it under the blanket with the screen turned as dim as it went. She didn't open anything. Amma's name was there from last night. *Lock the door, both locks, the chain.* Amma had answered with the chain. She thought of Appa asleep in his chair with his glasses pushed up on his forehead, a thermos of rasam going cold, and the note on the rack that said nothing about any of this.

She put the phone face down on her chest, over her heart. So it would be warm.

"Monday, Aunty," she said, almost without sound. "Church. Your name on the paper, and Appa saying something besides 'it's handled.' I'll make him. I'll be right next to him."

Her palm tingled once, faint, like a thumb pressed to the back of it.

She slept, finally, without deciding to.

* * *

Past two, the glider creaked under Caleb and stopped. He wasn't asleep. Kino could tell by the breathing.

Kino sat on the top step with his back against the post and Ix's tail across his boot, the empty bowl between his knees. For a long while he said nothing.

"Mi abuela told me once that the dark house isn't the one that hides things," he said at last, to the street. "It's the one that keeps them till they're ready." He turned the bowl a quarter turn. "You carried a lot for Shepherd. You can set one thing down here. Not a secret. Just a thing. Your pick."

He didn't look. He listened. And while he waited he ran his thumb along the birthmark on his forearm, the way Nana did when he was small and couldn't sleep, and noticed what he had not let himself notice all night.

The ache in his sternum was gone.

Not eased. Gone. Whatever the Lady was doing out there tonight, she was doing it where he couldn't feel the cost.

He let himself have that for exactly one breath. Then he put it away, because he didn't trust it, and went back to listening to the hill.

In the chair by the window, with his back to the wall and the curtain open two fingers' width, Malachi built the night in his head without paper. *ON SITE, 2:51 Monday. Already parked.* A man who announces himself isn't hiding. He's pressing. The voice had been a test of whether this house answered, and the phone was the same test, aimed at him.

Near four he noticed the one thing he'd have missed if he'd been sleeping. On Nana's bottom step, under the clay, the salt ring had not been disturbed. Nothing had come near it. Shepherd had waited for them to move first.

They hadn't.

He let that comfort him for exactly one minute.

Sometime in the small hours, after all of that, the glider creaked again. Caleb reached into his jacket, very slowly, so Kino could see every inch of the movement, and took out two foil Vesper packs he had carried all week without opening. He set them on the boards between them, beside the bowl. He didn't push them across. He let go.

"Those. They make the scar go quiet. Made me feel like I was doing the right thing for four seconds at a time." He breathed. "I don't want to feel right. I want to feel it."

Kino said nothing. Ix's tail stayed on his boot.

* * *

At 6:52 the fog thinned to gauze and the sun came up flat and gold on the bay side of the hill. In the kitchen it reached the end of the shelf.

Arenya woke to it on her face, the phone still warm on her chest. Across the room the green steel was warm too. She could tell without touching it, the way you can tell someone in the next room is awake.

Malachi was already outside the line. He walked down Hillcrest in his shirtsleeves, no coat. A man taking the air.

The curb in front of the blue house was wet with fog like everything else. Everything but a rectangle: car-sized, bone dry, as if something had stood on the street all night and kept the weather off. The lamp above it, the one that had dimmed and never gone out, still burned in full daylight, the color of weak tea.

He stopped a full car-length short and read it from the sidewalk with his hands in his pockets. He walked its edge once, outside it, like a chalk outline.

No oil. No drip. No coolant. A parked car leaves four patches where the rubber pressed and the damp couldn't reach; there were none. The corners were square, as if cut. Whatever had sat here had sat on the road without resting its weight on it.

Inside the rectangle, at the passenger door, the road grit held two shoe impressions side by side, toes toward the blue house. Leather soles. A man's ten or eleven, no tread. Someone had stood beside the car all night and not shifted his weight once.

On the flat top rail of Susmithaa's gate, in the fog-wet wood, was a fresh dry corner: a neat right angle, the width of a clipboard's edge. And in the lower hinge, caught where the wind had put it, a thumbnail curl of pink carbonless paper. The printed edge of a form. `…CEIVED BY:` and the start of a blank line.

Nothing written on it.

The lamp threw its weak light on everything around the dry patch except the patch. The rectangle sat in its own shade, as though the light were going around it. Up close the lamp hummed, and the hum wasn't steady. Four pulses, a pause, and the start of a fifth that never finished.

He touched nothing. He took one long look at the cottage window, turned his back on the lamp, and walked up the hill the way he'd come.

* * *

By the time Malachi reached the bottom step, the porch was awake. Nana was in the doorway with two cups, still in the cardigan, her hair in a braid she must have done in the dark. Kino sat on the top step. Caleb was on the glider, hands on knees. The foil packs still lay where he'd put them. Under its wet towel the comal hadn't moved, and its ring of grey salt was whole.

Nana held a cup out across the line so neither of them had to step. "Curb first. Then we decide about the clay. Tell it plain, señor. Old women hate a mystery before breakfast."

He drank first. "Plain, then. The Nissan didn't park. There's a dry rectangle at the curb, no tire marks, no weight on the road. Next to it a man stood all night in leather soles and never shifted his feet. He rested a clipboard on Susmithaa's gate rail. The lamp he stood under is still lit in full sun, and it hums four, a pause, and the start of a fifth that never finishes. The same count as the glass."

He looked at Caleb, then at Nana. "None of it's a threat. It's a man waiting to be handed something. Nobody gives it to him." Quieter, to Arenya through the screen: "There's one more thing. For you, not the porch. After breakfast."

"A man waiting to be handed something," Nana said. "Sí. They always are." She put her cup on the rail. "Bueno. With me standing here."

She let nobody else touch the clay. She came down to the step above it, bent slowly, and lifted the comal with both hands. Underneath, the dishtowel had dried in the shape of the pocket. Kino unfolded it a corner at a time while Malachi watched the salt, and nobody breathed on it.

The phone lay in the coat lining, face up. The battery was still in its own fold, a cold flat slab. The screen was lit anyway, a dim grey-green. One message, stamped **11:14 PM**, from a number that was only a row of zeros:

> LOST PROPERTY — CUSTODY CHAIN INCOMPLETE.
> RECEIPT TO BE COUNTERSIGNED BY NEXT OF KIN.
> SIGNATORY ON FILE: R. SOWRI.
> SUN 11/1. WE WILL COME TO HIM.

It held long enough for everyone to read it twice. Then it went out with no fade. When Kino touched the edge with one knuckle, it was cold as a stone pulled from a creek.

On the glider Caleb shut his eyes.

"R. Sowri," he said. "That was on the clipboard Monday. On the top sheet. I thought it was *her.*" He opened them and looked through the screen door at Arenya. "It wasn't her. It was him. They'd already filed your father."

Arenya read it twice, like everyone, and the second time the words stopped being words.

R. SOWRI.

Appa's initial, in the one place she'd never have looked, because she'd been so busy being afraid of the check that she'd forgotten he was the one whose name was on it. *We will come to him.*

The blanket slid off her shoulders. She didn't pick it up.

At the end of the shelf behind her the canister stayed warm. It didn't tick.

"Sunday," Nana said, looking at the dark phone, then at Arenya, then down Mission Street toward the freeway they hadn't taken. "Then your father talks today, mija."

"Sunday's tomorrow," Arenya said. Clipped, flat. The scared voice. "That's less than a day. And he doesn't talk on a phone." She looked at Malachi. "Not Caleb's, not my cell, not your prepaid, not the landline. They can hear a phone. They can't hear my father in his own kitchen with the lights on and my mother's hand on his arm."

"I'll drive," said Kino, already holding his keys. "You said 'beside,' and I heard it."

"You'll follow a block back," Malachi said. "Brights twice if it's grey. Nobody calls ahead. A knock from a stranger tells them nothing, but a knock from you at nine does." To Arenya, level: "Ask your father about the pen, not the paper. Who gave it to him. Whose hand steadied his."

Nana ruled it, as she had ruled everything. "The señor drives. Mija knocks. Mijo stays." She held up a hand before Kino could open his mouth. "Not because you're needed less. Because the Lady is outside my door and not outside theirs, and I want her where I can count on her. You and the boy and me, behind the salt. If they come with hands, they come here first. This is where the ash is."

Malachi took the cold phone in its towel and gave it back to her. Nana slid it under the comal beside the foil packs as if she were putting bread away.

Arenya stopped at the kitchen door and looked at the shelf. At Caleb on the glider.

"Nana, if she goes cold while I'm gone, don't let anyone sign for her. Put your hand on her and say her name. The whole one." Her voice caught on the surname and she let it. "Susmithaa Sowri. Caleb. Stay blue. That's your only job today."

Low, to Kino: "When we get there I'm going to ask Appa what he signed and who held the pen. If he says 'it's handled,' I need you standing close enough that I don't hit him."

Nana went into the kitchen and came back with a twist of grey salt in waxed paper. She didn't hand it to Malachi. She pressed it into Arenya's palm, the warm one, and closed her fingers over it.

"For his door, not his pocket. Pour it on the step yourself, before you go in. Then ask him." A beat. "And tell Raghavan that Itzel Lucero says he can stop being brave alone now. He'll know what I mean. He came to my porch in March and stood exactly where the señor is standing, and he wouldn't come in either."

It was 7:31 on Saturday morning. The fog had burned off Hillcrest. On the shelf the green steel lay warm in a stripe of sun. Down the hill the lamp outside the blue house finally went out, the way any streetlamp does at dawn: late, and as if nothing had happened.

Arenya went down the third step, the loose one.

She didn't trip.

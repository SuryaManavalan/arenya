Daly City on Halloween morning was grey and smelled of wet ice plant.

Down the block, somebody had put a plastic skeleton in a lawn chair. The fog had beaded on its skull. Kino's truck idled at the curb. Malachi's car stopped a block back and stayed there, and no brights flashed.

Every streetlamp on the street was off.

The kitchen door was round the side, past the recycling bins and the curry-leaf plant in its cracked blue pot. Amma brought that pot inside whenever the nights turned cold. It was still outside.

The back step had been swept.

Nobody swept it on a Saturday. The broom was still leaning against the rail, like it was waiting to be asked.

Kino knelt without a word and laid Nana's salt along the sill in a thin white line. The latch was up. The door stood open an inch. Through the gap came the KQED weekend news, very low, and the tick of a gas burner under a pot.

Appa sat at the kitchen table in his old PG&E fleece, reading glasses pushed up on his head. Amma's chair was empty. Her temple bag was gone from its hook. Saturday seva.

In front of him sat a cup of filter coffee that had gone cold a while ago. Beside it, a manila envelope. No stamp. No postmark. Block capitals:

**R. SOWRI — NEXT OF KIN.**

Sealed. His fountain pen lay across it, cap on. The green Sheaffer. The one he had signed her college forms with.

He looked up. He didn't look surprised. He looked tired, the way a man looks after sitting up all night deciding not to open something.

"Kanna," he said.

Then he heard himself say it. She watched the word land on him. His hand went flat on the envelope.

* * *

The last thing that had called her that was a kitchen light with nobody in it.

For a second her body couldn't tell which one this was. Her shoulders came up. Her jaw set. She made herself look at real things. The fleece. The glasses. The skin on the cold coffee.

It was him.

Nana's twist of salt was still folded in her warm hand. Kino had already done the sill, but Nana had said *you pour it yourself*, so Arenya crouched and tipped it onto the swept step in a short grey line beside his. She didn't hurry. Then she pushed the door the rest of the way open and stepped over both lines.

She didn't sit. She went around the table and put two fingers on the Sheaffer, nothing else, and slid it off the envelope toward his side. Cap still on.

"Don't open that," she said. "And don't sign anything. Not today, not tomorrow, not if it's Amma's handwriting on the front."

Her voice came out clipped. She let it.

"Appa, I'm not here about the note. I know about 2207. I know Aunty closed a box on Monday. I know somebody texted a phone at eleven-fourteen last night with your name on it and the words *we will come to him*." She breathed. "So I'm going to ask you one thing, and I need it to not be *it's handled*. In March, at that counter, when you signed. Who handed you the pen?"

Appa watched the Sheaffer travel back across the table. He didn't pick it up. He took the glasses off his head, folded them, and set them on the envelope where the pen had been. He was an engineer. He liked a thing with weight sitting on paper.

"Not handled," he said. "No. I will stop saying that."

The burner ticked. On the radio someone read the Bay Area traffic to an empty room.

"You know the day already. March nineteenth. The notary by the flower shop. Susi wanted a witness, and I was the only brother she had." He nearly smiled. "Rodel's pen skipped. One of those ballpoints on a chain. It only made a scratch. There was a man behind us in line, very polite, navy coat, a mailbox key in his other hand. He said, *Here, use mine.* He held it out cap first. Like that is good manners."

Appa looked at his right hand on the table.

"I took it. I am a man who takes a pen when one is offered. I wrote the R."

He turned the hand over. A grey stain ran along the inside of his forefinger and the pad of his thumb, where a pen rests. Not ink. Pencil lead. Faint and even. It looked like it had been there for months and had never once come off with soap.

"Susi saw before I got further. She put her hand on my wrist. Not hard. Like this." He laid his left hand over his right wrist to show her. "She took his pen out of my fingers and gave it back to him. She said, *Thank you, we have our own.* Then she took this one out of my shirt pocket." He nodded at the Sheaffer. "She held my wrist while I wrote *Sowri*. I was shaking, and I didn't know why. So the name on her paper is half mine and half his. The R is in his ink."

He breathed out through his nose.

"An hour later, Rodel telephoned to say the same man had rented a box."

Arenya said nothing. Somewhere in her head a line from a declaration lit up. *SIGNATORY ON FILE: R. SOWRI.* Half a signature. Half a family. Half a man in a navy coat.

Appa put a fingertip on the corner of the envelope. "This was on the step at five. Grey dust along the whole step in a straight line, like chalk off a ruler. I swept it. Then I sat with my coffee and decided that if they need a next of kin to sign something tomorrow, it should be me."

For the first time, he looked straight at her.

"They have my R already, Kanna. I am sixty-four. Better my hand than yours. That is what I meant by *handled*."

Behind her, past the two lines of salt, the curry-leaf plant stirred in its pot.

There was no wind.

* * *

"No."

It came out before anything smarter could. She pulled out Amma's chair after all and sat, close enough that their knees almost touched. She took his right hand in both of hers and turned it so the grey faced up between them.

Cold. Not the whole hand. Only along that stain, where a pen would rest.

"Appa, listen to me the way you listen to a load calculation."

He listened. He had always been good at that.

"They don't want *a* next of kin. The text says *custody chain incomplete*. Aunty's paper is half your hand and half his. Your R is already in their ink. If you sign whatever is in that envelope, it isn't you standing in front of me. It's you closing the chain *for* them. That isn't taking the hit. That's handing him the pen back cap first."

Her voice cracked on *cap*. She kept going over it.

"And they wrote *R. Sowri*. Not Raghavan. You know who else is an R. Sowri at this address."

She let that sit for exactly one tick of the burner.

"So nobody in this family signs anything alone. Not you. Not me." She squeezed his hand. "Itzel Lucero says to tell you that you can stop being brave alone now. She said you'd know what she meant. She said you came to her porch in March and wouldn't come in."

She tipped her head toward the door, the salt, the plant moving with no wind behind it.

"Kino laid that. I laid the other one. Come in this time. Get your coat. Leave the envelope under your glasses, and we go get Amma from temple. Daylight. Together."

Kino hadn't moved from the doorway. He stood on the salt, where he could see the street and the plant both. The curry-leaf stirred again and he watched it, not Appa. He waited until she was done.

"Señor Sowri," he said quietly. "Your hand's cold only where he touched it. That's a mark, not a wound. It means they didn't take your R. They lent you theirs."

He nodded at the glasses on the envelope. "Leave it sealed. Don't carry it, don't burn it. My Nana will want to see it with salt around it."

He crouched and held two fingers near the grey stain, not touching. "That dust on your step was a ruler line. Somebody measured this door. Come inside Nana's line, sir. Your daughter's right."

A beat.

"Nobody signs alone."

* * *

It was the name that did it. Itzel's. More than the argument.

Appa's hand went slack in both of hers. For a moment the retired engineer was gone, and a younger brother sat in his place, the one who had been told to wait in the car.

"She asked me to come in," he said. "In March. After the letter. Susi was already inside at Itzel's table with the salt, and Itzel stood at the screen door holding it open. I stood at the bottom of the steps and I thought: *If I go in, I am one of the household. Then they write Lakshmi down. Then they write you.*"

He wasn't looking at her. He was looking at a porch eight months gone.

"So I stayed on the walk like a fool, holding the car keys." He laughed once, wet. "Itzel said, *Raghavan, a man standing outside is still standing somewhere.* I thought she meant it as an insult."

He took his hand back and wiped his eyes on his PG&E sleeve. Then he stood. He moved without hurrying, the way he always had. The coat came off the hook. Car keys in the left pocket, wallet in the right. He left the envelope where it was, glasses folded on top. The Sheaffer he tucked into his shirt pocket and buttoned the flap over it.

He saw her notice.

"Mine," he said. "Only ever mine, from now on."

At the door he stopped at the two lines of salt, white and grey. She watched him remember the bottom step in March.

Then he stepped over them. One long stride. He stood on the swept concrete beside Kino.

The curry-leaf plant went still.

Behind them, in the empty kitchen, the wall phone rang.

The beige one by the fridge, the one only the aunties and the temple still called. It rang once. Stopped.

Then once more. Stopped again.

Like somebody counting.

* * *

She didn't answer it.

She turned in the doorway and looked at the handset on the wall, the coiled cord Amma had stretched across this kitchen for twenty years. She counted the way it had. One, and one.

Two rings. Two strokes keep an entry open until two people stand for it.

"Nobody picks that up," she said, low. "It's asking for someone to answer. Don't give it a voice."

She went back in, because the table still had a thing on it. She took the manila envelope, glasses and all, in both hands, by the edges like evidence, and carried it out over the salt. She put it in Kino's arms. Then she took Appa's elbow. The fleece was rough under her fingers.

"Truck. Kino drives. Malachi behind us the whole way. We go to the temple for Amma first, main road, in the open, and nobody stops."

At the step she looked back once. Cold coffee. An empty chair. The radio still reading traffic to no one. She left the burner on, then pulled the door shut behind her, hard enough that it clicked.

She wiped her thumb on her jeans. It had a little grey on it.

* * *

Kino took the envelope the way she'd handed it, flat in both arms. He didn't look at the writing.

"The burner," he said, and reached back through the gap to turn the knob off. A pot left alone was its own kind of open door. He shut the door and latched it.

He put the envelope on the truck floor behind his seat, under Nana's old blanket, and set a pinch of salt on top. He opened the passenger door for Appa and waited until he was in and belted, the Sheaffer buttoned over his heart.

"Temple," he told Arenya. "Main road. I'll keep Malachi's headlights in my mirror."

He pulled out slowly, past the skeleton in its lawn chair. He watched the blue pot in the mirror until the house was gone.

* * *

Malachi's engine was already running when he got out. He didn't wait for brights. He walked the block at an even pace, hands visible, eyes moving. The beige phone through the kitchen window. The curb. The roofline. The skeleton with fog on its skull.

Nobody on the street. No Nissan.

He stopped at the bottom of the swept step, outside both lines.

"Mr. Sowri," he said evenly. "My name is Malachi. I'm the man who stood outside your daughter's door last week and told her to stop. I was wrong about that." He looked at the envelope in Kino's arms, then at the grey on the concrete. "That line was laid last night at five by someone who knows your door. Whoever polite is, he's watched this house from somewhere I can find. Give me the time it takes you to get in the truck."

To Arenya, lower: "The landline rang twice. Counting. I'll take the temple route behind you, and I'll look at the roofs on the way. If a grey car shows, I don't flash. I pull alongside, and I'm the one it has to explain itself to."

He was already walking the perimeter toward the curb, reading the gutter for leather soles.

Appa had listened without interrupting, the way he used to listen to contractors. "Last week you told her to stop," he said from the truck's open door. "This week you are on my step. Fine. I have hired worse."

He belted in.

Malachi's walk took ninety seconds. The prints were there. Leather soles, size ten or eleven, from the swept step straight to the curb with no wandering. They ended at a dry rectangle in the wet gutter the size of a car.

No car had been parked there.

Round the side, past the recycling bins, he found what the man had come to see. The gas meter by the side door, the one Appa had once written the family's number beside. Its glass face had fogged from the inside. Someone had written in the fog with a fingertip, in neat block capitals:

**R.S. — SUN 9:00**

The dials under it read 4471. The same as before. They hadn't moved.

While he watched, the letters began to run, the way breath does on a mirror. By the time he reached the curb you couldn't have proved they'd ever been there.

He had seen them, though.

He got into his car. Ahead of him the truck pulled away from the curb, Appa in front with the Sheaffer over his heart, the envelope under Nana's blanket with salt on top. Malachi stayed two lengths back, close enough that nothing could get between them.

Behind them the house was shut and the latch was down. In the kitchen, the beige phone didn't ring again.

It was 8:14 on Halloween morning, and the Sowri family's first errand was to go and fetch Amma together.

Halloween came up the hill in costume.

By eight the street was full of children: a dinosaur, two Elsas, a boy in a bedsheet with the eyeholes cut too low. Nana's porch light stayed off. She did not care what the neighbors thought of that.

When a small witch came up the walk anyway, Nana met her at the salt and passed three Mazapanes across the line with the wooden tongs.

"¿Y tú, quién eres?"

"A witch," said the girl, and nothing more.

Nana nodded, satisfied. *Good. Don't give them your name.*

Appa slept on the couch with his stained thumb tucked into his fist. At ten-forty Amma lay down on Nana's bed without being asked, one bare foot sticking out from under the blanket, warm to the touch. The capped Sheaffer made a small hard shape in the pocket of her blouse.

I slept in pieces, sitting on the floor with my back against the bed frame and Amma's foot a hand from my shoulder.

At 2:17 I was awake before the jar was.

*Tk.*

I counted to sixty with my eyes on the ceiling and my notebook shut in my bag, where it belongs. Out on the porch, Caleb said something to the dark, flat and steady. I didn't catch the words. I caught the rhythm. It was the rule, and he was keeping it.

Nobody died. Nobody walked under the streetlight at the bottom of the hill, which dimmed anyway.

I decided that was not a good sign.

* * *

At 7:48 on Sunday morning the fog had made the hill the edge of a page.

I sat at the kitchen table in yesterday's sweater with Nana's coffee going cold in front of me. Nana had taken the comal down from its nail. She laid it on the salt by the side door, iron face down, wooden handle pointing at the street. Then she climbed the step stool and lifted the blue jar off the top shelf with a dish towel and set it on the counter by the door.

Mist beaded on the inside of the glass, like a can of soda in the sun. Dalal's glasses were a dark shape behind it, folded.

The canister stayed on its shelf. Nana didn't look at it. The rest of us did.

I had sent a message through the bond at some point before dawn. *Say it now, before nine.* Abernathy had answered, straight, with no patter. Which told me more than the words did.

The 110 was familiar. Not the route map. The way a lift drops a floor too fast. When the trolley woman said *pink transfer* he had felt known, and what scared him was being recognised. And under the copy's voice, back when it said *Kanna?*, there had been another one, walled off. Patient. It had known his name before he gave it. Something shorter than Abernathy.

*I'm not hiding it, love. I just can't reach it.*

I did not tell him that was fine. It wasn't. I told him to drink the coffee.

Kino was at the door with his boots laced. I put a hand on his sleeve for one second.

"Eight-ten, bottom of the hill. You watch which way he walks and you don't talk to him. If anything looks wrong, you come back up." I swallowed. "I want you back, not heroic."

"Don't take anything he holds out," Kino said. He looked at Amma. "Not even a flyer. Barefoot. Pen in your pocket. Stay behind Arenya, not beside her."

Then he said something to Nana in Spanish, low, and went out into the fog. The grey line on his footprints ticked once under his boot. I watched him until I couldn't.

Malachi stood at the front window with his coat buttoned and his hat in his hand. He had spent the night in Nana's kitchen chair with his back to her wall. He had written 2:17 on his wrist in her grease pencil, because he wasn't permitted a pen of his own. He said he'd be at the curb at 8:40, and that if he touched the brim of the hat, nobody answered the man until he'd finished.

Caleb took the porch glider. He sat in his socks with his hands flat on his knees, away from his pocket, and said, "If the scar goes warm, or the streetlight does that again, you'll hear it before I've finished the sentence."

At 8:20 Appa woke too fast, the way men do in hospital waiting rooms, and asked what time it was. Nobody told him why it mattered. The grey on his thumb had spread overnight past the knuckle and had the faint sheen that pencil lead leaves on skin. He looked at it. He looked at Amma's bare feet on the linoleum. He didn't ask anything else.

Nana put a coffee in his hands and sat between him and the side door. It did not look accidental.

I read Ava's text again. It had been on my screen since 7:49.

*Oven swung SW at 2:17 and has held 5.5 hrs. Not drifting. Pointing. Whatever's coming is coming to you, not past you.*

I thumbed out four words. *Come. Stay at the bottom. Don't climb.* I put the phone face down.

* * *

At 8:39 Caleb's voice came through the screen door, too loud, and then lower.

"Warm. Scar's warm. Not hot. Warm like a hand's been on it."

Everyone went to their places.

I stood inside the gate and didn't cross the salt. Amma came up behind me, barefoot, and I felt her hand settle on my shoulder blade. I put mine over the jar's dish towel, not touching the glass. Malachi went out to the curb with the hat on, stopped a pace short of the salt, and held his empty hands where the street could see them.

Down the hill Kino's voice came up through the fog, carried on purpose, because that was the rule.

"Two coming. Runner first. Dalal-ji behind, no glasses, walking in his prints. I salted the line behind them at the hydrant. Bus hasn't left. Faces in the windows are looking up here."

*Faces in the windows.* I held on to the gatepost.

*Thread's bowstring,* Abernathy said in my ear. *It's not pulling at the jar. It's pulling at Dalal. The jar's the anchor and the old boy's the weight.* A pause, and a flicker at my left shoulder. *Something's listening to him come up. And the runner touched his clipboard when he saw Kino's boots. Like he'd found a name already on the list. Don't give him yours.*

The runner came up out of the grey as an ordinary man. Forties. A shaving nick. A clerk's face at the end of a shift, lanyard turned to the blank side, clipboard flat against his chest. Grey dust on his oxfords. He breathed in when the fog rose and out when it settled, and he never blinked between.

Behind him, with one hand tight on an invisible rail, came Pt. H. Dalal in a white veshti and black dress socks. He squinted at the fog as if it were a bright window. He didn't look for a street sign. He looked for the runner's footprints, and he put each foot inside one, like a blind man on a rope.

I had spent two days wanting to hate him. It's hard to hate a man being towed.

The runner stopped exactly where Malachi had asked, toes one inch short of the comal's wooden handle.

"Return of personal effects," he said. "Proximal eleven. Signatory on file, R. Sowri." He looked at the comal, and the corner of his mouth moved. "A settlement plate. Somebody in this house knows the forms. But a settlement still needs a struck line from the signatory or the alternate. I can't close an entry against an object. Objects don't sign."

"Who is holding my glasses?" Dalal said. His voice was cracked and much older than it had been at the temple. He turned his head, listening. "I can hear them. They are on wood." Then, softer, in Tamil, to no one: *"I could not see the names this morning. For the first time in thirty years, I could not see them."*

On my shoulder blade Amma's hand tightened. Her other hand went toward her pocket.

I reached back without turning and found her wrist and pressed two fingers into it, once. *Not yet.* Her pulse was fast. I left my fingers there.

*He's told you the glasses aren't the trade,* Abernathy said. *They're the opening. He wants you to offer a person. Don't.*

I didn't. Malachi had the floor, and I'd told him he would.

* * *

I couldn't see Malachi's face from the gate. I could see his back, and I could see the moment he touched the brim.

His voice carried, even and unhurried. He let the silence run until the runner's next breath in, and he spoke on the out. He said the R on the entry was not the signatory's hand. It had been written in grey before anyone in this house held a pen. Your own receipt says the chain is incomplete, he said. So I'll ask it the way your office would. When an entry is opened in error by the issuing party, who voids it?

"Not the signatory. The issuer."

He said the effects came back on the plate. The runner would take them, strike the line in his own pencil, and say aloud what he had struck and why. *Opened in error. Returned. Closed.* Nothing on this side of the salt picked up anything of the runner's. Nobody here was his alternate.

"Your bus comes back at nine-twelve," Malachi said. "I'd like you on it."

The runner listened the way a clerk listens to a customer quoting the regulations back at him. He wasn't offended. He was interested. As if very few people had ever got this far.

"The R is in the signatory's hand," he said. "Shaky, but his. The ink is the office's." The fog rose. The fog settled. "Opened in error is a real category."

Malachi said, quietly, "His hand, your ink. Then the error is yours, not his. That's the whole of the category. Say what it costs you before you strike it. I'll hear it, and so will the house."

For the first time anyone had seen, the runner blinked.

"An error isn't erased," he said. "It's returned. The balance goes back up the line to whoever issued the ink. That's somebody's bad week. It isn't yours." He turned the clipboard a few degrees. "But I don't strike alone. A void needs a witness who keeps names, so nobody can say later the clerk made it up." He tipped his head back toward Dalal. "That's him. And he can't keep what he can't see."

Dalal went very still.

"So the effects go back on the plate, and he picks them up himself. He puts them on and he reads. Everything facing him gets read. True names and the other kind." The mouth again. "That's the price of a clean void. Not a signature. *Being seen.* Turn your back and he reads your back, which is nothing. Look at him and he has you."

In the kitchen the jar misted over all at once, and the glasses disappeared into white.

From the porch, flat, Caleb said: "Make him strike, not sign. And let him think it's clean. Hale's team counts closed entries. A void on his paper doesn't count against him. That's why he'll take it."

I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since Friday. Everyone in this yard had a name worth reading.

"Nobody faces him," I said. I didn't recognise my own voice. It was clipped, which is how I know I'm scared. "He told us the rule himself. Turn your back and he reads nothing. Every one of us from this house. A void doesn't need our faces. It needs a witness and a pencil."

"Not Malachi," Malachi said.

He had taken off the hat. He said it was the honest thing to do with it.

"Then I'm what he faces. He can read my name. It's mine to spend." To the house: "Nana, the jar on the wooden side, and the door closed behind you. Nobody hands him anything."

I opened my mouth to argue and found I had nothing. It was the same trade I'd have made if I'd been braver, or if I'd been him.

But I could ask one thing.

"Before you strike," I said to the runner, level, without turning my head. "The nine-twelve tick. Who is it booked for? Say it, or the void isn't clean."

* * *

The jar was cold through the dish towel, cold as a pipe in January.

Nana came as far as the doorframe and held out a wooden spoon, handle first. *"Con madera, mija."* I set the jar on the comal's wooden handle. I lifted the lid with the spoon, and mist poured out low across the salt like breath on a mirror.

Then I turned my back.

So did Amma. So did Appa and Nana in the doorframe, and Kino on the sidewalk, facing the bend where the bus had gone. So did Caleb on the glider, with his face to the siding. Abernathy turned his back so fast he flickered, and then, when that didn't feel like enough, he went into the pantry and put his face an inch from a shelf of Maseca. *Don't say my name,* he'd told me. *Not even in your head, love.* I didn't.

Down at bay C, I learned later, Ava stood with her phone dark in her pocket and her mind full of oat milk, on purpose. If she gave him anything, she'd give him a latte order.

Only Malachi stayed facing the street.

I heard the runner answer my question without turning. "The nine-twelve is booked for me. Me and what I carry back up. The balance sits on my lap, not on any seat of yours. That's the clean answer."

I heard Dalal. "I will read what faces me." The Tamil caught in his throat, and he finished in English. "That is all I have ever done, child. I never once read a back."

I heard socks on bare sidewalk. A hand finding wood. Glass touching glass.

Then nothing.

I faced the kitchen. I counted. I got to four, and I knew only because someone told me afterward what four seconds had cost.

Malachi told me, later, that the old man's eyes didn't stop on his face. They went through it like a finger down a ledger, years at a time, line by line. They stopped.

"Forty seconds," Dalal had said, very quietly. "A market. Heat. You were the one he was looking at." He had blinked behind the lenses. "Someone else wore glasses that day, sir. Not mine. You have been on a page since then, a page that is not ours." A swallow. "Now you are on mine as well. I am sorry. It is a true name. I will keep it properly."

Malachi did not tell me what the name was. I didn't ask.

And then Dalal's gaze had lifted past him, to the house, and read it from its windows and its number. *Lucero.* He mouthed it once, the way you memorize an address. His eyes went to the kitchen window and stayed there, a beat too long, with a small frown. The frown of a man who finds a page in his own book that is blank and shouldn't be.

In the pantry Abernathy felt nothing touch him at all. He told me, later, that this was worse.

"Read," said Dalal.

The runner took a pencil from behind his ear. I heard it from the gate. One long stroke, the length of a line on a form, unhurried.

"Proximal eleven," he said to the house. "Opened in error. Issuer's ink. Returned up the line. Closed."

A second stroke. Shorter. "Alternate struck with it."

Under the pestle on Nana's counter, the family's copy of the form went *tk*.

Amma's hand came out of her pocket, empty. On the couch Appa made a small sound, and I turned my head without meaning to and saw his thumb, and the grey going out of it like a tide going out. A crescent under the nail. Most of it gone.

Caleb said, mid-sentence, "Cool." He said it again, to the siding. "Scar went cool."

Then the fog stopped breathing.

I didn't see what Ava saw. I want that on the record, because I will be asked, by me, for the rest of my life. I had my back to the street and my eyes on Nana's calendar from a Salvadoran bakery. What I felt was the hill itself going quiet, the way a room does when a refrigerator you hadn't noticed shuts off.

* * *

I learned the rest from Ava's legal pad, afterward, in her small block capitals.

*8:48. Fog stopped mid-rise, held one count. Whole bank lifted, Westlake to ocean, higher than fog goes. Something under it turned over. Like a sleeper turning from a light. Then lay down again farther out. Sodium lamps up full.* Underneath, she had written the time again and underlined *turned over*, the way she'd chart an episode in someone else's file.

She had texted me, and I had not read it yet. *The thing in the fog lay back down offshore. I saw it. I'm not going to be cute about it.*

I don't think she was being cute.

The runner tucked the pencil behind his ear. "Good morning," he said, as though it had been one all along, and started downhill toward the shelter and the nine-twelve, keeping to the middle of the sidewalk. Dalal followed in his own steps, glasses on, unsteady. At the hydrant, he stepped over Kino's salt line without being told.

Passing Kino's turned back he said, to nobody, "The box was the other man's. I only keep the names."

Then the fog took them, and the hill was a hill on a Sunday morning, with church bells starting somewhere toward Colma.

I did not turn around until the bells had rung four times. I counted them.

The street was a street: wet sidewalk, a hydrant, a fine white line of Nana's salt, and nothing on it.

* * *

Amma's hand was empty. I took it. It was shaking, and so was mine, and neither of us commented.

Then I was across the kitchen on my knees in front of the couch, turning Appa's thumb to the light. A crescent under the nail. I laughed once, a short ugly sound, and pressed his hand to my forehead.

He said, "In order, kanna," in a voice that wasn't. I don't remember what I said back.

"Nana," I said. "Con madera. Thank you."

When I could stand, I went to the door and looked at Malachi's back. He was still on the curb, hat in his hand.

"Malachi. He has your name now. I'm sorry." I made myself finish. "It was your call, and you made it, and it worked. I was angry on Friday and I'll be angry about the lie for a while. But you stood on the wrong side of the salt with nothing in your hands. Come inside and eat something."

"It was spent on purpose," he said, turning, and nodded once. "Don't apologize for the price."

He put the hat on, because his hand needed something to do. He said the jar and the comal stayed where they were until Nana said, since the glasses had come off wood and it was her wood to lift. Then he came in and stopped in front of the couch and waited until Appa looked up.

"Mr. Sowri. In March my office's paper put its mark on you. I'm sorry for it, and I'm saying it to your face."

Appa regarded him for some time. He did not say it was all right. He was my father. He said, "Sit. You are standing in the draught."

Malachi sat at the end of the table with his back to Nana's wall and took the plate Amma slid toward him without being asked.

*You can come out of the pantry,* I said into the bond. *It's over. Mostly.*

Abernathy came out slowly, the way you come out from under a table after a car backfires, still flickering at the edges. *Blank page,* he said. *At the kitchen window. I felt nothing touch me, love, and I'd rather it had.* And then, because somebody had to: *Your dad's thumb looks better. Go and eat. I'll be here.*

I did not say what I was thinking, which was that I had also felt the window. Not a touch. A *lack*. A place where the morning ought to have been and wasn't.

I put it where I keep things for later.

* * *

Kino came up the hill by the road, not the shelter, with Ava's name in his mouth. He had called down to her before he came. "It's over. Come up the long way. Don't walk past the glass."

At the gate he crouched where Dalal's last grey print had been. It was gone. The sidewalk there was just sidewalk. He put his palm beside it and listened for the Lady, and he told me later that she was a step behind him now. Not turned around. But walking with him.

"Funcionó, Nana," he said in the kitchen. "Pero él vio la casa. Vio el nombre."

"Sí," Nana said. "Ya sé."

Caleb came in last, in his socks, and stopped on the mat inside the door. He didn't go farther. No one had told him he could.

"I'll sit the window with him if she lets me," he said, to Kino and me. "Or I'll stay off it. Your call." Then he said what he'd promised to say. His voice was steady, and I think that cost him more than if it had cracked. "The runner struck an alternate and an R. I helped kill the woman that R was written for. I know that doesn't close anything. I just want it said once, here, in front of Appa."

Nobody moved. The stove ticked.

Appa looked at the crescent under his nail. Then at the man on the mat with a plate held in both hands, a plate Amma had given him anyway.

"That's a good tide," Caleb said, to the thumb.

"Not on the floor," said Appa.

It was the voice he used for bad report cards and the second mortgage. He pushed the chair beside him out with his foot. "She would not have let you eat on the floor. Sit at the table."

That was all. It wasn't forgiveness. It was a place to sit, and in my family that has always come first.

Caleb sat.

Nana went out with the wooden spoon. She lifted the comal by its handle and tipped the empty jar back into the cupboard, mouth first. She did not sweep the salt where the runner's toes had stopped. She covered it, like before. At the gate she looked downhill a long time and crossed herself and came in. Nobody asked what she'd been watching for.

At 9:20 Ava reached the top of the hill with salt in her shoes and a cardboard tray of coffees from the BART cart. She stopped at the gate as if she'd forgotten how gates work. Nana took the tray, looked her over once from shoes to face, said, "Siéntate, doctora," and put a bowl of something with chile in front of her before she could say she wasn't a doctor yet.

"Clinical summary," Ava said to me, low, sitting. Her hands were around the bowl, not the spoon. "I'm fine. I'd like it noted that I am not fine."

"Noted."

"Your dad's hand?"

"Better."

"Good." She looked at her coffee, then at the window over the sink. "Okay. Good."

Amma poured. Malachi ate with his back to the wall. Kino ate standing. Up on the shelf, Susmithaa's canister sat a hand's width from nothing.

Somewhere in the Hillcrest morning a bus was climbing a hill that wasn't ours, with a clerk on it and a balance on his lap, riding up the line to the man who issued the ink. I thought about whoever was going to open that envelope. *Somebody's bad week.*

I thought: good.

Later, when the plates were cleared and the kitchen windows had steamed over from the stove, Kino carried a cup of salt to the sill. I was beside him. I saw it at the same moment.

At eye height, on the inside of the glass, where nobody had been standing, someone had wiped a clean circle the size of a palm in the condensation.

It matched the one in the shelter at the bottom of the hill.

Kino didn't say anything. He set down the salt, and he laid his own palm flat in the circle, not touching the glass, and held it there. Outside, the fog was nothing but fog.

"After we eat," he said at last. "I want to sit this window. Alone. An hour."

I looked at the circle. It was already starting to fog over again, from the edges in.

"Okay," I said.

It was the first thing all morning I'd said that I wasn't sure was true.

Appa didn't take the freeway. He took 280 the slow way, where the fog lay in the cuts between the hills like something poured and left to set.

The wipers went on intermittent. Then off. Then on again.

He drove at exactly the limit, both hands on the wheel, ten and two, the way he had taught her and the way she never did it.

He hadn't asked what happened in there. The thermos rode between the seats, steel and dented, and he still hadn't offered it.

At the Westlake exit he signaled and pulled into a Chevron. The pumps glowed yellow against the grey.

"Petrol," he said, though the needle sat above half. He got out, then leaned back down into the open door and looked at Kino in the back seat, and then at his daughter, the way he used to look at a breaker panel before deciding which switch to throw.

"Five minutes. I am going inside for the—" He gestured at the store. "Whatever they have."

The door shut. Through the glass she watched him walk past the pump without stopping and into the shop, where he stood in front of the coffee machines and did not touch any of them.

It was warm in the car. The engine ticked. Her left palm was still cool, but only cool now. Not counting.

On the dash, the clock read 7:58.

It was the first time since Wednesday that the two of them had been alone with no one listening who wasn't supposed to be. In the bond, Abernathy was quiet. He could have spoken. She had the impression he was choosing not to, and that it cost him something.

"He's not buying gas," Arenya said, low. "He's giving us five minutes." She turned the thermos over in her hands without opening it. "Kino. Hale nodded at us. Through a van, through a pole, through the fog. He knew which window."

She got out the notebook and wrote the time. Then stopped, the pen hovering.

"Here's what's bugging me. That call was a recording of polite. The visitor writes through people. So is Hale a man, or is he a form? Something it fills in when it needs a hand?" She looked back at him. "And I said no three times in there and it felt great, and I don't trust that. Nothing has been that easy since Wednesday. What did we pay? You saw more than I did."

Kino didn't answer right away. He watched Appa through the glass, the man at the coffee machines as if they were a panel he hadn't been cleared to open. He didn't lean between the seats. He stayed in the back with his forearms on his knees, and turned his left wrist over so the birthmark faced up.

"Your dad's giving us five minutes," he said. "I'm going to use them. I've been carrying two things since Livermore and I'm tired of carrying them."

He said it level, the way he'd read a trail closure aloud.

"One. The notebook. It isn't just a notebook. When you say something true out loud, it gets heavier. I watched it at the meter. I watched it again this morning, when you told Rodel *nothing accepted*." His thumb rested on the spots. "It isn't answering to the Lady. It isn't answering to Nana. It's answering to *you*. I should've told you Wednesday. I didn't, because I wanted to be sure. Being sure isn't my job. Telling you is."

On her knee, the page lay open to *7:58* and an empty line. It didn't move. It didn't need to. She could feel the weight of it settle against her thigh, the way a phone in your pocket gets heavier when you know it's about to ring.

"Two. This one's harder." He looked at the fog sitting in the cut past the pumps, not at her. "For weeks, every time I dream on the mountain, the Lady's there. And she's got her back to me. Not angry. Just turned. Looking at something I can't see. I haven't told Nana. I haven't told anybody."

A short, dry sound that wasn't quite a laugh.

"Twenty years of fasting and night-walks, and I can't get her to look over her shoulder."

Then he did look at Arenya.

"I'm not telling you so you'll fix it. I'm telling you because you went into that shop and said no to a man nobody else could see. You're owed somebody who isn't keeping things from you. So I'm done." A beat. "Your turn, if you want one. Or we can just listen to the engine tick."

A truck went by on Serramonte. The windows hummed with it, then went quiet.

Inside the Chevron, Appa had picked up a cup. He held it under the spout and studied the buttons. The cashier said something and he shook his head politely and kept standing there. He was buying them time, and doing it badly, which was the only way he knew how.

"Okay," Arenya said. "You still haven't answered me, though. What did it cost?"

Kino took it the way he'd take a trail report. Slowly. All of it.

"Three things I saw." A finger. "The Nissan's window fogged from the inside only while we were in the shop. Nobody breathing in there. So something was sitting in that seat, keeping it warm. Then Hale gets out at 7:40 like he'd been poured into the coat. A man doesn't do that. A form does. I think you're right. I think Hale's what gets filled."

A second finger.

"The nod. He didn't look for us. He looked at the gap. He knew where the car was because we told him. Rodel wrote it in a book that wasn't his, and Hale read it." He glanced at the notebook on her knee. "Everything we write is a place somebody can find us."

He lowered his hand.

"As for what it cost." He was quiet a moment. "I don't think the no was free. I think it was *entered*. You said it true, so it counted, and what counts gets kept. Somewhere there's a line that says *Arenya Sowri declined*, and a clerk who never errs has it. That's good. It's also your name on a page the other side can read."

The pump light caught the four brown spots.

"My cold patch is still a four. Yours stopped counting. I'd trust that less than I'd trust it hurting." He looked toward the shop. "And if Hale's a form, who's holding the pen? Dalal's hook on the *y* ties him to that check. I want to know if that hand's in a hurry. Bell kept to the calendar. Every March, like a train. This one moved up. Somebody got impatient, Ari."

She looked at his wrist for a long moment. Four spots. Nothing clever came, which almost never happened.

"Okay," she said. "Your Lady first, because you went first."

She closed her thumb over the pen.

"I don't know anything about mountains. But I've written about a lot of dogs. And a dog that turns its back on you in a doorway isn't ignoring you. It's standing between you and whatever's coming up the walk." She shrugged, embarrassed. "That's a listicle, not theology. But you said she isn't angry. Maybe she's just busy *facing* something. Maybe it's the same something we are."

Then she looked down at *7:58* and the empty line, heavy against her leg, waiting.

"My turn. The Tartine footage. The thing that started all of this. I didn't get it with a press pass." She kept her eyes on the page. "Saiesh and I cloned Teddy's keycard. I invented a company project called Urban Mortality Patterns and requested the footage under his credentials. His name, my hand."

A breath that wasn't a laugh.

"Do you hear it? That's what Dalal does. He writes *R. Sowri* on the back of a check. He writes through Rodel's pencil. He borrows somebody's number for the paperwork. I did the exact same thing with a badge reader and a Google Form, because I wanted to be the one who found it. Not *a* person. *Me.*"

She pressed the pen cap down until it clicked.

"So if the clerk keeps true things, it has that too. Somewhere under Teddy's name there's a line in my handwriting. I'd rather you heard it from me in a Chevron lot than find out the way Appa found out about the check." She glanced at the shop. Appa was still holding an empty cup under the spout. "And you're right. Everything we write is a place they can find us. Aunty said writing is not keeping."

She looked at the page. Then, slowly, she wrote anyway, in her own hand.

*7:58. Told Kino the truth. Nothing signed for anyone else.*

"If they're going to find me," she said, "they can find me there."

The pen dragged on the last word, as if the paper had gone thicker beneath it.

The new line didn't look like the time above it. *7:58* was her ordinary quick scrawl. *Nothing signed for anyone else* sat darker and slightly indented, the way writing looks when it's been pressed through from the other side of the page. She hadn't pressed that hard.

Then her left palm, the spot that had gone quiet past Glen Park and stayed numb all morning, turned warm.

It didn't hurt. It felt like a coin that had been held in someone's fist. One slow pulse. Then it stayed.

Whatever had stopped counting her had started again. And this time it was a number she had put there herself.

Between them, Kino's wrist lay turned up in the flat light. For half a second the four brown spots looked less like a receipt and more like a signature. Four marks, no name beside them.

He didn't pull the arm back. Something in his shoulders let go.

"Thanks for the dog," he said. "It's better than anything I've got. Facing the walk." He nodded slowly. "I'll take that to the mountain."

Then, quieter: "The keycard. Okay. You told it plain, and I'm not going to say it was nothing, because it wasn't. But you wrote it down knowing they can read it, in your own hand, with your own name. Dalal can't do that. He only signs as somebody else." He looked at the page. "That line didn't come out looking like a confession. It came out looking like a witness."

Across the lot, through the fog, Appa was coming back with his head down and a cup in his hand. He had paid in coins, she'd seen it, counting them onto the counter one at a time. He picked up a second cup, thought about it, and put it back.

"When he opens the door, say nothing about Teddy," Kino said. "Let him have his coffee. Tonight, though, when you can, tell Teddy yourself. Before somebody holding a pen does it for you."

He sat back and turned his wrist down at last as the driver's door handle clicked.

* * *

Appa got in with the damp on his shoulders and the smell of burnt Chevron coffee following him. He put the cup in the holder and didn't drink it. He checked the mirrors in order, left, center, right, the way he had every time he'd started a car for forty years, and pulled out onto Serramonte without a word.

The on-ramp looped them up into the fog, and then they were on 280 heading south. The hills on either side were only shapes. Reflectors ticked past.

After a mile he said, to the windshield, "The thermos is rasam. Your amma's. I warmed it at five."

A pause while the lane markers went by.

"I didn't give it before because you were going in there. You don't eat before an exam."

Another mile. The wipers made one pass, though it wasn't raining.

"Did it go okay?"

He asked it the way he'd ask about a transformer inspection, plainly, ready for a bad answer. His hands stayed at ten and two. He didn't look over.

"You don't have to tell me what it was. Just whether it went okay."

In the back, Kino looked out his window and kept quiet.

Arenya reached between the seats and picked up the dented thermos. Still warm. She unscrewed the lid and the smell of tamarind and pepper filled the car, and for a second she couldn't talk.

"It went okay, Appa." She poured a capful and held it in both hands. "I said no. Three times. Nobody signed anything, and nobody got added to anything. Rodel's fine. He's scared, but he's fine. He's getting a new lock."

She took a sip. It was exactly as peppery as it was supposed to be.

"You were right about the exam thing. I'd have thrown up." She watched his hands on the wheel. "And Appa? The 'it's handled' thing. I'm still mad about that. But you waited till 7:40 and you didn't ask me to stop. That was the right answer."

She held the cap out across the gearshift, the way she used to when she was nine and he drove with one hand.

"Drink some. You haven't had anything since five."

He looked at the cap for a long moment, long enough that the car drifted a few inches toward the lane line. Then he took it with his right hand and drove with his left, the way he wasn't supposed to and always did.

He drank it in one go and handed it back.

"Too much pepper," he said. "She always made it too much pepper."

His voice caught on *always*, very slightly, the way a needle skips. He cleared his throat and put both hands back at ten and two.

He said nothing about *it's handled*. A mile later, at the Crystal Springs exit, he reached over without looking and turned the heater up one notch on her side.

That was as close as he'd come to an answer, and they both knew it.

Past the reservoir the fog thinned and then gave way all at once to sun on the dry gold hills. Light flooded the windshield. Kino shut his eyes against it.

She had driven this stretch before and felt everything go quiet: the dot, the dime, the cold. This time the warm spot in her palm stayed. It didn't fade at the county line. It didn't flicker. It rode south with her, steady as a pulse.

The one entry in all of this that was written in her own hand.

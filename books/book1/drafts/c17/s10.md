Halloween was happening on the hill.

You could hear it without seeing it. A plastic pumpkin rattled two streets over. A kid yelled *trick or treat* at a door that didn't open. Out past Fort Funston the foghorn kept time, low and patient, like it had somewhere to be and wasn't in a rush.

Nana's porch light stayed off. She didn't hand out candy and never had. "They can go to the Guzmáns'."

Kino's truck ticked as it cooled at the curb. Malachi stood at the gate with the latch untouched and his hat in both hands at his belt. The salt line ran white under the gate, a finger's width from the toes of his shoes.

He had stopped exactly there.

He knew where to stop.

Caleb hadn't left the glider. He was watching the street behind Malachi, not Malachi, and rubbing his thumb along the scar at his crown like he was trying to start a fire.

Through the window over the sink I could see Amma at the table, both feet flat on the linoleum, the green Sheaffer in its jar by her elbow. Nana stood at the stove with her back to the door, stirring something that did not need stirring.

"Arenya," she said, without turning. "Your water's still salty. Dry your hands before you open my gate for a man."

I dried them on the dish towel, front and back, between the fingers. The smell of salt stayed anyway. Then I went out through the screen door and down the steps and stopped at the gate, on my side of the line, with the latch between us.

"Hat in your hands," I said. "That's new."

He didn't answer. He was good at that.

"Here's how this goes. You don't cross until you've said two things to my face, out here, where my grandmother can hear you through the window. Your real name. And who pays you. Not 'a concerned party.' A name." I glanced back at the kitchen, at Amma's flat feet. "Then you go in and introduce yourself to my mother. She's heard worse from me today. She's been very patient about it."

I put my hand on the latch and didn't lift it. "The notebook stays shut. Go ahead."

* * *

Malachi stayed where the salt stopped him. He didn't lean on the gate.

"Mrs. Lucero. Arenya." He said it to the screen door, evenly, so the kitchen could hear. "I'll wait until I'm asked. A man who needs inviting shouldn't be in a hurry about it."

Then, quieter, to the window and the woman at the table: "Mrs. Sowri. My name is Malachi Shawani. I told your daughter I was Department of Energy. That was a lie. I work for the Central Intelligence Agency, and I was sent to this city to watch people die of something I'd seen before."

Kino had come down off the steps. He stood a pace behind my shoulder on the inside of the salt, arms loose, and he didn't look at Malachi's face. He looked at the hat. At the coat pocket with its small folded bulge.

"You gave her half," Kino said, low. "Give the other half. Then tell my Nana-in-the-window whether the Agency knows you're standing at a gate in Daly City, or whether you're out here on your own paper."

Malachi turned the hat a quarter turn. "Malachi Shawani. That's my real name. It's on a passport and on a file I'd rather you never see. Who pays me is the Central Intelligence Agency. Operations. I'm a case officer, detached to a small domestic cell that was never meant to exist on paper. The Energy card was ours. It's on the dashboard of a car I'm not going back to."

"And the Agency?"

"My team knows I'm off the grid. They think I'm working a lead. They don't know I've told a civilian family what we're looking at, and I'd like them to keep not knowing." He met Kino's eyes. "So no. This is my own paper, and it's thin. If it comes out, it costs me, not you."

From the glider, Caleb's voice came flat across the dark. "Scar's cool. Reporting it. Nine minutes since the last time." A pause. "CIA. Okay." A short breath that wasn't a laugh. "I'd have guessed worse. All I can confirm is he drove me out Friday and didn't ask for anything. It isn't a reference."

"Wow," I said. "Bronze medal for the cover story. Department of Energy."

It came out dry, because dry was what I had. Then I looked past him at the window, at the woman sitting very still in it, and the dryness went out of me.

"You lied to my face in my own apartment," I said. "I'm not going to pretend that's fine because you took your hat off."

I lifted the latch and swung the gate open, and stayed planted in the gap.

"Here's the deal. Nana decides where you sit. You tell my mother what you told me, including the lie, and you don't ask her to forgive it. You tell us everything your team knows about the runner and about nine o'clock. You leave one thing out and I find it later, you're done here." A beat. "And your team doesn't hear about this family from you, or from anyone who talks to you. That's the price of the invitation."

I stepped aside, one pace.

"Come in, Malachi Shawani. Step over the salt. Don't scuff it."

* * *

The screen door creaked behind me. Nana's voice came through the two inches of open window, not to him, to the pot.

"Central Intelligence. In my mother's time they were the ones who came in the good cars." The spoon went around once. "The boy asked you two things, mijo. Answer the boy. Then answer her. Then I'll think about my gate."

Malachi answered Amma first. He'd have known that was the order that mattered. She'd asked from the table, politely, in her parent-teacher voice, whether he had watched her Susmithaa.

"No, ma'am. I wasn't there." He didn't look away from the window. "I'd like to say I would have stopped it, but I don't know that I could have. I read about her afterward, at a desk, in a file that had a number where her name should have been. I learned her name later than I should have, from your daughter."

He stepped over the line in one long stride, high and clean. His heel didn't touch the salt. His coat didn't touch the gate. On the far side he stopped on the walk and waited to be told where to stand.

The salt noticed anyway.

Where his shadow had passed over it under the porch light, a short stretch of the white line had gone grey. A hand's width. The color of pencil rubbed on a thumb. It didn't smoke or hiss. It looked like something that had been written there and erased.

Kino saw it first. Then Caleb, from the glider: "Scar's still cool." He sounded less sure than he had a minute ago.

Nana came down one step in her house slippers, the wooden spoon still in her hand. She looked at the grey patch for a long moment. Then she looked at the left pocket of Malachi's coat.

"Not you," she said. "What you carry." She held out her free hand, palm up and steady. "Give them here, the way they are, in the cloth. Don't unwrap them on my walk." She didn't turn her head. "Arenya. The blue jar under the sink, the empty one with the lid. *Ándale.*"

Behind her, through the open door, Amma had stood up. Her feet were bare on the kitchen floor. She was looking at Malachi with the face she'd worn at Susmithaa's graduation, the one that held so completely still that nothing could leak out of it.

I touched her elbow on my way past. I didn't say anything. There wasn't anything that would have been the right size.

Under the sink I found the blue jar and wiped the inside with a clean corner of the towel. When I came back out, carrying it in both hands, Malachi was taking the handkerchief out of his pocket with two fingers, by the knot.

"As they are, Mrs. Lucero," he said, and laid it across her palm without letting the cloth open. "Cord uncoiled, left on the lamp table at the Fremont hall. I haven't put them on. I haven't let anyone else touch them." He stepped back off the walk onto bare concrete and gave the grey salt room. "If it's me that's marked it, I'll stand wherever you tell me until it's mended."

He set his hat on the porch rail, crown down. Kept his hands open and visible while Nana's fingers closed.

I held the jar open under her hand. "Cloth and all," I told him quietly. "Like she said."

The handkerchief went into the jar the way a sleeping cat goes into a box: one soft fold, and then it was still. I pressed the lid down and held it there with my palm, the way I'd held Amma's foot.

Inside the blue glass, a mist rose from the bottom up, as though someone had breathed into it. And through the mist came a sound almost too small to hear.

*Tk.*

A pencil point, touching paper, once.

The glass cleared.

Nobody on the porch said anything. Kino's eyes met mine and went back to the pocket that was now empty.

Nana took the jar from my hands and held it at arm's length, the way she carried the hot comal, in the hand that wouldn't be signing anything. "The glasses," she said, answering the question I'd asked at the gate. "Not him. A man's shadow doesn't stain my salt. What's in his pocket does." She looked at Malachi over the lid. "You carried them clean. *Bueno.* It still marks. It just marks less."

"Pour it, mija. Fresh salt over the old." She tipped her head at the grey. "Don't sweep it. You cover it. Sweep it and you've only moved it."

I knelt at the gate with the canister and poured, slowly, a finger wide, the same as the rest of the line. The white settled over the grey. The grey did not come up through it. But it didn't fade underneath, either. You could still see where it was, the way you can see a scar under a sleeve.

I said it out loud, since I'd said I would. "It's still there. It's under. It's not going anywhere."

"*Bueno,*" said Nana, and carried the jar inside.

* * *

She set it on the top shelf, next to Susmithaa's canister, not touching. A hand's width apart.

I watched her do it. Then I washed my hands at the sink again, salt and all, and said low enough that it was only for her, "Nana. Something's pulling on the jar. Thin. Downhill, toward the bus stop. It wasn't there before the lid went down."

She didn't turn around. The spoon went around once.

*Oi. Oi, love.* Abernathy, in the bond, in the voice that was trying so hard not to be worried. *Don't look at the shelf. Look at your mum's plate and listen with the side of your head.*

I looked at Amma's plate. I put a tostada on it.

*That thread's finer than anything we've seen and it's pulled tight. West and a bit south. The 110 stop. I'm not saying it's a coincidence, I'm saying I'd rather it was.* A breath he didn't need. *The glasses are his, and he keeps names. Somebody on the far end just felt a name get shut in a box. That tick was a pencil checking a line. We've been counted again, guv.*

*Don't cut it. Cutting tells whoever's holding it you can feel it. Salt it, leave it, put Caleb on the porch to watch the stop.* A pause. *And eat something. Pozole. I can't, so you're doing it for both of us.*

And then, smaller, in the lower register that he never let me hear for long: *And that voice. The walled-off one. I haven't forgotten I owe you it. Not tonight. After he's done talking.*

The clock over the fridge said 6:41. Nana pointed the spoon at the far end of the table, at the chair with its back to the wall.

"Sit. Eat." There was pozole, steaming, and a plate of tostadas, and the lime already cut. "Then talk. Lakshmi listens, and so do the rest of you. Nobody interrupts him until he's finished."

* * *

Malachi ate three spoonfuls. Properly. Not for show, because he was told to.

Then he set the spoon across the rim and folded his hands on the table where my mother could see them, and he told it.

Eleven years ago. A city he wouldn't name, because the name didn't matter here. A man who looked at someone across a market for forty seconds and then died, and a file that said *cerebral event*. A report Malachi wrote that said otherwise. The report came back with one line on it, telling him to forget.

"I did," he said. "That's the first thing I'm ashamed of."

Then this spring. The same deaths, here. Nine his cell counted, each of them locking eyes with someone, holding for half a minute or a minute, and going. Four people and a budget line nobody reads. "They know about the nine. They know about the eyes. They don't know about salt, or pencil, or this house. Nothing that matters tonight came from them. It came from your daughter, and from walking."

He counted the rest out on the table with one finger. The text at 11:14 Friday, signed with a shepherd's crook: *lost property, custody chain incomplete, Sunday the first, we will come to him.* The fog on the side meter: *R.S., Sun 9:00*, dials reading 4471. Dalal at the temple, saying a runner comes at nine. The form with its pencilled R that wasn't Appa's hand. The oxfords in San Bruno, lifted straight up. The pink transfer. The *8:12* beside the first Sunday bus at Daly City station, which put a man at the bottom of the hill around 8:40. The phone in the hall at 3:12.

"Nobody answered," he said. "That was right."

What he didn't know, he said plainly, and he said it where she could hear: who the runner worked for, what happened if nobody signed, what the balance did if it carried forward. "I won't guess at those in front of you."

Then the last thing, and I had told him *everything*, so I watched him decide to do it.

"Earlier this week I ran a query through a highway patrol system to find your daughter's car. It's on record with my name on it. If anyone from my agency comes to this door, that's how. I'll do everything I can to keep it buried. I can't promise it stays buried."

He laid his hands flat.

"Tomorrow I'll be at that curb, outside the salt, where he has to stand. Nobody in this house signs anything, answers that phone, or carries that form past the line. That's my plan. It's yours to change."

The clock moved from 6:44 to 6:52. In the living room Appa turned over on the couch and didn't wake.

Amma picked up the Sheaffer, turned it end over end once, and set it back down square with the table edge.

"Nine," she said. Her accent had come forward, the way it did when she was tired or very angry, and she was both. "Nine that your four people count. And for eleven years you forgot, because someone wrote one line on your paper." She looked at the pen. "My husband also signed what someone put in front of him."

That was all she said about it. It wasn't forgiveness, and it wasn't a sentence. It went down on the table next to the pen, and Malachi, to his credit, let it lie.

"I have one question," she said. "Then something to tell you." She lifted her chin toward the hall, where the form lay under the pestle on its bed of salt. "That paper has my name on it as well. *L. Sowri*, with a tick. If this man comes at nine for Raghavan and nobody signs, you don't know what happens. Fine. But Raghavan is not the only name. So if nobody signs for him, does he ask for me?"

She didn't wait for him to say *I don't know* again.

"Because this is what I am telling you. Tomorrow my husband sleeps on that couch. If anyone in this house goes to that door, it is me. Not my daughter. Not this boy. Not you, Mr. Shawani." Her eyes came to me and stayed. "I am the one with the pencil already next to my name."

"No."

It came out before I'd decided to say it, too loud for Nana's kitchen. I put my hand flat on the table next to hers, not on it, and made myself go slower. The way I would if I were laying out evidence for someone who'd already decided I was wrong.

"Amma. Listen to me like I'm wrong and you're checking. The form doesn't say *Raghavan or Lakshmi*. It says *received by, next of kin*, then *alternate signatory*, with a tick already next to you. Who ticks a box before anyone has signed? Someone who expects to need it." My voice cracked. I kept going. "Appa's R is in pencil, in someone else's hand. That's one. If you walk to that door tomorrow you aren't standing in front of him. You're the second. You're the person they ticked the box for."

I looked at her foot under the table, bare on the linoleum, and then back at her face.

"I held that cold in my hands all afternoon. It went up to my wrist. I am not giving it back to you at nine tomorrow so you can go and be brave at a gas meter." I breathed. "Nobody signs alone and nobody signs for anyone. That was the rule this morning and it's still the rule."

I turned to the stove, to Nana's back. "You said *trade.* He's lost his eyes and he'll come for them first. So we don't give him a name. We give him back his glasses, at the curb, outside the salt. In return he takes the receipt and doesn't come back up this hill." To Malachi: "You'll be at the curb. Fine. Then I'm there too, on the inside of the line, because I'm the one who started reading his paperwork."

And to Amma, quieter: "You can stand behind me. Not in front. Please."

Malachi let a moment pass before he spoke. She'd earned that. "I don't know whether he asks for you. I won't pretend to. But I know how paper like this works when it's built to be signed. It's built to be signed by whoever is nearest and most willing. You've just told me you're the one most willing. That makes you the one he'll lean on hardest." He glanced at Nana, then back. "If someone has to stand at the curb, it's me. Without a pen. Without a name. If he asks for L. Sowri, I tell him she isn't available and make him say what he'll do about it in front of witnesses. I'm better at making people say things than anyone else in this kitchen."

Kino had come in from the doorway and stopped at the end of the table where Amma could see him without turning her head. "Auntie." Quietly. "Nobody's taking that from you. You've earned the right to stand at that door. But the box with your name was ticked before you had a say. I'd rather not hand him the one person who'd walk out there and be brave."

He laid out the rest like a trail. Malachi at the curb. Arenya inside the line. Caleb on the glider that looked down the hill. Himself at the bottom of the hill by 8:10, in the shelter, before the 110 let anyone off. "I want to see which way he walks, and whether he walks. If he steps up the hill, I come up behind him and he's got no way back down the bus line." And, flat, to Malachi: "Nana says yes first. And nobody hands him anything across the salt. If he wants his eyes he sets the jar down and walks backward."

From the mat by the door, Caleb asked if he could say one thing about the form. He waited for Amma's nod, or for the spoon to say no.

"Don't let anyone tell you it's only paper. I've seen the other end. A runner doesn't take the signature, he takes the stub. The stub tells the van the canister was delivered. The lift counts, the file closes. If the form carries forward it stays open, and open means somebody keeps coming. The tick next to your name is how the open file picks the next person." He looked at the jar on the shelf, then at the floor. "If Shepherd's quiet tomorrow, that isn't good news. He was quiet before the Nissan. Scar's cool as of now. I'll say it every time so you can hold me to it."

"Kino's rules are better than mine," I said. I picked up my spoon, finally, because Abernathy was right and my hands wanted a job. It was very good. I didn't say so yet. "Kino at the shelter by 8:10. Caleb on the glider. Malachi at the curb. Me behind the gate. Amma behind me." I let that last one stand without looking at her. "Anyone sees anything that isn't on this list, they say it out loud. No one goes quiet and brave."

Malachi picked up his own spoon. "Yes. That's the order." Then he added two things. A signal: his hat brim touched, or the hat taken off, meant he'd heard something the whole line needed to hear, and nobody answered the runner until he'd finished. And a rule. "Nobody says Susmithaa's name, or Appa's, or Mrs. Sowri's, within his hearing. Names are what he keeps. If he asks something I can't answer, I say I'll consult and step back across to you. We decide on our side of the salt."

To Amma, last: "Thank you for hearing me out. I'd have walked away if you'd told me to."

Amma looked at my palm, turned up on the table beside hers, for a long time.

Then she put her hand into it. Not gently. It was the grip she'd used for crossing El Camino when I was six.

"Behind you," she said. "Yes."

She looked around the table: Kino in the doorway, Caleb on the mat, the CIA man with his spoon set down. "But not in shoes. I will stand barefoot, the way I stood this morning, and I will keep this in my pocket." The Sheaffer, capped. "Not to write. So that I know where it is."

Then, lower, only for me: "Eat, kanna. You said so yourself."

She picked up her spoon. It was the first thing she'd eaten since the truck.

* * *

Nana turned off the burner. She turned around for the first time since Malachi sat down, the spoon in her hand like a pointer, and went through us one by one. Kino. Caleb. Malachi. Me.

"Yes," she said. "To the trade. With my terms."

The spoon came up.

"One. The jar goes down on the curb on my comal. The wooden side. Not from anybody's hand. He picks it up off the wood, or he doesn't get it. Two." The spoon pointed down the hall, where the pestle sat on its salt. "The form doesn't leave this house. He doesn't get our copy. If he wants it closed, he closes it on *his* paper, in front of you, with his own pencil, through his own line. He crosses it out. Not us."

The spoon found Malachi. "You make him say that he's done it. Out loud. You told us you're good at that."

"Three. Nobody here says the dead girl's name to him." Her mouth went flat. "He hasn't earned it."

She set the spoon in the sink.

"Now eat, all of you. Caleb, sit down, that is a chair, not a test. And at eleven everybody sleeps. Except whoever is on the glider."

Caleb sat. He looked surprised by the chair, as though it had been put there by somebody who'd planned on him.

I ate. The pozole was very good, and I told Nana so, and she waved the compliment away without looking at me, which is how you know she took it.

Up on the top shelf the blue jar sat quiet. Beside it the canister, a hand's width off. In the bond the grey thread hung taut and still down the hill, like a fishing line something was holding but not yet pulling.

Out on the porch the glider creaked. Down at the bottom of Hillcrest the shelter light was on, and nobody was in it.

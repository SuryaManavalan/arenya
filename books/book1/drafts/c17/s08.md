280 South, 9:24 a.m.

The fog hadn't burned off. It sat on the hills like wet wool, and Kino's truck smelled of coffee and old floor mats. The heater only worked on high.

Two car-lengths back in the mirror, Malachi's headlights hung steady as a stitch.

Appa sat up front with his grey hand on his knee, palm down, as if he were hiding it from himself. The envelope was under the seat behind him, wrapped in Kino's jacket. Everyone in the truck knew exactly where it was.

In the back, Ix had put his chin on Amma's knee. She hadn't let go of her temple bag. She was turned toward me instead of the road.

She had asked once. She wasn't going to ask twice.

*Since when* was still in the cab with us, riding along with the heater noise.

The Colma exit came up on the right. Rodel's office was two minutes off it. Kino put his signal off and kept both hands on the wheel.

"Rodel's keeps till Monday, Appa. Nana's first." Then, lower, in the voice he uses on frightened hikers: "Amma, whatever she tells you is going to sound crazy. I've seen it with my own eyes and I'm still saying it's true. Let her get to the end before you decide anything."

He turned the heater down one notch. It did nothing. He let the road be quiet.

I laid the pink slip on my knee, ink side down, and kept two fingers on it in case it tried to slide off somewhere.

"August," I said. "A man on the Montgomery BART platform stopped and stared at a woman across the tracks for forty seconds. Then he fell down. They called it an aneurysm. That afternoon I wrote *Eight Low-Shed Breeds for Seniors* and couldn't stop thinking about him."

I breathed.

"There've been nine since then, Amma. All the same way. I kept a wall in my apartment. String, printouts, everything. I took it down before you came over for Deepavali so you wouldn't worry. I photographed it first."

Nobody said anything. I kept going.

"I also committed a crime. I copied Teddy's keycard so I could pull café footage. He knows now. I might not have a job on Monday." The cab was so hot I could hardly breathe, and I let it be. "That's the small part."

I looked at the back of Appa's head, not his hand.

"In March, Susi-athai paid a hundred and one dollars for an archana in my name. My star, our gotra. That same month a polite man in a navy coat handed Appa a pen. I think that's how we got written down. I think that's why she's—"

My voice snagged. I made it keep going.

"Why she's on Nana's shelf and I'm in this truck."

I turned the slip over so the blue *ROHINI* faced her.

"And since this morning, you're on it too. So you get everything, out loud. Nobody writes any of it down, because whatever we write, they can read. And nobody in this car says *handled* again."

* * *

280 ran empty and gold.

Amma didn't interrupt once. One hand stayed on Ix's head. She had no idea he was there, and she stroked the air just above his ears anyway. She didn't move at *nine*. She didn't move at *crime*.

At *Susi-athai*, her thumb stopped.

She took the slip by its corner, the way she'd take a hot plate, and read it twice. Then she folded it along its old crease and handed it back.

"March," she said. Her voice was very level. "Susi called me. A Sunday. She said she was doing a family archana at Livermore, for health, and the priest wanted everyone's star. I was making dosa. I told her yours from memory, and Kino's, and your father's."

She looked at the back of Appa's head.

"I didn't say mine. She didn't ask for mine." A breath. "So somebody else knew it."

"Lakshmi—" Appa's grey hand was flat on his thigh. He didn't turn.

"No. You had since March."

She didn't raise her voice, which made it worse. Then, to me, gentler, in Tamil first and English after, like when we were small: "*Kanna.* What do they want her *for*? Not how. I heard how. For what?"

Ix lifted his chin off her knee. His ears went flat.

Behind the seat, under the jacket, the envelope hadn't moved. But the cab had gone cold along the floor. A draft with no window open, the way a room cools when a door opens somewhere else in the house.

The green sign came up: **Hillcrest Blvd — 1 mile.**

"I don't know all of it, Amma," I said. "I'll tell you what I do know, and I'll mark which is which."

The cold reached my ankles through my socks. I kept my voice level anyway.

"Something keeps accounts. Nana calls it El Contador. It's a clerk. It writes people into a ledger, and an entry that's open stays open until two people stand for it. They don't want Appa's pen to hurt him. They want it to *finish* the page. A countersignature from next of kin closes the chain. That's the word on the text they sent, and it's why they said they'd come to him." I swallowed. "Closed entries get collected. I don't know exactly by whom. Bell, maybe. That's a guess, and I'm saying it's a guess."

"For what, I can't tell you. I can tell you what it did to Susi-athai. She was the hand in the R. She steadied his wrist."

I made myself look at the back of Appa's head.

"Appa, I'm not saying this to hurt you. Her name goes on the shelf and in our mouths, not in their book. That's why nobody signs alone, and nobody signs *for* anybody."

I put my hand flat on Amma's, over Ix's invisible ears.

"Also, there's a thing in this truck you can't see. A wolf, on your knee. His name is Ix. He's Kino's, and he likes you. If he's got his ears back, it's because something just came in that doesn't belong." I tried a smile. It mostly worked. "Amma, say it with me. *Susmithaa Sowri.* Whole name. Out loud."

Kino's eyes went to the mirror, then the rear glass, then the road. "Say it quiet," he said. "Better yet, ask it at Nana's table, where there's salt." He flicked the heater to high, which was all it did. His left forearm lay on the wheel with the birthmark showing, and he was murmuring something in Spanish, a street-corner prayer to the Lady.

"Appa, both feet flat on the floor. Keep your hand where it is. Ix, stay on her. One mile. Nobody opens a door till I've walked the line."

Amma looked down at the warm weight on her knee that she couldn't see. Her hand stayed under mine.

"Susmithaa Sowri," she said.

Not a whisper. She said it the way she'd say it to a clerk who had spelled it wrong.

A beat later, from the front, rough: "Susmithaa Sowri." Appa's feet were flat on the floor. His grey hand hadn't moved. But he'd said it.

The cold didn't leave. It *pulled back*, sliding off our ankles toward the rear of the cab like water down a tilted floor, and pooled behind the seat, under Kino's jacket. Ix's ears came halfway up. Kino's breath went out of him in a long, careful line.

Two turns later, there was Nana's street, with the porch light still on in daylight.

* * *

She was at the gate in her cardigan and slippers, a mixing bowl of coarse salt on her hip, as if she'd been standing there since we left. Malachi's car pulled in behind and stopped a careful length off the curb.

Kino killed the engine.

In the quiet, everyone heard it. A small dry *tick* from behind the seat, like a fingernail on paper. Then another.

Nana's eyes went past all of us to the back of the truck. "¿Qué traen ahí, mijo?"

"Nobody move yet," I said. I held a flat hand out toward Amma and Appa, patting the air. "Nana. It's the envelope. R. SOWRI. It's been ticking since the engine stopped."

I didn't touch Kino's jacket. I got out on the street side, in my socks on the cold concrete, and walked to the gate. I stopped one step outside the salt so my shadow wouldn't cross it.

"You said it should be seen inside the line. Tell me where to carry it, and in what hand. I'll do it. Not Appa. Not Amma." I looked back once at my parents' faces in the glass. "And can someone get them through the gate first? They've been sitting on top of this long enough."

Kino took the bowl from her hip and poured a fresh line across the walk, just inside the gate, white and unbroken, wrist steady. Nana watched the wrist the whole way like she was grading it. She gave one short nod.

Malachi stayed on the street side, outside the salt, hands where Nana could see them. "No postmark," he said. "Sealed. Nobody has read it. I'd like that to stay true until we choose who does." He opened Amma's door and offered his arm. "Mrs. Sowri. Eleven minutes ago you asked a better question than I've asked in a month. Come inside the line with me."

She took his arm without looking at him. She stepped over the salt in her temple socks and stopped just inside to turn around, because she wouldn't go farther until her husband did.

Kino brought Appa door to door, a hand flat between his shoulder blades. When Appa's foot cleared the salt, his grey thumb jerked once, like someone had tapped it. He closed his fist over it and kept walking.

Malachi stayed outside, back to the house, eyes on the street.

Behind the truck seat the ticking went on. It wasn't regular. It counted in clusters. *Tick tick … tick.*

Nana set the empty bowl on the gatepost and looked at me, in my socks, one step outside her line.

"No entra cerrado, mija. Nothing sealed with their glue comes in my house." She pointed with her chin. "On the salt. Half in, half out. You carry it with the hand you don't sign with, and you don't put it near your chest."

She lifted her cardigan's hem and took a short wooden spatula from her apron pocket, worn black at the edge from years on the comal. She held it out handle-first. To Kino, not me.

"Y lo abre él. With wood. Not a blade, not a pen. The one who reads it is whoever's name is on it, or whoever stands for that name." Her eyes went to the gate, to my parents. "You decide that. Not me. Not them."

Under the jacket, the envelope ticked twice more and waited, as if it had heard.

I pulled my right hand, the one I sign with, up into my sleeve. With my left I lifted Kino's jacket away. The envelope was warm, and its ticking ran up my wrist like a pulse that wasn't mine. I carried it at arm's length, low, away from my chest, down the whole walk. I didn't speak.

At the line I set it half in and half out. The ticking stopped mid-count, as if interrupted.

"It's Appa's name," I said, to the concrete. Then I looked up. "So it's Appa's call. Not mine. Not Nana's. But he doesn't read it alone. Amma and I stand on either side, on the salt, so the name has two people under it. If he says no, we burn it in Nana's comal and deal with whatever follows. If he says yes, Kino opens it with the wood."

I held his eyes. "Appa. Your name, your hand. Do you want to know what it says?"

Behind me, Malachi's voice, pitched for the gate and not the block: "Whatever you decide, decide it as the two people it belongs to. Nobody will hurry you. I'll hold the street as long as it takes." A pause. Drier. "If a man in leather shoes walks up this block, I'll be the one who greets him."

Kino stood a pace off with the spatula low in both hands, blade to the ground. He didn't look at the paper. He watched Appa's face. "Take your time. It'll keep. If it's no, Nana's comal is forty feet away and the fire's already lit."

Ix had come off Amma's knee to lie on the salt behind his heels.

Appa looked at the envelope for a long time. It was his name on it, typed, in capitals, the way a hospital types a name.

"Since March," he said, not to anyone. Then, to me, very quietly: "Yes. I want to know. I don't want to *not* know any more."

He held out his left hand, the clean one, sideways, without looking. Amma took it. She stepped onto the salt without being asked. I took the other side. The grey hand hung at his hip between us, and he kept it closed.

* * *

Kino knelt. The spatula's worn edge went under the flap.

The glue didn't tear. It *peeled*, dry, like an old bandage, and the envelope gave one last tick and stopped. Nobody breathed. Inside was a single carbon-set form, white over pink, folded in thirds. Kino flicked it open with the wood so no one's fingers touched it.

> **RETURN OF PERSONAL EFFECTS — CUSTODY RECEIPT**
> REF: PROXIMAL / 11
> ITEM: One (1) container, sealed. CONTENTS: SOWRI, SUSMITHAA. CONDITION: intact.
> PRESENT LOCATION: c/o LUCERO, I.
> RECEIVED BY (NEXT OF KIN): **R** ________________________
> ALTERNATE SIGNATORY: L. SOWRI ✓
> *On countersignature this entry is closed and any balance carried forward to the next proximal reference.*

The **R** wasn't typed. It was pencil, the same soft grey as Appa's stained thumb, with a slight tremor in the downstroke where someone else had been holding the wrist. The check beside Amma's name was the same grey, small and neat, the kind a teacher makes. In the bottom corner, a faded round stamp: a shepherd's crook inside a circle. The pink copy underneath was complete except for the lower-left corner, torn away in a ragged little triangle.

Nana read *LUCERO, I.* upside down from the step and said nothing at all. She only reached behind her, without looking, and put a hand on the doorframe of her own house.

Amma read the line with her name on it. Her knuckles went pale on Appa's hand.

"That's what the slip bought," she said. "If you won't, they ask me."

* * *

Abernathy was talking in my ear before I'd finished reading.

*Guv. Don't touch it, don't look at it, just listen. Two threads.*

*Grey one runs from the R into your Appa's fist, under the thumbnail, tight as fishing line. That one's set. Second one's thin, from your Mum's tick to her left foot, where the slip was. Salt's frosted both where they cross, but it ain't cuttin' 'em. And the slack one's takin' up slack, love. Slow.*

*They ain't pullin' on the paper. They're pullin' on the slip. Her sock, her Clark, the pink archana. That's the hook. Get that off her and I reckon the thread's got nothin' to bite. Dunno it for certain. Say so if I'm wrong.*

*Your Dad's is in his skin, in the stain. Salt'll slow it, won't cure it. Don't let him pick up a pen today. Not even his own.*

*And don't tell the table I said "reelin'." Sounds like I'm enjoyin' it. I ain't. I'm sick as a dog and I don't even have a stomach.*

*Not a word about "reelin'," I promise,* I told him. *And thank you. That's the first useful thing anyone's said in four minutes. Say "now" or "no" when I move her foot, and nothing else.*

I still had Appa's hand. I read the form again the way I'd read a source's quote, looking for the sentence they hoped I'd skim.

"Don't anyone sign it," I said. "Don't anyone *touch* it." It came out clipped, the sharp voice I get when I'm scared. "Three things. One. *Present location, c/o Lucero, I.*" I didn't look at Nana. "They know where Susi is. Nana, I'm sorry. It means the salt held, and it also means they're writing your name down, and I hate that."

"Two. The R is pencil, Appa's stain, but the tremor is Susi's hand steadying his wrist. They kept a copy of March and they're reusing it. And Amma's check is a *backup*. That's what the slip was for. They didn't need you today, Amma. They were making sure the page had a second line."

"Three." I pointed with my chin. "Somebody tore that corner. Whoever has it has the other half. And the stamp is a crook. Caleb's handler texted him *ON SITE* under the name Shepherd. It's the same office." I breathed. "*Balance carried forward.* I think if nobody signs, it doesn't just sit. It rolls to the next name."

"A ledger word," Kino said, still on one knee. "Nothing gets erased. An open entry rolls down." He looked up at Nana. "Did you hear Lucero? Do I move you?"

"Mijo." Nana almost laughed. "I buried a husband out of this house. Your mother learned to walk on that floor. They want to write *Lucero* on a piece of paper? Que lo escriban. I'm not a box."

Malachi turned his head, only his head. "A carbon set tears along the perforation when the original's handed over. The stub stays in the book. Someone holds a book with this reference in it, and the edges will match. Mrs. Lucero, I'm sorry. A name on a form is a way of finding a door. It isn't a key."

Then I knelt in front of Amma on the salt.

"Amma. Your left foot. Please. Shoe and sock, both, now." I asked it like a favor, but my hands were already on the Clark's strap. "I'll explain in a minute. I need that foot bare on the ground."

The shoe came off. I handed it to Kino. "On the wood, not your hands. Out by the comal, away from the paper, stood on its heel. The sock goes in Nana's fire."

"Appa. One rule until tomorrow. No pens. Not even to sign a grocery list. If you need to write, tell me and I'll write it. Okay?"

*Now,* Abernathy said. *Slack one's gone limp as wet string, love. Frost's creepin' back up it. Your Dad's still taut. Same as before.*

* * *

Nana went down the two steps, took the heavy stone pestle out of the molcajete on the porch rail, and set it square on the folded form, half in the salt and half out, like a paperweight on a grave.

"There. It stays where it is. Nobody carries it, and nobody burns it until we know what burning it *does*."

The screen door creaked. Caleb Strand stood in the doorway in his socks with a dish towel over his shoulder. He wasn't looking at any of us. He was looking at the faded stamp showing past the edge of the stone.

"That's a runner's book," he said. His voice was thin. "The crook. They don't use it on the crew side. That's for the ones who *close*. Carbon sets, bound at the top, a clipboard with a steel spine. The runner keeps the stubs. The stubs are how the office knows a receipt was delivered." He swallowed. "I saw one once, on a man who waited at the curb all night outside a house in Pacifica. He never came to the door. He didn't have to. He just stood there until somebody came out and signed to make him go away."

He glanced at Appa's grey fist and away, fast.

"He was polite. They're always polite. He takes his shoes off if you ask."

Amma stood very still on the salt. She looked down at her own bare foot, then at the fist where I held the pink slip, and her brow creased. The slip had been *there*. The thread, if there was a thread, still ran *here*.

"Nine o'clock," she said. "So he comes, and he stands, and he waits for one of us to get tired." She lifted her chin. "Then we don't get tired. What do we need?"

"Caleb, thank you," I said. "That's the first time anyone's told me what he *does* instead of what he is." I stayed on one knee beside her foot. "He doesn't break the door. He stands at the curb until somebody gets tired of him and walks out with a pen. So the whole job tomorrow is that nobody walks out."

I looked up at Amma. "Four things, out loud, so nobody has to write them down.

"One: shifts. Nobody sits at that window alone. Two people, always, and a pair of eyes that isn't family, so we don't get tired in the same place. Malachi on the street, Kino inside the line, Caleb on the porch. We trade at the hour.

"Two: food and sleep. Real food, Nana's stove only, and you and Appa lie down for a few hours. A runner waits for tired. Don't give him tired.

"Three: the book. The torn corner, the stubs, whoever carries a steel-spined clipboard with a crook on it. If we find the book before nine, we don't wait for him at the door. We're already at his.

"Four." I turned the slip over in my fist and didn't open it. "This stays in my hand until we know what burning it does. And Dalal-ji said he's the one who knows the words. Which means he knew exactly how this works before anyone told him. Nobody calls him. Nobody visits him. Nobody lets him be kind."

Softer, to Amma only: "Your foot's cold. Come in, please? We can plan with our shoes off just fine."

Malachi didn't turn from the street. "I'll add a fifth. A man who waits for tired will also wait for hungry, and scared, and for someone who walks out to *make him go away*. One rule on the porch. If he arrives, nobody answers him. Nobody offers him water, a chair, or a kindness. Politeness is how he gets in."

Kino was already walking Amma's Clark out past the porch on the spatula's flat. He stood it on its heel on bare dirt by the comal and came back wiping his hands on his pants. "Salt on every sill and the side door of the Sowri house before sundown," he said. "And I want to see that curb in daylight before he does." He held the screen door for Amma, then Appa. He was last across the threshold.

Amma held my shoulder for balance and peeled off the sock. Her bare foot on the salt was brown and narrow and the coldest thing in the yard. She let out a breath she hadn't known she was holding.

"Oh," she said, surprised. "That's better."

She didn't say what was better.

Nana took the sock in the comal tongs and dropped it on the coals. It burned like any cotton sock, quick and blue-edged, but for a second the smoke smelled like a freshly sharpened pencil. We all looked at the pestle. Nothing under it changed. The grey check beside *L. SOWRI* stayed exactly as neat as before.

Nana grunted. "So. Fire takes the thing. Not the writing."

* * *

The kitchen was hot and close and smelled of cumin and burnt chile skins. Nana put café de olla in front of everyone, and eggs, and tortillas straight off the comal, beside the dead phone and the Vesper packs. Nobody ate from another kitchen.

Caleb sat with both hands around his mug and answered the way Malachi had asked, slowly.

"Pacifica. Linda Mar. A stucco house with a boat on a trailer in the drive. Two Februaries ago. An old man, Mr. Okafor. His wife was ours." He didn't say *proximal reference*. You could hear him not say it. "The runner held the clipboard flat against his chest, both arms, like a hymnal. Never put it down. Black oxfords, laced tight, polished, but grey along the stitching, like he'd walked through pencil dust. Lanyard turned backward. Seven-forty in the morning, Mr. Okafor came out in his robe and signed on the hood of his car just to make him leave. The runner thanked him, tore the copy, and walked to the bus stop on Highway 1. Got on the 110. No car. Never a car." He finally drank. "I think they're not allowed to drive. I think it's in the rules."

I took a tortilla because Abernathy had said eat, and because my hands needed a job. I tore it and didn't eat it yet.

"The oxfords are still on the rack in San Bruno," I said, to Malachi. "Toes out. Grey in the welt. Same man, or the same kind. Someone has to come back for them."

"Probably bait," Malachi said from the chair by the door, where he could see the street through the gap in the curtain. "I'd watch them anyway. A man who can't drive is a man with a schedule, and the shoes are the one thing of his that sits in one place." He turned his cup a quarter-turn. "He's met my face at the temple door. Caleb he hasn't seen. Bus route first. The 110, a stop near Linda Mar. Buses have cameras, and a polite man who rides every day is a regular. I can ask without a name or a number. I'd like to borrow Kino's eyes at the temple lot this afternoon and be back before dark."

"No car, no plate, a bus," Kino said. He set his plate down. "Then the route is a map. He has to ride something to Daly City and walk the last blocks. That's where I'd watch." He glanced at the stamp-hidden pestle through the window. "Burning the paper makes it a bonfire for them. Keep the pestle where it is."

Appa hadn't touched his eggs. With his left hand he unbuttoned his shirt pocket and took out the green Sheaffer, the one Susi had steadied him with. He set it on the oilcloth and slid it, not to me, but to Amma.

"Keep it till Monday," he said. "I don't trust my hand."

Amma looked at it a long moment. Then she wrapped it in her paper napkin and tucked it into her blouse, against her collarbone, the way she carries temple money.

"Amma," I said carefully. I didn't reach for it. "Nana said nothing of theirs sits near the chest. That pen has Susi's hand in it, and the stranger's ink. Would you put it in Nana's clay jar with the Vesper packs? Not forever. Until Monday. I'd rather it sat under a lid than over your heart."

I turned to Appa. "And thank you. Handing it to her was the hardest thing you could do with that hand, and you did it with your left."

Amma took the napkin out of her blouse. It was warm. Nana lifted the lid off the clay jar on the shelf, under Susi's canister, where the packs and the dead phone lay in coarse salt. Amma set the wrapped pen among them herself, and Nana put the lid back on.

Amma pressed her palm flat against her collarbone where it had been, and frowned, like someone listening for a sound that had stopped.

"Hm," she said. That was all. She sat down and finally ate.

Malachi's offer sat on the table like a second plate. "Mrs. Sowri, I owe you an introduction. You'll have it tonight, at this table, with Nana listening and the door salted. You asked Arenya for the beginning. You should get mine too."

Amma gave him one short nod. *Tonight.* Appa looked at the man who owed him an apology, and didn't look away.

By ten-thirty the plan had a shape. Kino and Malachi would go to San Bruno, the temple lot and the shoe rack and the bus stops. Caleb would take the porch, beside the pestle. I would stay with my parents, inside the line, in a kitchen that smelled of chile and coffee.

Out on the walk, Amma's left Clark stood on its heel in the dirt, toes pointed at the street, waiting like something that expected to be collected.

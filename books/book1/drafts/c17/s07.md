They took Skyline down to San Bruno with the windows cracked.

Appa rode in the middle of the bench seat with his knees against the dash and his right hand open on his thigh, grey side up, as if he were letting it air. Nobody turned on the radio. Behind the seat, the blanket-wrapped envelope hissed every time the truck braked, salt settling in the fold.

Malachi stayed two lengths back the whole way. Twice a grey sedan swam up in the mirror. Twice it turned out to be somebody's Corolla.

I counted the Corollas. It was something to do with my brain.

* * *

Amma's temple did not look like a temple from the street. It had been a Lutheran church. The steeple was still there, painted saffron now, and a vinyl banner stretched across the old notice board: **SRI VENKATESWARA SATSANG — SAT. ABHISHEKAM 7:30 AM — ALL WELCOME.**

The parking lot smelled like camphor and the Jack in the Box across El Camino. Kids in Halloween costumes ran between the cars with silk pavadais bunched under their capes. A Spider-Man in a veshti went past at full speed.

It was the most ordinary place I had been in four days. That should have been a comfort.

In the old vestibule there was a long wooden shoe rack. Chanting came through the hall doors, winding down; a bell rang three times. Kino stopped on the threshold. Ix left his heels, trotted once along the rack, and sat.

Chappals. Light-up sneakers. Amma's brown Clarks on the second shelf, toes in, the way she always left them.

And on the bottom shelf, at the end, a pair of men's black oxfords. Size ten or eleven. Toes pointed out at the door, laces tucked inside. Polished. Dry. No grey line on the tile, no ruled dust anywhere.

Whoever wore them had taken them off properly, the way you're supposed to, and walked in barefoot.

Through the open hall doors, forty backs sat on the carpet. Down the side aisle, the swinging door to the kitchen was propped, and ghee and pongal drifted out of it. So did Amma's voice, telling someone, not unkindly, that they had put in far too much pepper.

Appa had seen the shoes. He stood very still in his socks with one hand on the rack.

I put my hand flat on his sleeve, the clean side. "Appa. Look at me, not the shoes."

Then, low, to the others, without turning my head: "Nobody goes in alone and nobody runs. Amma's in there with forty witnesses next door. That's the safest place in San Bruno as long as we're in it."

I set my sneakers on the rack beside Amma's Clarks, toes in. Then I took Appa's grey hand and didn't comment on it.

"Kino, the door. Watch the hall. Malachi, you're with me and Appa. We walk to the kitchen like three people who came for pongal." My mouth did something that wasn't a smile. "Amma will say we're underdressed. Let her."

* * *

Kino pulled his boots off and put them on the top shelf, laces tucked, toes out. He set his back against the frame where he could see the hall, the rack and the lot through the glass.

"Ten or eleven," he said, barely above breath. "Dry. Barefoot means he's inside, sitting with everybody." He looked at the backs of forty heads and did not look for the one that was too still. "Anyone gets up and walks toward that kitchen, I'm already moving."

To Ix, in Spanish, hardly a sound: *Dime si huele a lápiz, flaco.*

Malachi crouched beside the rack as if tying a shoe he wasn't wearing. Two seconds. The oxfords had been resoled more than once, the heels worn down on the outer edge. Nothing in the toes. No paper.

But in the seam where the upper met the welt, packed in like lint, was a fine grey dust. It wasn't dirt. It shone faintly under the vestibule light, exactly the way the ruled line on the Sowri step had shone.

Ix put his nose into the left shoe and yanked it out. He sneezed, once, hard, and sat back down facing the hall. He didn't growl. His ears stayed up.

*Pencil,* Kino said, quietly, for the dog. *Sí, flaco.*

"Walk at Appa's pace," Malachi told me. "Don't stand between him and the hall. If a man in socks offers you a pen—"

"We have our own."

"Good."

* * *

The side aisle ran past the old pews, stacked now against the wall, and a framed photo of the Tirumala hills. The back kitchen was all steam. Two aunties I half recognized stood at the sink. A rice cooker the size of a drum ticked on the counter.

Amma stood at the big pot in her plain Saturday cotton, pallu tucked into her waist, a wooden paddle in both hands. She looked up and saw the three of us in jeans and socks.

"All of you. Like this." Her eyes went to Appa's face and her voice dropped a full register. "Raghavan. What happened?"

She didn't wait for the answer. She turned, as if it were the most natural thing in the world, toward the steel prep table by the back door.

A man sat there in a white veshti with a sacred thread across his chest, grating ginger into a bowl. Sixties. Silver at the temples. Reading glasses on a cord. A plain blue ballpoint tucked behind his right ear, the way a shopkeeper keeps one.

Black dress socks.

"This is Dalal-ji," Amma said. "From Fremont. He does the archanas at Livermore, second Sundays. He came to help with the pepper." A little dry, that. "He was asking about you, Raghavan. He knew Susmithaa."

The man set down the grater and wiped his fingers on a towel, one at a time. He stood and folded his hands. His voice was soft and enormously kind.

"Sowri-garu. My deepest condolences." His eyes rested on me, mildly, the way a man looks at a child whose horoscope he has already read. "Your sister did a beautiful archana for this one in March. One hundred and one dollars. Very generous." He smiled. "I am so glad the family has come together. Sit, sit. There is plenty."

Behind him the back door stood open on the parking lot, propped with a cinder block. Appa's grey hand had closed around my fingers hard enough to hurt.

*A hundred and one dollars.* The exact number I'd have asked him about.

I squeezed back, just as hard, and stepped half in front of Appa without letting go. Then I crossed to Amma and slid my free arm through hers, the arm holding the paddle, until the paddle tipped down and her hand was in mine.

"Amma. We came to eat, yes. But first we're all going to go stand in the parking lot. Together. Right now. Leave the pot." I kept my voice pleasant and my eyes on his hands. "Thank you, Dalal-ji, that's very kind. We won't sit. And we won't take a pen, a pen cap, or a receipt. We have our own."

I didn't smile. I tugged Amma gently toward the daylight.

Malachi moved with us, but put himself between the prep table and the family, one pace to Dalal's left, hands empty and visible. His voice was as even as the man's.

"Mr. Dalal. A box in Daly City is being held for you on vacancy." He let that sit one second. "You'll forgive us. We're leaving by the door that's open. Please stay where you are, with the ginger. Aunties, nothing's wrong. The pepper is a family matter."

At the back door, Kino had arrived from outside, without a sound, in his socks. He filled the doorway with daylight behind him and did not come in. He didn't need to. He only stood there with his arms loose and his weight forward, so that a man looking through the steam could see that this was no longer his way out.

* * *

Amma didn't let go of the paddle when I took her arm. For one second she held it. The aunties had gone quiet at the sink, hands in the water.

Then she laid the paddle across the pot, reached past me, and turned the flame off. She did it without hurrying, the way she wouldn't hurry in her own kitchen if the roof were coming down.

She looked at Dalal over the rim of the pot and spoke Tamil, low and flat, in the voice she used on contractors.

"*You cashed it. I saw the back of that check. My husband never wrote like that in his life.*"

Dalal didn't sit and he didn't step forward. He looked at Malachi's empty hands, then past the steam at Kino. Something moved across his face. Not fear. More like a man revising an estimate.

He lifted the blue ballpoint from behind his ear. Malachi's weight shifted.

Dalal only laid the pen on the steel table. Flat. Cap pointed at himself.

"Box?" he said gently, to Malachi. "I don't keep boxes, sir. I keep names." And to Amma, in careful Tamil: "*I said her name to God, amma. That is my work. God has many ears in this country. Some of them pay.*"

Then, in English, to Appa, softer still: "Tomorrow at nine, someone will come to your side door with a pen. It will not be me. It will be someone who does not know the words."

He sat back down and picked up the grater.

"If you want someone who knows the words, I am here until the prasadam is served."

Nobody answered him. Kino stepped aside, eyes never leaving the man, and we walked out past the cinder block into the parking lot, all of us in our socks.

Cold asphalt. Bright sun. Somewhere a kid was shrieking about candy. Ix had come around the outside of the building and leaned his whole weight against Kino's shins. No grey Nissan. No Camry. Only minivans with stick-figure families on the back glass.

Amma pulled her arm out of mine and fixed her pallu. She looked at each of us in turn: her husband, her daughter, her daughter's boyfriend in his socks, the tall stranger who had called the pepper a family matter.

"Somebody," she said, "is going to tell me everything. Now. In the car. And Raghavan—" she took his grey hand and turned it over in the sun, and her mouth went thin "—*since when.*"

Through the kitchen window you could see Dalal grating ginger. The blue pen lay where he'd left it.

"Since March, Amma," I said, before Appa could take it on himself. "And he'll tell you the rest. All of it. But not here, and not in the open in our socks with that window behind us." I jerked my chin at the glass, the steel table, the pen lying cap-first. "Nobody touch that pen. Nobody go back in alone for shoes."

"I'll get them," Malachi said. "I'm the one he's already looked at."

"We'll both go," said Kino. "Ix stays with Amma."

"Quédate con ellos," he told the dog, and Ix went to Amma's heel as if he'd been assigned there at birth.

I turned Amma toward the truck and kept her hand in mine. "Here's the short version, because you deserve it before the long one. Susi's death wasn't an accident. They're using Appa's signature. And tomorrow at nine they're coming to our side door. We are not going to be there. We're going to Nana Itzel's, behind her salt, and I'll tell you every single thing in the truck." My voice cracked on *every*. I let it. "I should have told you weeks ago. I'm sorry. Please get in."

* * *

Malachi held the vestibule's glass door open with his back, so Kino never had to turn away from the hall. Forty people stood now for the arati; the flame went round; nobody looked back at the door.

Between them, they cleared the rack in one armload. The oxfords they left. Toes out, laces tucked, waiting for their owner.

Kino stepped around them like a ranger around a snake.

In the lot he handed Amma her Clarks first, with both hands, because that is how you accept something from someone who is helping you. She took them the same way. Malachi stopped beside her door.

"Mrs. Sowri. I'm Malachi Shawani. I owe your husband an apology, and you a better introduction than a man with his hands up in your kitchen. I'll give you both in the car. I'll be behind you the whole way, in daylight."

To Kino, lower: "Don't go home first. And if the beige phone rings in that house tonight, nobody answers it."

Kino nodded. "Nobody eats anything from that kitchen," he said to the rest of us. "Ni un grano."

Amma stepped into her left shoe and stopped.

She bent. She slid two fingers inside and drew out a slip of paper folded once. Pink. The kind the temple office tears off for every archana.

She unfolded it. She read it. I read it over her arm.

**ARCHANA — SRI VENKATESWARA — SAT 10/31**
**NAME: LAKSHMI SOWRI · STAR: ROHINI**
**PAID ✓**

Blue ballpoint. A neat, careful hand.

She didn't crumple it. She folded it once more along the same crease and held it out flat on her palm, to me, the way you hand over something hot.

"I did not ask for this," Amma said. Her voice was perfectly steady.

I took it. It weighed nothing. It was the heaviest thing I'd carried all week.

She climbed into the truck behind Appa, the second Clark on, and sat with her hands in her lap and her back very straight.

"Drive, Kino. Arenya, start at the beginning."

The truck pulled out onto El Camino with Malachi two lengths behind. The saffron steeple shrank in the mirror. Ix laid his chin on Amma's knee and kept it there all the way to Hillcrest, and in the back the salt hissed every time we braked.

I started at the beginning.

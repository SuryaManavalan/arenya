Teddy's piece went live at six. By 6:09 it had four hundred shares and a "??" from Sacramento.

By 6:22 Arenya was walking across the Presidio in the blue-black dark, and the bridge was a string of red lights hung from nothing.

The fog sat on the Gate like a held breath. A quarter mile out. Not moving.

He was on the picnic deck, early, as promised.

Typhus Quinn was taller than he looked on a screen and more tired. Runner's shell over a collared shirt, like a man who couldn't pick which person to be this morning. Two paper coffees on the rail. A manila folder flat under his left hand. He wasn't looking at his phone. He was watching the fog.

The foghorn sounded, two low notes. Then nothing.

Eleven seconds later, it sounded again.

She felt that interval in her teeth.

He held a coffee out across the rail the way you'd offer something to a stray. "I didn't know how you take it, so I didn't do anything to it." The horn went. He waited it out, and she saw that he was counting too. "I'm going to ask you to tell me I'm wrong about something. I'd like you to actually try."

She took the cup. It was hot enough to hurt through the paper, which made it the first honest thing that had happened to her since Monday.

*Eyes on his face, love,* said the voice at her shoulder, thin as a draft. *Not the van.*

She hadn't known there was a van. Now she did. White Sprinter, Mason Street, engine off, a laminated sign in the windshield: GGNRA / FOG-SENSOR SURVEY. The lamination was new. A jogger in a black headband had already passed the hut once, too evenly, without looking at the water.

Her people were out there somewhere in the dark. Kino on the seawall with his hood up. Ava beside him, toe an inch short of the hut's bottom step, where a strand of bull kelp lay tied in a knot nobody ties. Caleb against the service wall. Malachi under the cypress, being a man who wasn't there.

She wrote *6:24, Crissy, TQ* at the top of a clean page. It was a habit. It was also so he could watch her do it.

"Black's fine," she said. "I drink wine out of a mug that says *Investigative Badass*. I'm not precious." The horn. *Nine. Ten. Eleven.* "You've been counting too. Okay. Show me what's in the folder. If it's wrong, I'll say so and show you where. If it isn't wrong, I'm writing that down too."

The corner of his mouth almost became a smile and thought better of it. He laid the pages on the wet rail one at a time, squaring each edge with his thumb. It was the only nervous thing he did.

A scope trace, soft from folding: a long slow pulse, a hand-drawn bracket, *11.0 s*. "A drive coil on a test pad at Hunters Point. It wasn't plugged in. It did that anyway."

A tide-gauge plot from Alameda with a small wobble circled in red. Eleven seconds. Monday, 6:52. "A bay that shape can't ring like a bell. It rang."

A photocopied notebook page, columns of figures in a small careful hand. At the bottom, underlined so hard the pen had torn the paper: ***WHERE FROM?*** And under it: *Unit 8 — "Melnikov"?*

He kept two fingers on that one a second longer than the others.

Then two lines in his own print. *Mon 17:03 — coil spike, 1.4 s, new signature.* *Tue 19:46 — coil temp −3.1 °C in 52 s. Alameda residual +4 cm (recovering).*

"Here's what I need you to tell me is wrong. My machine isn't reacting to the bay. The bay is reacting to my machine. And my machine is eating something I didn't put in it." He tapped the last page. "Last night at 7:46 something took a hand off my coil, and the bay got better. Four centimeters." He turned his phone to show her the stake holes in the grey mud. "Your colleague stripped the metadata. He didn't strip the waterline. That's last night's low tide."

Her pen stopped on 7:46.

That was when the first stake came out. She didn't say so. She didn't look at him for a second, because her face would say it for her.

* * *

Behind the hut, the jogger came back on her third pass, slower, and stopped at the foot of the steps to stretch. Heel on the rail, head down. Eight feet away. Red nails, chipped. Her left foot planted an inch from Ava's kelp.

*Two more sweeps,* Abernathy said. *Something in that van is sweeping the deck on the horn, cold and grey, and every pass hangs longer on your bag. Where the Lamp's kipping. Say the word and I'll stand in front of it.*

*No,* she thought. *You're down four and I'm not trading you for a bag.* She shifted her weight as if the wind had got to her, left shoulder to Mason Street, the bag behind her hip. And she did what she was good at.

"You're wrong about one word, and it's *my*," she said. "A coil that rings with nothing plugged in isn't your machine. It's somebody else's instrument sitting on your pad. You built the part that shows up on a scope. Whatever's doing the eating has its own yards and its own trucks, and its registered agent is on Sand Hill Road. It isn't yours, Mr. Quinn. It's *in* yours."

His jaw set. At *Sand Hill Road* he closed his eyes for exactly one horn.

"Bayline," he said. "Bayline Remediation is a vendor under Applied Field Research. I signed the group's charter in 2023. I've never read its vendor list." He said it like a man reading his own name off a charge sheet.

"Who's Melnikov?" she asked. "You put your fingers on him like a grave."

"Yuri Melnikov. A machinist in Dogpatch. Died in August. Cardiac event, fifty-one." His voice didn't change. The coffee in his other hand shook once. "My cousin wrote that name in a column of power figures in September, with a question mark. Then he died too. There is no Unit 8 on any drawing I've signed. I checked them all twice."

Adonis. The T. on the back of a stone in Colma. She wrote nothing, and kept it off her face.

"Also," Typhus said. "Elias pulled your badge history. You were in my lobby system in September. Somebody *else* pulled it on Saturday, and it wasn't him."

"September was me, and I'm not apologizing at six in the morning. Saturday wasn't. My old card's in my boss's desk drawer, and it hit your reader twice anyway." She wrote *SAT — not me — WHO* and underlined it harder than she meant to. "Somebody has a copy of a copy."

Then the jogger's head came up.

Not toward the gravel, where a tall man in a coat had come down out of the cypress, unhurried, hands empty. It turned the way a compass needle turns. She looked straight at Malachi and said one word to the bud in her ear.

Arenya didn't hear it. She saw him not hear it. His stride never changed. It was the most frightening thing she'd seen all week.

The jogger pivoted off the rail. Her left shoe came down on the kelp.

The squeal was thin and sharp, like feedback, loud enough that Kino flinched at the seawall. The woman's hand flew to her ear, and for one second her face wasn't flat at all. On Mason Street the Sprinter's roof gave a soft hydraulic *tick*, and the cold beam Abernathy had been tracking swung off the deck, off her bag, and went to the woman.

The horn sounded. Eleven seconds.

Arenya swept the five pages into the folder with the flat of her hand and pressed it to Typhus's chest. "Hold that against you. Walk with me." She steered him to the far end of the deck, the hut between them and the van, the bag on the seaward side of his tall frame. "Wind," she said, loud. "It's brutal out here."

Then, into the tail of the horn, bright and annoyed, the voice you use on a friend who's late: "*Mr. Shawani!* There you are! Do you know how long we've been standing out here? Your coffee's gone cold!"

It was the name on his lease, the only one she was allowed to say. She was giving that listening ear something louder than the name he didn't use.

"Whatever happens in the next ten seconds," she murmured to Typhus, "you don't know her, you don't know him, and you're having a normal meeting with a reporter. Can you do that?"

He didn't answer. He didn't move either.

Ava came up off the seawall babbling about hearing aids and microwaves, phone raised, glasses fogged. Kino stepped square into the woman's path, hood back, ranger-bored. *Fog advisory, ma'am. Promenade east.* She went around him and gave the kelp a wide berth that looked almost superstitious. That put her shoulder a foot from Malachi's.

He said something low as she passed. Arenya only caught the end of it. *Overpaid.*

The woman's stride broke. Half a step.

Then she was walking east into the fog with her hand pinned to her ear, and she never looked back.

* * *

Behind the hut, Caleb had his palm flat on a van door, and he heard a voice he knew.

She didn't learn that until later. In the moment all she saw was the slider open six inches, a head come out low, find an empty curb, and pull back in. The door thunked. The Sprinter pulled off Mason without lights and turned toward Lombard. On the roof, the dish folded flat.

The cold lifted off the deck like a hand taken off a shoulder.

The bridge was showing its towers now, rust-red, against a sky going pewter.

"That was for me," Typhus said. His voice was very quiet. "Or for you. I can't tell which, and I don't like that I can't tell." He took a plain white card from his shell. A handwritten number. He wrote a second under it in the same careful print. "The top one is mine. The bottom one is Elias's personal line. Not the company's. He'll find out who copied your badge. I'll find out who wrote Unit 8."

He hesitated. Then he decided something.

"Last night at 9:14, the Mill's intake logged an object it couldn't classify. *INTAKE EXCEPTION 0001, NO SOURCE.* The system has never thrown that error. It's in a lockbox in the Lantern control room, two floors above my coil." He looked at her notebook, not at her. "I don't know what it is. I think you do."

*Love,* said the voice in her ear, and it was not the put-on voice. *My wrist. Left one, where the cuff sat. It went cold in a ring. Exactly the size of it.*

She wrote *9:14* and *EXCEPTION 0001* and underlined both. She was thinking about a brass cuff riding a line toward a stack. She kept it off her face and out of her mouth.

"If I shut the coil down today," Typhus said, "does the bay get better? Or does whatever's eating my machine find another mouth?"

"I'm not going to lie to make the morning easier," she said. "I think the answer is both. Last night we took one hand off it and the bay got four centimeters better. So starving it helps. But it's staked in more than one place. It'll lean on whatever's still open, harder." She tapped page five. "So don't make it a switch. Make it a throttle. Drop the coil to the lowest draw that keeps your logs honest. Copy everything, the trace, the exception, the lockbox entry, to someone who isn't on Sand Hill Road's payroll. If they want it dark, they'll want it dark quietly. Don't give them that."

She closed the notebook on the pen.

"And don't be alone in that control room. Take Elias. Or take someone from the floor who's afraid of you in the good way." A beat, dry, because she couldn't help it. "Thank you for the coffee. It was awful. I finished it."

He read the bottom number twice, lips moving, and gave her the card back. "Memorized. Burn yours too." He looked out at the bay, flat as a held breath. "Adonis would have liked you. He'd have argued with you about the throttle."

It was the first thing he'd said all morning that wasn't in order.

He walked to the Rivian with the folder against his chest and didn't check his phone. At the Mason Street corner his brake lights held a long second on the bare curb, as if he were memorizing that too.

On the bottom step, Ava's kelp lay in its knot with a grey half-moon of rubber across it. Still tied.

The sun cleared the East Bay hills.

The horn sounded. Eleven seconds. And then, for the first time all morning, twelve.

Then eleven again, as if something out past the Gate had taken a breath and lost count.

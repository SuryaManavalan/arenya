The dashboard said 9:12. The ink on the back of Arenya's hand said 12:12, and that was the time that mattered.

Crisp Avenue dead-ended in chain-link and fennel. Past the fence the old drydock lay like a long black rectangle cut out of the night, and at its mouth squatted the pump house: corrugated sides, a tar roof sagging like a wet hat. A concrete apron ran from the south roll-up down to the pilings.

Eleven pilings. Malachi had counted them in daylight.

Tonight the waterline made them look like twelve.

MO-0117 sat nose-out under the one sodium lamp. The blank signature sheet was still on the dash. But now a ballpoint pen lay across it, uncapped, set at a neat diagonal like a place setting.

It pointed at the pilings.

Her phone buzzed. Typhus, the second number.

*They're moving the box. Two lanyards, M-Ops, not mine. Loading dock 9:20. I can stall the door maybe ten min. Your call.*

She read it twice. Three miles north, a box with Abernathy's cuff in it. Right here, a boy.

*Stall it,* she typed. *Ten minutes. Time every second and tell me who's carrying.*

"The box is a text away," she told the others, low, back against the Transit's cold flank. "The people are right here."

*Deal's good, love,* said Abernathy, in her ear that wasn't an ear. *The cuff's been in that box since Tuesday. It'll keep ten minutes. Ollie won't.* A pause. *Also you've had nothing since the toast. Yeah, I counted.*

* * *

Malachi went along the corrugated wall like a man who had every right to. The padlock on the roll-up wasn't a key lock. It was a matte-brass puck with a four-digit wheel, faintly warm from a hand. A paper tag hung from the shackle on a zip tie: **MO / ROLE ACCESS — SEE M-OPS.** Under it, in ballpoint, *Thu 21:41*, the 1 crossed and redone.

"Don't try the wheel," Arenya said, when he came back. "A lock that waits for a role notices who isn't one."

Through the seam in the door came a sound that wasn't machinery. One long, low held note, like a wet finger on the rim of a glass the size of a room. It held and held, and somewhere above hearing it wasn't finished.

Down at the water, Kino pressed his palm to a piling and felt the hum run from one to the next like a nerve. The warmth on his forearm climbed. Out at the creek mouth a patch of bay that didn't move with the swell turned toward him. Family. But it was crowded against the shore the way a dog crowds your legs when there's something in the yard.

*Not what are you,* he asked it, in Nana's old words. *What is behind you.*

It leaned. He felt it, a rug pulled from under someone else's feet. And he found the thing under the floor: the twelfth piling that wasn't a piling, a hollow pile standing in the drydock sump, tall and narrow and wet, a throat. It drank. With every slow pull the tide along the whole shore sank a finger's width and did not come back. The note was the throat holding its breath before a swallow. It was tuned to every pitch but one, and Kino knew the shape of the gap the way a tongue knows a missing tooth.

A name. It was waiting for one to be said into it.

"Don't say any name near that door," he told them. "Not Ollie's. Not yours. Not even to be kind." And past the tide-woman, past the creek mouth, something enormous lay very still, listening for the swallow.

*Whoever's not in the room when it's said,* Abernathy added, to Arenya alone, *is who it's waiting on. And if you hear a voice use a short name of mine tonight, don't answer it for me.*

Up Crisp, headlights swung in off Innes and stopped. Engine running. Fourteen minutes early.

* * *

A white fleet SUV, MO-0120 glowing on the door. Three men got out. A fourth shape stayed in the passenger seat, lit blue from beneath by a screen.

Arenya took six frames from the Transit's shadow and didn't ask herself whether it was legal.

The three walked down the middle of Crisp, not toward the apron but toward the roll-up. Under the last working streetlight, the one on the left stopped. Five-nine. Thin in the shoulders. A fade growing out. He reached up and touched the back of his head with two fingers, the way you check a bandage.

"That's him," she breathed.

Caleb was already moving.

He didn't go toward the building. He went the other way, up the fence line through the fennel, hands jammed in his pockets because it was the only place they held still. Ava's fingers slid off his wrist. He walked the way he'd walked for fourteen months, the forgettable way, a man who was on the roster and had always been going this way. He came in on Ollie's left, sixty yards from that seam, with the whole building between his mouth and the throat.

"Ollie," he said, low, not looking. "Ollie Baptiste."

The boy's stride caught. A skipped record.

"That itch at the back of your head isn't focus. It's a Crown. The drops aren't focus either. I wore that same jacket for fourteen months and I didn't remember deciding anything." His voice cracked and went too loud, and he hauled it back down. "Your name's Ollie. Keep saying it."

Ollie's eyes moved sideways and down, to Caleb's hands shaking in plain sight under the streetlight. That was where it landed.

"I'm Team Four," he said. "I'm on the —" The word *roster* didn't come. His mouth made the shape of something shorter, a hard consonant and a number, and he stopped it with his teeth.

Caleb held out his right hand, palm down, so the boy could see it jump. "That's day four. That's what it costs to wake up. It's ugly. It's still yours." And then, almost a joke, the first he'd made in fourteen months: "They gave me a number too. Turns out I'm terrible at being a number."

Ollie swallowed the number. What came out was quieter than breath.

"...Ollie."

He stepped off the asphalt into the fennel. A second step. On the third the light slid off his shoulders and he was in the dark gap beside the pump house, shaking, small and fast. His first one.

* * *

"*Baptiste.*"

The bearded flanker had stopped dead in the road. He said it flat, the way you call a dog. "*Apron.*" His hand was at his nape and staying there, his face gone slack and listening, like a man on a call.

MO-0120's passenger door opened. A woman got out without hurrying. Backwards lanyard. A green sea-glass ring that caught the streetlight when she shut the door. The wind brought the chlorine smell of a pool.

Inside the pump house, the note dropped.

Not stopped. Dropped. The pitch it had held for Ollie fell away, and the throat slid, searching like a needle across a radio dial. Kino felt it pass over names the way a hand passes over coats on pegs. It slowed on one.

On the dash of MO-0117 the uncapped pen rolled a quarter turn on its own and pointed at Malachi.

"The operator's withdrawn," the woman called down the road, pleasant, administrative, the voice that calls the next number at the DMV. "Thank you, Mr. Strand, that's noted." And then: "*Mr. Shawani.* Under the guarantee, performance falls to the collateral. It's a signature, not a swim. Shall we keep it civil?"

The bearded man stepped off the road toward the gap.

"No names!" Arenya hissed. "Nobody answer her."

She stepped to the edge of the sodium cone and opened her hand. The Lamp woke in her palm, not a lance, just light. She thought of every streetlamp on Crisp at once and pushed, and a white wall went up across the asphalt at the height of his eyes, the color of all of them together, silvering the fennel tops. She touched her own collar, Malachi's signal, because there was no time to wait for his.

The bearded man walked into it with his eyes squeezed nearly shut and did not slow. He wasn't the one doing the seeing. Something above his head was reading him a map.

*Ten seconds of glare, love,* Abernathy said. *Then break line of sight. Don't stand in it.*

Kino went still at the wall, the way animals are still, and gave the throat a coat with nothing in the pockets. Its search ran over him and found Nana's house sewn into the lining, and moved on. Not far. It drifted up the street after the one coat that had been named aloud.

Ava took Ollie by the sleeve like a stranger on a train platform. Caleb took the other arm. "Count your breathing," she told the boy. "That's all." They steered him through the fennel toward the team car, the Transit between them and the green ring, and the bearded man twelve feet behind, and closing.

Malachi walked up the middle of Crisp, unhurried, hands open, drawing the whole conversation away from that seam.

"Civil," he said. "Then let's be precise. A guarantee answers a default. Your operator didn't default. He was withdrawn. Your word. You noted it yourself." He stopped out of reach. "So tell me who the M is. Is M a person, or a role? Above Mill Operations, or beside it? One word. Then call your man back. He's twelve feet from a boy who just said his own name, and I think you would rather write *withdrawn* in the log than something else."

For a long moment she said nothing. The glare had washed her to a cut-paper figure. She turned the green ring once.

Then she lifted her left hand shoulder-high and closed it into a fist.

Twelve feet behind Caleb, the bearded man stopped mid-stride, one boot in the fennel, as if someone had pulled a plug. His hand came down. He turned and walked back to the road without looking at any of them.

"A role," the woman said, barely voicing it, so the wind took half. "Above. Not beside." The ring again. "And it isn't a debt, Mr. Shawani. Nobody told you, because nobody would sign if they knew. The blank isn't a confession. It's an appointment. Whoever puts a name on that line *is* M-Ops for the term of the instrument. The chair. The key. Every box that opens to the role."

She stopped. Then, quieter:

"It's been empty since the summer. They wanted it filled by someone who had already sold the thing that makes a person say no."

She looked once at the gap in the dark where Ollie shook between two people, and her face did the sorry thing again. This time she didn't hide it. "I'll write *withdrawn*. Both of them. Get him warm. The first night is the worst."

The SUV made a slow three-point turn and went up toward Innes, no headlights until the corner.

* * *

The note didn't stop. It settled, sinking back into the pilings like a breath let out through the nose. The throat closed its mouth around nothing and went still, unfed, to wait for another Thursday. Out past the creek mouth the enormous attention eased, the way a cat stops watching a door. The tide-woman came off the shore and floated loose again.

On the dash, the pen pointed at no one.

In Ava's fist the sixth knot loosened a single fiber, and held.

Arenya closed her hand. The white wall went out of Crisp like a thumbed switch. Her knees wanted to sit; she let the Transit hold her up.

*Stand down on the box,* she typed to Typhus, one-handed. *Don't stall it, don't touch it. Just watch who carries and write down the time. Thank you.*

"M-Ops is a chair," she said, hoarse, to Malachi. "Whoever signs *is* the key. That's L-3. That's the cuff. That's why it's blank. Nobody signs it. Not tonight, not Friday, not for me."

"She told us the one thing she could say without signing it," Malachi said quietly. "Remember that when you decide what she is."

Kino put his hoodie around Ollie's shoulders without a word. Caleb sat on the bumper with his hands pinned between his knees and said, to the road, "I said it out loud and nobody drowned. I'd like that on the ledger somewhere. Small column."

Ava put a granola bar and half a tangerine in Arenya's hand before she could say she was fine. "Eat. Slowly. That's a clinical instruction."

Arenya crossed to the car light and didn't touch the boy. "Hi. I'm Arenya. You did the hard part already."

Under it, in the place only she could hear, Abernathy's voice had gone careful.

*When she said "chair," love, the coat on the peg went warm. Not cold. Warm. That hook was cut to fit somebody, and I'm not sure it's me.* A breath that he didn't have. *I'll tell you the rest when I've got it. I won't give you a guess dressed up as a fact.*

She ate the granola bar. It tasted like cardboard and mercy.

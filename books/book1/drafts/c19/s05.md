The tide turned under the pilings and the foghorn stopped counting.

Arenya waited until the heater had fogged the glass and her hands had quit doing that thing. Then she turned Typhus's card over under the dome light. Under the printed number, in a square engineer's hand: *after 9.*

Her phone said 9:05.

"Engineers," she muttered. "Even the secrets have office hours."

She put the call on speaker, low, so Malachi could hear if he wanted to. He wanted to. He had two messages face-down on his knee and a mirror full of empty Hudson Avenue, and he said, quietly, to the car: "Let him answer. Don't fill the pause."

Three rings. A breath. Hers, then his. Behind it, a kettle and the hum of a monitor.

"It's the woman from the Crissy bench," she said. "You said after nine. One question, then I'll let you go. INTAKE EXCEPTION 0001. Is it a piece of cloth, about the size of a cuff? Where's the lockbox, and who holds the key?"

She kept her pen over the page.

"Fourteen centimeters by six," Typhus Quinn said. No hello. "Woven. Non-conductive. It reads four degrees under ambient and doesn't equalize. The form says *textile, provenance unknown*. So, yes. A cuff." Paper rustled. "Lockbox L-3, Lantern control room, under the rack console. My badge doesn't open it. I've never had a door in that building not open for me."

He stopped, as if checking the sentence for errors.

"I asked Elias who holds the key. He said it isn't a person. It's a role. *M-Ops.* He wouldn't say the rest on a work phone." A longer pause. "That's two answers. You owe me one. Why is a piece of cloth colder than the room it's in?"

On the bond, Abernathy had gone very still.

*Oi. Yeah. Still here, love. Bit cold round the edges, that's all. Like a hand left out of the blanket. Don't fuss.* A beat. *That cuff's mine. I can feel it in the drawer, the way you feel a tooth that's been pulled. Ask it plain. No heat.*

She'd asked it plain. She answered the same way.

"Because it isn't from the room." Level. "It's something's livery, and it's been somewhere the room never was. That's the true answer, and it's all I can give you on a phone. It belongs to someone who's lost it. He wants it back."

She wrote *M-Ops. Role, not person.* and underlined it twice.

"Don't let anyone move that box before Friday. If you can't stop them, count how long it takes. That's the one thing you're better at than they are."

She hung up before he could ask the follow-up.

*Eat something,* said the bond. *Sleep. I know. I'm a hypocrite. I'll tell you about Porter after. Properly.*

* * *

In the Sunset, the porch light was on, as if somebody had called ahead.

Kino had known she'd be at the door, and she was: house slippers, a hammer already in her hand, held out handle-first. Neither of them said anything about how she knew.

"Ya, Nana. Lo prometí."

He knelt at the sill with stake number two warm in his fist, the tide-smell still on it. A pinch of salt along the threshold, left to right, the way she'd taught him. The iron point went into the seam under the door.

He said it low, Spanish first, then English, so the house would hear both. *This is for the woman of the slack water. Hold her here, not in the ledger. When I walk her to the sea, you let her go.*

Three strokes. Not hard. He counted them and didn't rush. He wasn't driving it into the wood. He was asking it to be held.

The house settled. Not a creak; the long sound an old frame makes when someone heavy finally sits down. Nana laid her palm over the nailhead and kept it there a moment longer than she needed to, and the iron stayed warm as a cup someone had just put down.

"Now she's family," Nana said. "And family eats. Come inside, mijo."

Far down the hill, the tide turned at the creek mouth and didn't pull.

* * *

Overnight, Caleb's hands would not stay still.

The eleven-beat lived in the radiator, in the fridge, in his own wrist. The craving had a shape now: a little brown bottle with a dropper cap, in the left pocket of a jacket on a chair.

He took it out with two fingers, like a live round, and set it on the table beside Ava's log, in the light, where everyone could see.

"Left pocket," he said, too loud, then lower. "I said I'd tell you first. I want it. I'm not taking it. If I reach, say my name. Don't touch me. Just say it."

He counted aloud until the shaking was only his.

Ava sat up with the log. The kelp letter lay beside it, its fifth knot pulled tight and wet, as if it had just come out of the sea. She wrote one line, *Fifth knot, 9:00–9:05, wet, not salt-dry,* and did not touch it again.

In the morning the count held to nine and then broke. The tremor ran up into his shoulders, and his right hand jerked an inch toward the bottle. A muscle, not a choice. It stopped.

Nobody touched the bottle.

* * *

Thursday, 6:10 a.m. The 703 number called Malachi again. No voicemail. A text: *RECALL — ACKNOWLEDGE BY 18:00 THU.*

Someone had done the arithmetic for him. It landed three and a half hours before the pump house.

He did not answer. An answer would tell them his phone was on, and where, and that he was obedient. He pulled the battery from the second phone and put it in a drawer. The first stayed face-down on Ava's counter, ringing at nobody.

In the afternoon he drove Crisp Road once, alone, in daylight, the way a man looks at a rental. The south roll-up door, rusted the color of dried blood. A rail spur to the apron. The pilings forty yards out in water as flat as tin. It was all where Hale had drawn it.

Not quite all. The roll-up had a new brass padlock, still shiny. And by the fence sat a white Transit, nose out, plate frame reading **MO-0117**, a hangtag on the mirror, nobody inside. On the dash, a ballpoint and a clipboard. The top sheet was blank except for a line at the bottom for a signature.

Hale's door was true. Someone had also been here since Hale.

* * *

At 3:30 the light outside Ava's window had gone the grey of a dishwater afternoon, and Arenya did three things in order.

She ate the toast Ava had put in front of her. She texted Teddy: *Second piece holds till Friday. Real reason in person. Sorry.* And she asked Caleb to say his own name aloud, twice, because he'd been counting everybody else's.

"Caleb Strand. Caleb Strand."

The second came out steadier than the first. It was the first time in fourteen months he'd heard it in his own mouth and believed it.

"Pump house at nine-thirty," Arenya said to the room. "Before we go, I want Ollie Baptiste's face in my head."

Caleb gave it to Ava, who wrote. Five-nine. Thin in the shoulders. A fade growing out. A dimple on the left. A hand that kept going back to the base of his skull, like an itch. "He'll smile at you first," Caleb said. "They told him it's focus. He thinks he's lucky."

Kino sat across from him, not beside, and put his palm flat on the table next to the bottle. "Next time, start the count on my breath. Mine's slower." And to Arenya: "Ask Ollie his own name before he asks yours. People tell the truth right after they've said it." He set a line of salt along the table's edge, unspoken. "Tonight I walk the pilings before you do. If the ground hums wrong, I say one word, and we leave."

Ava turned his wrist to the light. "Warm is good. Warm and spreading goes in my log." Then, to everyone: "Clocks have drifted three minutes since Wednesday. Nobody sets a watch off a phone tonight. We set off mine."

She wrote **+3:00** on the back of Arenya's hand.

Caleb began the count again on Kino's breath. It held past nine. Past ten. Past eleven. His hand stayed on his knee.

Out on the curb, a step above Malachi, he said it without turning: "Yes. I'll say the words to him. I don't say *will try.* I say yes."

Malachi looked at the bay. "Then that's the plan."

At 6:00 p.m. exactly, the phone on the counter stopped ringing. Nobody had touched it. It did not ring again.

The light went. The bay went the color of a coin.

At 8:40 they were in the car.

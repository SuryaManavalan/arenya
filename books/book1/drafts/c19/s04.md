The T stopped between stations and the doors stayed shut.

Arenya felt it before she saw it. Every rider turned toward the bay windows at once. A nurse in scrubs. Two boys with a speaker. A man hugging a bag of laundry. Same face on all of them: someone who had forgotten something terrible and was about to remember.

Eleven seconds.

Then twelve.

Then it let go, and they went back to their phones, a little sheepish, the way people do after a sneeze in a quiet room.

Except the woman at the rear doors. She kept her palm flat on the glass, facing the water, and she cried without making a sound.

Arenya didn't look at her for long. If she did, she'd stay.

"Eleven," she said, low, to Ava and Kino. "Then twelve. It's drinking more."

Ava turned her watch toward the light. *+3:00*. It hadn't moved.

* * *

Low water had left the shoreline a wide shine of mud. Two Bayline trucks sat nosed onto the gravel with their headlights off, a work lamp clamped to one tailgate. Two laborers were unloading stakes wrapped in tarp. Between the trucks stood a man holding a light, and the light was the wrong color: grey and cold, an office at three in the morning. She couldn't see his face. Only the page he read from, in a low flat voice.

Up on Hudson, under a dead streetlight, a dark sedan idled. MILL OPERATIONS on the door. A woman at the wheel, lanyard turned backwards over her shoulder.

And out past the rod that still stood in the mud, something ankle-deep and patient was facing her.

Arenya wrote it all down. *Green ring on her thumb. Chlorine.* She got behind the nearer tailgate, out of the lamp, and put the Lamp's weight in her hand through the bag. Unlit.

*Abernathy.*

*Right, love, quick and quiet.* His voice was close and a little too cheerful, the way it got when he was frightened. *The clerk's lamp turns when he reaches a name he doesn't like. I'll say "turn." You don't step in it, and you don't look at the page. Pages look back.*

*And the thing on the water?*

His edges stuttered. Two of him for a frame. *Not borrowed, guv. Borrowed things have a seam. This one's got the tide-woman's patience and none of her manners, and it's facing you. Something's read your name tonight.*

*Great.*

*Be bad at only watching from behind the tailgate. Eight fifty-six. I'm on your shoulder.*

* * *

Ava called the count in her case-report voice, barely a breath. Eight fifty-three. Eight fifty-four.

At the waterline, Kino crouched behind the riprap with his left hand open over the edge of the shine and the jaguar mark turned up. Arenya watched the mud lean toward his palm. Not a wave. It went still instead, in a long tongue across the flat, and a voice came out of it, made of the sound a boot makes pulling free.

Arenya couldn't hear all of it. She heard enough. *Three nails. Pull one and I am loose. Loose, I go to the one who reads her.*

Then, quite clearly, toward her tailgate: *Choose for me. I am tired of men choosing.*

On the mud the laborers set the first stake to its line. The clerk lifted his page into his own light and read, ledger-dull: "One for the slack. One for the ebb. One for—"

He stopped.

The paper trembled in his hand. The light slid off it and began to sweep: riprap, rail, gravel, slow as a lighthouse.

*Turn,* said Abernathy.

Arenya folded herself smaller. One hand over the bag so the Lamp couldn't glow. Eyes shut, fixed on the gravel behind her lids. She did everything right.

Her shoe scraped anyway.

"Kino," she breathed. "Don't let her be nailed. Not for Thursday. Bargain, but don't *pin* her. Ask her price. If the light finds me, I'll answer it myself."

Somewhere to her left, Ava said, "Hey!"

Arenya opened one eye. Ava had walked off the platform with her phone held up, screen blazing white like a flare, waving it at the mud. "Is this the right place for the sunset kayak thing? My phone says the tide's *wrong*."

It was the stupidest thing Arenya had ever seen. It was the bravest thing Ava had ever done. Arenya would tell her so later, and Ava would say it was a clinical response to proximity.

Up Hudson, a car alarm began to scream.

* * *

The laborers turned toward the horn. The clerk didn't.

A hand settled on the top of Arenya's head, or something that felt like one: Caleb, low and silent beside her in the dark. He had left the car. He wasn't supposed to be here. He kept his eyes off the page and his hands where anyone could see them, and he said nothing at all.

Then Malachi stepped past her.

He'd come out of the dark behind the sedan, and he walked into the line between the light and the tailgate as if he were crossing a lobby. Coat open. Hands empty.

"Evening. Federal, Department of Energy. Nobody's in trouble yet. I'd like to see the permit for those stakes."

The beam found him. It crossed his chest and *stuttered*, the way a scanner chirps at a barcode it has already rung up.

The clerk glanced at his page. Back at Malachi. Something like professional courtesy crossed his face.

"Permit's on file," he said. "Under your name, sir. You're the collateral."

The beam slid past Malachi's hip. The edge of it came to rest on Arenya's shoe.

It was cold as low tide. It sat there, patient, a finger on a sleeve. The clerk didn't look down. He wrote one word in his margin without looking at the paper. Arenya could hear the pen.

*He knows someone's here,* she thought. *He doesn't know who.*

She tucked her foot in slowly, as if pulling it out of the light could un-ring it. She kept her chin on her chest.

"Collateral," Malachi said, mild as a man confirming a reservation. "Then I'm entitled to see the instrument. A lease with a blank signature line isn't a lease. Who circled it in red?"

Behind his back, two fingers moved low against his coat. *Down. Don't speak.*

Over on Hudson, the sedan's window had come down two inches. The woman with the green ring was holding a clipboard to the glass. A Bayline intake sheet, one signature line circled in red.

Blank.

The sedan pulled away, unhurried, toward the marina road. She hadn't been surprised to see him. Arenya had the impression, absurd and awful, that she was sorry for him.

* * *

At the water, the tongue of slack had curled around Kino's wrist. Arenya could see his shoulders from here. Square. Not afraid, or too afraid to show it.

"No nails," he was saying. "I didn't pull three out of you on Tuesday so they could put three back. But you don't go to her, *abuela*. She's got a lamp, a dead woman's canister, and a light coming at her right now. She can't carry the tide too. Not this week."

He turned his arm over, the Lady's thumbprint and the ruled stroke the clerk's people had written on him, side by side.

"Come to me. I was raised for this. Twenty years of being ready for something to choose me, and nobody has. So I'm asking. Loose, you come up the creek to my door, not hers. Salt on the sill. Water in the bowl. I'll walk you to the sea myself." A breath. "That's an offer, not an order. Name your price. If it's no, I'll pull the nail anyway."

Nobody ordering anything. Arenya hadn't known he could do that. She wasn't sure he had either.

The water was quiet a long moment. Then: *"You asked. Nobody asks."*

And a price. His own nail, the one he'd pulled out of her. Drive it into his own door-sill with his own hand. Held by a house, and not a ledger.

*"But their nail goes in at the turn. Spoil it first."*

"Yes," Kino said. It wasn't much of a word. "My nail, my sill. Done."

"Thirty," said Ava, from somewhere she shouldn't be.

The laborer lifted the maul.

Kino didn't throw like a pitcher. He threw the way you salt a doorway: low and flat from the hip, a pinch fanned off the side of his hand, timed to the tide and not to the man. He let it go on the slack's outbreath. The salt rode the still water across the mud to the foot of the stake. *Sal para la casa,* he said. *No para el libro.*

The maul came down.

The sound was wrong. Iron on iron, flat and dead, a bell struck underwater. The laborer frowned at his own hands. The stake stood in the mud, holding nothing. A thin white rime crawled up it from the waterline and stopped at the first notch.

The cold went out of the air. The slack slid away from Arenya's tailgate without touching it, out along the flat toward the culvert and the Sunset.

*Held,* said the mud, almost shy. *By a house.*

The clerk felt the break before he heard it. He looked at his page and the line he could no longer finish. He gave Malachi a small, tired, collegial nod.

"The instrument is executed Thursday, sir. You'll be asked to sign the blank." He folded the page into his breast pocket, face in. "Everyone is."

To the laborers: "Leave the other two. The slack's gone off."

The work lamps clicked out.

Out past the piling the foghorn sounded, and Ava counted it without meaning to.

Twelve.

* * *

Afterward, Arenya stayed crouched at the tailgate longer than she needed to. Her shoe was still cold.

On the mud, an extra stake leaned where nobody would ever drive it. Gulls had started up again. Caleb's alarm was still going two blocks back, and he muttered something about the fob, and nobody laughed, and then Ava did, a little crack of a laugh with no air in it.

Kino stood up from the riprap. He had one hand in his hoodie pocket, around something that had gone quiet.

"Typhus's number is at nine," Arenya said. Her phone was warm against her thigh. She didn't take it out.

"Not yet," said Malachi. He was looking at the road where the sedan had gone. *Collateral*, said his face, and nothing else.

Later, under the dome light of a parked car, Ava would take out the kelp letter and count the knots twice.

There were five. At dinner there had been four.

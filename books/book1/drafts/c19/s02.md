Wednesday didn't happen in order.

It came in pieces, and the pieces were all in a hurry.

Teddy called before the car cleared the Presidio gate. "Six hundred words, front of the metro page. You know what happened? Monolith's comms office phoned my editor at six-forty. Asking about my *sources*. Plural."

"Give me till Friday," Arenya said. "I'll have a name. Not Monolith's. And Teddy, print the bay. Don't print *sources*."

She didn't mention the keycard. She wrote it in the notebook and underlined it twice.

On Ava's phone the aggregator had already got there first. SAN FRANCISCO'S BAY IS ACTING STRANGE. A photo of the Alameda gauge. A harbor pilot: *It's like the water's holding its breath.*

Which was either good journalism or a confession.

* * *

At 9:15 Rodel left a voicemail, in his careful notary English, and he sounded frightened in a polite way. Someone had left a crate of oranges at the Colma office. For the Sowri household. No card. Which he was obliged to tell her he had not accepted.

"Don't accept it," she told his machine. "Don't touch it. Please don't open it. Photograph it from the door."

Polite fear was still fear.

The photo came that afternoon. Oranges on his office carpet, perfect, every one of them turned the same way. Toward the door.

* * *

The clocks ran fast all morning.

The board at 22nd Street showed a train *departing* while it was still pulling in. Kino's phone and the dashboard disagreed by forty seconds, then ninety. Nobody said it, but they all knew it. The Mill was drinking hurry, and the city was overpaying.

Ava logged it. She picked one honest clock, the GPS on her old watch, and wrote the offset beside every entry, as if the truth might hold still if someone kept books on it.

At 10:58 they stopped at Nana's mailbox in the Sunset. Between a PG&E bill and a pizza menu was a pink carbon, folded in thirds, no envelope. Two-part form. The handwriting was shaky and pressed so hard it had embossed.

*Thursday, after the low water. They want her off the hill and onto the flats. I can tell you where they'll set it. I need you to tell El Contador I helped. Colma, the 110 stop, tonight 7. Come alone or don't come. —H*

Her wrist went cold where the frostbite was.

She read it standing up. Then she handed it to Malachi without a word.

"Nobody writes like that," she said, "unless they're scared or want us to think so."

Kino took it by the edges and smelled it once. "Pencil dust. Cold stairwell. No smoke." He folded it back in thirds. "Written on his knee, standing."

"Don't go alone," Malachi said. "People who ask to be vouched for are usually guilty of something worse."

He didn't forbid it. That was the thing about Malachi. He never did.

*Abernathy,* she said on the bond, *tell me about Porter. Even just the shape.*

For a long time there was nothing. Then:

*It's a weight, love. Like a word you've said so many times it's gone smooth. When the page opened I said "yes" before I'd had my own say. Like answering to your name in a crowd before you've turned round.*

A pause.

*And the voice that said it wasn't this one. Flatter. Older. Tired, and sort of polite.*

*I don't think it's a thing I did,* he said. *Feels more like a thing I was called. Don't know if that's better.*

It wasn't. She didn't say so.

*The clocks running fast felt familiar,* he added. *Not frightening. Familiar. That's what bothers me.*

* * *

At 1:20 they pulled over on Judah outside a laundromat. Its clock said 1:23. Ava's watch said 1:20.

"Plus three," Ava said, and wrote it down.

She had Caleb's hands across the console. They were cold to the second knuckle, and the tremor was fine, a hum more than a shake, like a wire with current in it. When he tried to hold a pen still against the dash, it drew a small seismograph.

"I'm not driving," Caleb said. He'd said it before anyone else could.

Malachi took the wheel and adjusted the mirror for his own height, no comment.

Caleb sat with his palms up on his knees as though they belonged to somebody he was babysitting. "It's louder," he said, flatly. "The off-beat. Everything that isn't eleven. I can hear it from here." He looked, for a second, almost pleased. Then not.

Malachi's day had been paper. The Tunnel Avenue lease: Bayline Materials LLC as tenant, guaranteed by Applied Field Research, Inc. The signature a stamped initial. *M.*

He said, to nobody, "A stamp is just a man who doesn't want to be there."

He'd bought Corinne two days with a single line. *Friday. A name you can file is a name that can be subpoenaed. Give me the yard first.* He was not paying the two days. He was only renting them.

By then the split was set. Malachi to Colma at seven, with Caleb in the car at the curb, wired, in earshot, not driving, not walking. Arenya, Kino and Ava to Second piling at 8:56. Ava would call the clock. Kino would take the water. Typhus's card would stay in Arenya's pocket until nine.

Kino had spent his noon in Nana's kitchen. She'd pressed a pinch of salt into his palm without being asked and closed his fingers over it. *Con respeto, mijo. Sin pedir nada.* The second stake rode against his ribs inside the hoodie, humming low, in eleven.

It was a plan. All of them agreed on it. Arenya sat with it for a moment and found she didn't want to look at it.

Ava hadn't moved. She was holding a folded burrito wrapper, a small square, in one hand, and she had not said a word since *plus three*.

"Hey," Ava said. "You ate. Good. How are you doing? Not the plan. You. I never asked yesterday."

And she waited. Actually waited.

Arenya opened her mouth to say *fine*, and the word wouldn't come.

So she looked at the laundromat clock instead, the one lying by three minutes, and told the truth to its face.

"Not good." She turned the empty coffee cup in her lap. The one from Crissy Field. She hadn't been able to throw it out. "Monday I buried my aunt in somebody else's grave. Tuesday I found out a stranger paid a hundred and one dollars to put my name in a temple book. This morning a woman with red nails went through my bag and I stood there doing math about angles."

A breath.

"I used to write *ten signs your cat is secretly depressed*. I was good at it. I knew which one to put at number seven so you'd keep scrolling."

She made herself look up.

"Here's the worst part. I'm not scared the way I should be. I'm scared the way you are on a roller coaster. Some part of me has wanted this my whole life. The cracks in the world. And now I'm standing in one, and people are dying in it, and my family's in it, and I still..." She stopped. "I still like being right. That's an ugly thing to find out about yourself at a laundromat on Judah."

Ava didn't say anything clinical. For once she didn't have a symptom for it.

"Thank you for asking," Arenya said, quieter. "And for waiting. Nobody's done that since Sunday. Ask me again after tonight? I'll have a better answer. Or a worse one. But it'll be honest."

She took the wrapper out of Ava's hand, because Ava had been holding it like a promise, and put it in her bag with the cup.

Across the street the laundromat clock ticked over to 1:27.

It was, by every other clock, 1:24.

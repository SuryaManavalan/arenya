Colma after dark was a town of the dead with a few streetlights for the living.

Malachi sat at the far end of the bench in the 110 shelter, coat collar up, and looked at the cemetery wall. A mile of brick and cypress and wrought iron. A marble angel lit by somebody's solar lamp. The bench was cold enough to feel through denim.

His phone said 7:03. His watch said 7:00. Down the road the Serra Bowl sign said 7:05, and while he watched it said 7:06. Everything in the city had drifted three minutes fast since the morning, and he had stopped being angry about it. He noted the gap and let it be a number.

Across El Camino a SamTrans bus idled at the curb. Doors open. Cabin lights on. The route sign dark. Nobody had got off, and no one sat at the wheel.

A bus that wasn't going anywhere was a message, or a cage.

Next to him on the bench someone had left an orange, its navel turned toward the road. He didn't touch it.

"Caleb," he said, to the wire at his collar, without turning his head. "The bus. Tell me if anything in it moves."

*Copy,* said the wire, flat and low. *Staying in the car. Hands are bad, so I'm not driving. Nothing's moved. If I say* orange, *you get up. Don't ask me why.*

Forty feet back, in the dark car, Caleb was counting. The lamps buzzed. The cypresses creaked. Everything in Colma moved to one slow rhythm, and the bus was the only thing that didn't drop a beat. It kept perfect time with nothing at all. That was what was wrong with it. He watched the windows and the gap beneath the chassis and never once looked left.

"You wrote that you'd be early, H," Malachi said to the empty shelter. "I'm on time. I'm alone, as asked. If you want to be vouched for, you'll have to be seen."

Then he let the silence work.

The bench creaked under a second weight.

The man had not been there, and now he was. Fifties. A Carhartt jacket gone grey at the seams, work boots with the steel showing at one toe. A weathered, ordinary face, the face of a man who drove a van for a living. His hands lay folded and very still. He smelled of turned earth and diesel.

His boots were dry. No wet grass. He could only have come through the gate.

"Early," Hale said. "Like I said."

In the car, Caleb knew the voice before his brain caught up. The van. The dark. *Keep your head down, son.*

"Don't look at the bus, Mr. Shawani. It's not for you. It's for me, if this goes wrong." He didn't smile. "The Counter sends transport when a balance comes due. Doors open, no driver. You get on, or it waits, and it's very patient. That one's been waiting nine days."

He nodded at the orange without touching it. "That's not mine either. Rodel's people leave them for the dead. Somebody thinks one of us is."

Across the road the bus doors gave a soft pneumatic sigh, like something breathing out. They did not close.

"I can give you the Thursday site," Hale said. "Where the lattice goes up. Which door Team Four uses. Where the boy's people will be standing when it blows. What I need is your word to El Contador that I'm worth more off that bus than on it. Your *word*. Not your name." A pause. "I know what you did with your name."

Malachi looked at the orange, and then at the dry boots.

"You came through a chained gate. I'll assume you know what that tells me." He kept his hands on his knees, where they could be seen. "I won't give you my name again, Mr. Hale. You've already sold it. I'll give you something smaller and harder to take back."

He counted it out.

"One. I'll carry what you tell me to El Contador exactly as you say it. No edits in your favor or against. Two. I'll say you came unarmed and spoke first. That much I've seen. Three. If the Thursday site is true, I'll say so. If it's false, I'll say that too, and the vouch dies with it." He turned his head at last. "I won't promise you're worth more off the bus. I'll promise you were worth hearing."

Hale listened without moving. When it was done he breathed out through his nose. It might have been a laugh, from a man out of practice.

"Worth hearing. That's fair. That's more than fair."

Across the road the bus door sighed again and stopped halfway, as if it were listening too.

"Now the price of mine," Malachi said. "Who bought my name? Say that first. Then give me the door."

*That's Hale,* Caleb murmured through the wire, barely a breath. *Four. He wrote the grey R on the card. He knows how a vouch lands and he picked it on purpose. Make him name what he's owed.* A swallow, audible. *I'm still in the car.*

"I'll tell you true, and you won't like how little it is." Hale kept his eyes on the orange. "I didn't sell it to a face. I sold it to a lease. Bayline Marine Services put it on their books as an asset, paid for in water rights. Somebody wanted a man who could talk to things that live under the Bay. Your name is a key to that. The guarantor on the paper is Applied Field Research. Signed with one letter." He shrugged. "M. I never met M. I met a woman in a grey suit at the Embarcadero with a pen that wrote in salt. I got eleven seconds of her and a check that cleared."

Then he gave the door, plainly, like a delivery address.

"Thursday. Hunters Point. The old drydock pump house off Crisp. Team Four goes in through the south roll-up at 9:30. The lattice goes live at 9:41, and it doesn't run off a coil. It runs off the *pilings*. That's what the stakes were for. They're tuning the water." He looked toward the car, the dark windshield. "Strand's old crew stands the apron. Dorsey. Pruitt. The new kid. When the water answers, the apron's the first thing it takes back."

The bus door closed. It didn't slam. It eased shut with a polite hiss, and the cabin lights went out one row at a time, back to front. The bus pulled from the curb with no one at the wheel. It did not signal. It turned north toward the city and was gone.

Hale watched it go. His folded hands were shaking now.

"That's provisional. It comes back if your third part goes the wrong way." He stood, and left the orange where it lay. "Your name's still on their books. I can't buy it back. But a lease can be broken if the asset won't perform."

Malachi stayed seated. The man had earned that much, not being loomed over.

"South roll-up, 9:30. Lattice at 9:41, off the pilings. Apron: Dorsey, Pruitt, the new kid. I have it word for word." He touched nothing. "A lease that breaks if the asset won't perform. Whose asset, Mr. Hale? Mine, or the Bay's?"

He watched the shaking hands, not the face.

"And the woman with the salt pen. Eleven seconds. Tell me one thing about her I could find again."

Behind them a car window came down with a grinding sound. Cold air and the smell of turned earth came in.

"Hale." Too loud. Caleb pulled it back down. He didn't get out. "It's Strand. Fourteen months on your team, and I don't remember most of it. That's what the Crown is for."

Hale's back was half to the car. He waited. Then he turned his head.

"The new kid. How long's he been on Vesper? Does he know what's in it, or did they tell him it's focus?" Caleb's hands shook against his knees. "You said the apron's the first thing the water takes. Then you knew when you wrote the card. That's why you're standing here. Not for the lease."

"Yours," Hale said to Malachi first, without hesitating. "The Bay already belongs to itself. Nobody leases that. They leased *you*, to talk to it for them. If you won't talk, or you say the wrong thing, the paper's worthless." He took longer over the second question. "Sea-glass ring. Green, on her thumb. Lanyard on backwards, so I wouldn't see the card. She smelled of pool chlorine. When she left she didn't go to a car. She walked to the Ferry Building and took the Alameda boat."

Then he turned fully to the open window, and something in the ordinary face gave.

"Ollie," he said. "Ollie Baptiste. Twenty-two. Six weeks on it. They told him focus. They told all of you focus." He swallowed. "Yeah, Strand. I knew when I wrote the card. That's why I wrote it."

"I'm not asking you to forgive me," Caleb said. "I'm asking you to pull the kid off the apron."

"I can't walk him off. If I pull him, they know I talked, and the bus is back before morning." Hale shook his head once. "It has to be someone he's never met who knows the words." He looked at the hands on Caleb's knees. "You know the words. You used to say them to me."

He went back the way he'd come, through the iron gate. On the far side he pulled the loose chain through the bars and looped it closed, though it couldn't be locked from that side. He walked among the headstones without a light. The cypresses took him in eleven steps.

The orange stayed on the bench, navel to the road.

The Serra Bowl sign said 7:19. Malachi's watch said 7:16. Somewhere north a piling was filling with tide.

By two o'clock the fog had come down over Daly City and stayed, and the Sowri house had filled the way houses fill when someone dies.

Shoes were piled three deep in the entry. Folding chairs had appeared from somewhere. Aunties Arenya half-recognized sat on the sofa with their hands in their laps, and somebody's teenage son texted in the hallway with the volume of a boy being very quiet at a funeral. The kitchen smelled of coffee nobody was drinking. Amma had set Susmithaa's cracked brass lamp on the shelf in the puja alcove, beside a drugstore-framed photograph from someone's wedding: Aunty laughing, out of focus, caught mid-turn. Appa had lit the lamp an hour ago with a kitchen match. The flame was small and steady.

Arenya's right hand had stayed loose since Seton. It ached the way a hand aches after gripping something all night. Susmithaa's phone sat in the hospital bag by her feet, and it hadn't buzzed again.

Then someone knocked, twice.

Amma's hands were in the sink, so Arenya got there first. She wiped her palms on her jeans, stepped over the third layer of shoes, and opened the door.

An old woman in a pinned shawl. A young man behind her, black shirt, a heavy pot held high against his chest. Fog behind them both, so thick the houses across the street were only porch lights. She didn't need an introduction.

"You're Itzel," she said, and her voice came out rougher than she'd meant. "She said you'd come with pozole. I'm Arenya. Susmithaa's niece. Please, come in. My mother's going to want to see you."

She kept her right hand open and visible at her side. She didn't know why. It seemed like manners.

The old woman took both of Arenya's hands, pot or no pot, and held them. Her thumb passed over the right palm and stayed a half-second longer than courtesy. She didn't say what she'd found.

"Mija," she said. "Ella me habló de ti. *Mucho.*"

The young man toed off his boots without bending and set them neatly in the pile, toes out. "Lo siento mucho," he said, to the height of a mother's face, and then he didn't look at Arenya again. He looked at the lamp.

Nana Itzel looked at Arenya the way you'd look at a letter in familiar handwriting: the face first, then the open hand, then, for one unhurried second, the closed one. She reached up and laid two dry fingers on Arenya's cheek.

"Sí. You have her chin. She told me you'd be the one who opens the door." She stepped over the shoes without looking down. "Joaquín, the kitchen. Put it on the stove, low. Don't spill on her mother's floor."

He went down the hall with the pot. As he passed the alcove, the lamp flame lay down sideways.

It didn't flicker. Arenya had watched a lot of candles in the last day, and she knew what flicker looked like. This was something else. The flame leaned toward the boy flat and patient, like a dog's head turning at a footstep, and held the lean the whole length of the hall. No window was open. The furnace was off.

Her right fist answered it. A pulse ran up her wrist, warm, once, like a second heartbeat out of step with the first.

She said nothing about it. She closed the door on the fog, slid the bolt, and flexed the fingers slowly so nobody would be watching her do it.

In the kitchen the tap shut off. Amma came around the corner drying her hands on her kurta, saw the shawl, and stopped. "Itzel-amma," she said, and her voice broke on the second word. They met in the middle of the hallway, and Arenya looked at the floor to give them that. She picked up a pair of someone's sneakers that had been kicked sideways. It was something to do with her hands.

---

Kino had not stopped walking, because if he stopped the pot would tip. But his stride had shortened by half, the way it did on a trail when something in the brush went quiet all at once.

*If the flame leans when somebody walks past, you look at who walked past.*

It was him. He was the one who'd walked past.

The cold in his forearm climbed to the elbow. He kept his eyes on the kitchen doorway and his hands on the towels, set the pot on the back burner, and turned the knob down to the lowest blue ring. He left his hand on it a second too long. It was the one thing in the room he understood.

Then he looked back down the hall, not at the girl, but at the lamp. It was still lying toward him, through the wall. Over Amma's shoulder he found Nana's eyes and held them. He asked her the only way he was allowed to in this house. *¿Soy yo?*

Nana took the question and turned it over the way she turned tortillas, without hurrying. She shook her head. *No.* Then her gaze slid past him down the hall, to the girl who was flexing her right hand where she thought no one could see. Nana watched the hand. She looked back at Kino, and the corner of her mouth moved.

*Not only you.*

He read it off her mouth the way he'd read a track in wet clay: the size of it first, then which way it was going. *No* meant he wasn't the wrong thing in this house. That loosened something in his chest he hadn't known was clenched. The second part took longer to land, and when it did it landed lower and colder than relief.

---

When he came back into the hall, Arenya was there, with a foot of space between them and somebody's sneakers in her arms. Her question was already out. The lamp had eased upright, but it trembled toward the two of them, as if it couldn't decide.

"Her grandson," he said. A foot of hallway was close, and he didn't back up, but he lowered his eyes to her collarbone, which was polite and gave away nothing. "Joaquín. Kino." The corner of his mouth almost moved. "I saw it. I don't know why it did that. I'm not going to guess in your mother's house." Then, because it was true and she'd just buried an aunt: "She sent my grandmother tea bags with too much sugar. Nana kept every one."

Something almost like a laugh caught in Arenya's throat. She put the sneakers down before she did something stupid with them. "She put sugar in rasam once. Said it was for balance. Nobody argued."

She looked at the lamp. It wavered between them, and she decided she was too tired to pretend she hadn't noticed.

"I'm not going to guess in my mother's house either," she said, low, under the murmur from the living room. "But I'd like to talk to you and your grandmother later. Somewhere with fewer aunties. Not about the lamp. About what she told Susmithaa. And what Susmithaa told me." She tipped her head at the front window, at the white pressed against the glass. "And thank you, for whoever's out there. I'd rather someone watched that street than not."

"He's called Ix. He doesn't like being thanked. He takes it as an insult and sulks for a week." Kino moved half a pace sideways, so that they stood shoulder to shoulder facing down the hall, the way you stand next to someone at a trailhead to look at the same ridge. If the flame wanted to lean, it could lean at both of them at once. "I'll tell him. Those go by the door, by the way. Toes out. In case anybody has to leave fast."

The flame sank back to a small upright bead, the way a dog lies down when the people it guards finally stop moving.

---

The house filled in around them. Amma settled Nana in the good chair. Two aunties from the Fremont side wanted to know whether pozole was like sambar, and Nana told them gravely that it was exactly like sambar, completely different. Steel bowls came out, then paper ones when the steel ran out. Somebody's uncle asked for extra chili and was told, in a tone Susmithaa would have known, not to be rude about the chili.

Kino counted exits by habit, then faces. At nineteen he stopped. There were twenty.

The twentieth stood by the bookshelf near the alcove: mid-fifties, grey cardigan, a paper bowl of pozole he hadn't touched. He had the kind of face you forgot while you were still looking at it. Nobody was talking to him. The aunties' eyes passed over him the way water passes around a stone. When Appa came by with the ladle, he filled the bowls on either side and skipped the man's, without seeming to notice he had.

The man wasn't looking at the photograph, or the lamp, or Nana. He was looking at Arenya's right hand. He watched it the way an appraiser looks at a ring in a pawnshop window: patient, and pricing.

Kino felt his eyes try to slide off him, toward the kitchen, the lamp, the window, anywhere else. He kept his voice level and a little warmer than before, a polite stranger remarking on weather. "Arenya. Quick, and don't look. Who's the man by the bookshelf? Gray sweater. Whose cousin is he?"

"He's not Fremont side." She said it to the steam of the bowl he'd pressed into her hand. "And he's not anyone's cousin. Everyone here has been fighting about seating since noon. I'd know." She held the bowl in her right hand on purpose, so that the hand was busy and obviously just a hand. "Kino."

"Don't walk toward him. Don't walk away. Don't ask your father about him. Appa skipped his bowl and didn't notice, and that isn't rudeness. It's *made*." He picked up a second bowl he didn't want. "I'm going to stand by the alcove with my back to the lamp, where I can see the whole room. If he comes toward you, say my name. Just that. I'll be there before you finish it."

She nodded once. He went.

She found the black screen of the television and used it, the way she'd used a laptop at Tartine. A grey cardigan, an untouched bowl, a face she already wanted to forget. She made herself keep it. *Mid-fifties. Left-handed. No ring.* She wrote him into her head like a headline she'd need later, then crossed to the sofa and sat between two aunties, who shifted without a word and patted her knee.

"Amma's going to say it's too spicy," she announced to the room, "and it isn't. Aunty would have said so."

Under her breath, to nobody in the room: *Abernathy. Look at him. Tell me what you see. Does he have light, or is he another hole in a fence? And before you answer, I haven't forgotten you told me nothing since 3:19. We're having that talk later. Right now, look.*

*Not a hole, love,* he said, close and quick, the cockney sitting on him like a borrowed coat. *Dull, middle-aged, pilot-flame colour. But he's wearing something. Ring of frost round his head, like a hatband. His light bends round it, all smoothed over, which is why your eye skids and the aunties' do too. Nobody remembers inviting him because the ring won't let 'em.* A flicker of something older under the words, half a syllable of a voice that didn't do "love." *Same cold as the corridor this morning. The grey coat were a void. This one's a bloke carrying a piece of the void on his head. Borrowed. Leashed, maybe. And he's pointed at your right hand, guv. Only that. Level as a spirit-bubble.*

*Eat your pozole,* he added. *Keep the hand busy. Keep the lad close. And the talk later? Yeah. I know. I owe you one. Two, actually.*

*Eat something, though. Proper. You've had one coffee since yesterday.*

---

Kino took the alcove with his back to the lamp and his shoulders loose, a boy in a black shirt with a bowl he didn't eat from. He didn't stare. Every ten seconds, as he'd been trained on Tam at night, he looked at something else, then came back. Staring spooks the thing you're watching. Looking away and back lets it forget you.

Behind him the flame stopped trembling and leaned hard toward the sofa. It leaned on his shoulder, almost, to see past him.

The man set his bowl down on the shelf beside a row of Agatha Christies. He crossed the living room through gaps that opened just before he reached them. An aunty shifted her chair. A child stepped back against his mother's knee. None of them looked up.

*If he moves toward her, I move first.*

Three steps from the sofa the man stopped before the garlanded photograph, put his palms together and dipped his head, correctly, the way someone does who learned it from an Indian colleague a long time ago. When he straightened he was close enough that Arenya could smell him: wool, cold air, and faintly, impossibly, frost on a windshield.

"I'm sorry for your loss," he said, kindly, in a flat Midwestern voice. "She was very good at keeping her hands still." His eyes dropped to the bowl in her right hand and came back up. "Eat. It's good pozole."

Kino had already moved. He came around the arm of the sofa and took the empty space at her shoulder where a mourner would stand, and the aunties' eyes slid off him. His did not slide off anyone. He looked straight at the man's face, the face he wanted to forget, and held it, and let himself be seen holding it. The cold in his arm answered the frost smell with a long, low ache. Behind him the lamp stood straight up, the way a guard dog stands when it's decided.

"She's eating," he said, quietly, in the voice he'd use on a hiker off the trail. "Let her."

Arenya took a spoonful, chewed, and swallowed before she answered. She looked at the knot of his tie, not his eyes. Abernathy had said not to, and she'd decided he was right about this one.

"Thank you. It's my aunt's friend's recipe. She'd be glad you liked it." She lifted the spoon a little, pointing at the Christies. "You've got a good eye for her bookshelf. Did you know her, or did you just read the spines?" It was the voice she used on PR people who said *no comment* too fast. "Kino, this is the kind of man who sends a card. I'd like to get his name before he leaves. Wouldn't you?"

"Wouldn't mind it." Kino shifted his bowl to his left hand and left his right empty and easy, the way he'd stand when a bobcat hadn't decided yet. "Names are polite. It's a house of mourning. I'm Joaquín Balam. Friend of the family. And you?"

The man looked at Kino for the first time. It was a long, mild look, a man noting the make of a car in his driveway. Then he looked back at the tie-knot of Arenya's gaze and almost smiled, as though she'd passed something small.

"We never met," he said. "I read about her. For a long time. She was a careful woman." He considered the shelf. "*Curtain* is upside down. I don't think that was an accident." And to the question beneath the question: "Bell. People call me Mr. Bell. It's on nothing, so don't bother looking."

His head turned a degree toward Kino without the rest of him moving. "Your dog's very well trained, son. He watched the street all the way through." A pause. "I came in the back at eleven. Before any of you."

He reached into his cardigan pocket. Kino's whole body tightened. The man stopped with two fingers showing and waited, patient, until the boy breathed. Then he drew out a plain white sympathy card, already sealed, no name on the envelope, and laid it on the arm of the sofa beside Arenya's knee.

"For the family. Open it when the aunties go home."

He stepped back. The gaps opened for him again, an uncle turning, a child ducking, and he walked toward the kitchen and the back door, unhurried. In five seconds he'd be through it.

Kino didn't follow. Nana had said no heroics, and a man who leaves by the back door after announcing he came in by it wants to be followed. He stayed at Arenya's shoulder, kept his hands off the card, and watched the cardigan cross the room until the kitchen doorway took it.

*Ix. Back door. Don't touch him. Where he goes, you go. Slow.*

He sent it down the line he'd had since he was nine, a thought pressed outward like a palm on a window. Out in the fog, something low and heavy turned without a sound.

He bent as if to take her empty spoon. "Don't open it," he said, for her alone. "Don't let anyone move it. Nana will know what it is. He came in at eleven, Arenya. Whatever he wanted, he got a long look at your mother's house first. Nobody sleeps here alone tonight."

Her left hand had already gone flat over the envelope, as if she were smoothing her skirt. The paper was cold through her palm. She had never had a sympathy card feel like a dead battery.

"Nobody moves this," she told the aunties on either side of her, low. "It's for my mother. I'll give it to her myself." One of them nodded without understanding. They both sat a little straighter anyway. She turned her head just enough. "He said Aunty kept her hands still. He's been reading about her for a long time. About us. About me."

Then, to the room, bright and tired and ordinary: "Amma, Itzel-amma, come eat. It's getting cold."

And under it, to the one who could hear: *Abernathy. Tell me I'm not imagining that the card is humming.*

---

Ix took the scent at the back step: wool and windshield frost, slippery as a wet bar of soap held in the teeth. It didn't let go.

Two blocks downhill the man walked to a beige Camry parked under a cypress, a rental barcode on the rear window. He got in. Then he did something Ix didn't understand and Kino, through the line, did: he reached up with both hands and *lifted something off his head*, like a man taking off a tight hat at the end of a shift. The frost smell dropped by half. What was left smelled like a tired man, and like blood. He sat with his forehead on the steering wheel a long time. A thin line of red ran from one nostril, and he wiped it on his cuff.

He made a phone call. Ix had no words, but the cadence came through the line: short, flat, a report. Then a longer pause, and something in the voice that, on a person, you would call *reluctance*.

He drove toward 280. At the on-ramp, where the fog thinned and the cars got fast, Ix stopped and looked back toward the house, waiting to be told. In the rear-view of a passing car, three characters of the plate: *7K,* and something that might have been an *R*.

*Enough,* Kino sent. *Come home. Slow, by the wet side of the street.*

The house came back to him all at once: steel spoons, aunties, the card beneath Arenya's flat hand. He picked up her abandoned bowl from the floor, a reason to be at her knee, and spoke to the steam.

"Beige Camry. Rental barcode in the back window. Plate starts 7K, maybe an R. He took something off his head in the car like a hat, and his nose bled afterward. The cold on him is made. It costs him." He set the bowl on the sofa arm. "He called someone and sounded sorry about it. The humming's real. I can feel it from here. Write the plate on your hand, not your phone. Then let's feed your mother."

She had already pulled the cheap pen from under her notebook. She turned her left wrist over and wrote on the inside of it, small block letters: *7K_R? BEIGE CAMRY. RENTAL BARCODE.* Then, under it: *NOSEBLEED. CALLED SOMEONE. SORRY.* It was the first time she had taken notes on her own skin, and her hand was steadier than she expected.

"That's the first useful thing anyone's said to me in two days," she said. She slid the card into her bag behind the notebook, next to Aunty's phone, and zipped it. "Mine until Itzel-amma looks at it." A crooked smile. "And tell Ix he's invited to the pozole. He can sulk about it."

---

The afternoon did what afternoons in a mourning house do. It went slow, and then all at once. The Fremont aunties left at four with Tupperware. The uncle who'd wanted more chili left at five, having had more chili. Appa fell asleep upright in his chair with the ladle still in his hand, and nobody took it from him.

By six the fog had come down to the porch rail and the windows were black mirrors. Four people were left in the living room. Amma sat on the floor by the alcove with her knees drawn up. Nana was in the good chair, shawl unpinned. Kino, who had not yet decided he was allowed to sit, finally did, on the floor beside Amma with his back to the wall and one knee up, the lowest in the room and next to the one person who'd been crying all day. His sleeve was pushed back. The birthmark showed in the lamplight, pale and cold. He didn't hide it and he didn't point to it. It was there in case she wanted to ask.

And Arenya, on the sofa, with her left hand still flat on an envelope that hadn't warmed by one degree in four hours.

*You're not imagining it,* Abernathy said in her ear. *Not humming. Cold. Soaked through like damp in a cellar wall, from riding too long next to that hatband. And there's a thread off the corner, guv. Fine as a spider's. Frost-light, running off toward the back door. A tag. Like a ring on a pigeon's leg. Whoever's holding the other end knows where the card is. And while it's in your hand, or your bag, they know where you are.* A pause. *Which is why he handed it over. Bit of a present, that. We could cut it, but it'd ring at the other end. Or we park it somewhere daft and let 'em follow a card to the wrong place. Your call. I'm only the squire.*

On the shelf, *Curtain* was still upside down among the Christies. The lamp burned small and straight.

"Bueno," Nana said, and breathed in like someone about to lift something heavy. "The aunties are gone. Now we talk."

Arenya lifted her left hand off the envelope for the first time in four hours. The palm was damp and numb. She held the card out flat on both palms, not yet handing it over.

"Before anything else. I think this is tagged. There's a thread on it, from the corner, running toward the back door. Like a ring on a bird's leg." She heard how that sounded and didn't stop. "I'm not going to tell you tonight how I know that. Maybe soon. But if I carry it, whoever's on the other end knows where I am. Where we are. It shouldn't go upstairs, and it shouldn't be near Aunty's phone. Can you put it somewhere that thread can't follow? And can you do it without telling them we noticed?"

Kino looked at the envelope, then past it, a little to the left, where a lamp's circle would end. "I can't see a thread. But the card's shadow doesn't sit right. It's pointing at the hall." His voice was low. "It's a mark, not a bomb. It tells him where it is and nothing else. That's why he left it and didn't take anything. Don't carry it. Don't burn it. If he's counting on us to notice, we shouldn't do the thing he can predict."

Nana did not take the card. She looked at it on Arenya's palms the way she would have looked at a scorpion on a tortilla: not afraid, just deciding where to put her hands. "Joaquín. The towel from the pot."

It was a thick cotton kitchen towel, still damp with steam, smelling of hominy and chile and forty years of her kitchen. She draped it over the envelope without touching the paper, folded the corners in like a tamal, and lifted the bundle in both hands. She carried it into Amma's kitchen, opened the freezer, and set it at the very back, behind a brick of frozen parathas and a bag of peas gone white with frost. She shut the door and stood with her palm flat against it.

"Cold hides in cold," she said, coming back and wiping her hands on her skirt. "And it smells of my kitchen now. Whoever holds the string will feel a house, and soup, and a girl who stayed home. Let them feel that." She sat down heavily. "We don't cut it. Cutting is a word, and they would hear it. We just bore them."

Arenya turned her right hand over in her lap and opened it, palm up, in the lamplight, where Amma could see it if she looked.

"Amma, I lied to you at the hospital. The wrist. It wasn't the steering wheel." She kept her voice quiet. "I'll tell you everything I can, tonight, if you'll let me. I'm done doing this alone."

Amma was already looking. She got up on her knees, took the hand in both of hers, and turned it over the way she used to check for splinters when Arenya was six. Her thumb stopped on the heel of the palm.

"Your aunt's hands were warm like this," she said, very quietly. "Always. December, January, it didn't matter. I used to say it was the ghee. She used to laugh." Her grip tightened. "Your father knew. In March, he knew something, and he didn't tell me, and now *you* —" She stopped. Breathed. When she looked up her eyes were wet and furious and completely steady. "You don't lie to me again, kanna. Not about this. Tonight. I don't care how strange."

"Sí. Everything," Nana said gently, the way you clear your throat before interrupting a prayer. She lifted her chin toward the bookshelf, toward the one spine standing on its head. "But first. Your aunt turned a book upside down, mija. That man saw it in one look. I have been sitting in this chair for four hours trying not to look at it." The faintest smile. "She always made me find the sugar myself, too."

Behind Kino's head the lamp flame didn't lean. It stretched a full inch taller, toward the shelf.

Kino got up quietly so Amma wouldn't startle. He didn't go to the bookshelf. He checked the back door, the deadbolt and the chain, and put his palm to the cold front glass: nothing but fog, and under the porch one slow heartbeat that wasn't his. He came back and stood at the end of the shelf with his hands in his pockets, close enough to be a wall and far enough not to be reaching.

"It's your aunt's book," he said to Arenya. "Your hand should be the one that turns it. Whatever she hid there, she hid it for you. I'll watch the doors."

She gave Amma's hand a squeeze and stood. "Two minutes," she said. "Then all of it, from the beginning, even the parts that make me sound insane."

Her knees cracked and she didn't care. At the shelf she stopped close enough to smell dust and old paper and, under it, the faint ghost of sandalwood. Seven years of Christies, lined up by title in her careful way. Except *Curtain*, spine to the wall.

Poirot's last case. Of course she'd picked that one.

She laid two fingers of her left hand on the spine. Her right hovered an inch behind it, and she felt the heat leave her palm and gather in the book. She slid it out, turned it right side up, and held it against her chest with both arms.

"You're being very dramatic, Aunty," she whispered. "Fine. I'm reading."

She opened the cover.

The book fell open by itself at the place where something had been pressed flat for a long time: chapter seventeen, the spine cracked white. A temple receipt, thin and pink, the carbon-copy kind, from the Shiva-Vishnu Temple in Livermore. *Archana, 1 — $11.00.* Dated the second Sunday in March, three days after whatever she had told Appa. On the name line, in a volunteer's ballpoint: *S. Sowri.* Under *Nakshatra,* someone had written Arenya's birth star, not Susmithaa's.

She had paid for a prayer in Arenya's name.

On the back, in fountain-pen ink gone slightly brown, small and upright:

> *Ari —*
> *The lamp knows who. Itzel knows the rest.*
> *Don't let them count you.*
> *Keep your hands still until you can't. Then don't.*
> *— S.*
> *(Not too much sugar. I know.)*

The last line was in fresher ink, as if she had come back and added it later.

When Arenya's right hand closed on the receipt, the heat went out of her palm all at once, the way it had when the flame leaned. A heartbeat later it came back stronger. Behind Kino, the lamp's small flame split in two, side by side, like two people standing at a trailhead. For one second.

Then it was one again.

In the good chair Nana Itzel had her hand over her mouth.

She read it twice standing up, because the first time the words didn't go in. *Ari.* No one had called her that since she was nine. She had forgotten that Aunty ever did.

"She paid for a prayer," she said, small and flat. "In March. In my name. With my star. Eleven dollars. She kept it in a murder mystery for seven months."

She crossed the carpet and knelt in front of her mother and put the receipt in her hands, writing side up. She didn't explain. Amma read it three times. On the third her lips moved on *Ari.* She folded it along its old crease, very carefully, and held it against her sternum with both hands. She didn't cry. That seemed to cost her more than crying would.

"*Don't let them count you,*" Arenya said to Nana. "That's the net. That's what I saw in the dream. She knew it in March. Who are *they*? And what is the lamp supposed to know about me?"

Kino hadn't read it. It wasn't his. But the lamp was at his shoulder, and he had seen it split in the corner of his eye: two flames, side by side. *One of them is her. The other one is a door.* He did not let himself think about which of them he would have wanted to be. The cold in his forearm eased. It didn't leave. It settled into something he could carry, like a pack after the first mile. He took a half step closer to the window, so that anyone coming for the sound in this room would come through him first.

Nana unpinned the brooch from her shawl and turned it over in her fingers: a small silver jaguar, worn nearly smooth.

"I know what she told me over tea. Eleven years of Thursdays. Not more." She looked at the lamp, not at Arenya. "*They* have no one name. My grandmother called them *los que cuentan.* The ones who count. In her time they came with priests' ledgers. In my mother's time, with government clipboards, asking who in the village had dreams. Now they come in cardigans with rental cars. Different coats. Same hands. They write down people like your aunt. People who carry something. Then the people go quiet, and nobody can say quite when it started."

She set the brooch on her knee.

"Your aunt carried a light. Not a metaphor, mija. She said it came down her mother's side, a long way, and never to the eldest, never to the one you'd expect. She said that in March she felt it start to *look* for somewhere to go. She said it was looking at you." Nana's mouth went thin. "I told her to tell you. She said you'd laugh, and then you'd write an article about it."

"I'm not going to laugh." Arenya said it before Nana had finished, then made herself say it slower. "She was right that I'd have written an article. I'd have been wrong, but I'd have written it. I'm sorry I made her think she couldn't tell me."

"The lamp knows who the light belongs to now," Nana went on. "But it split, and I have been on this earth seventy-eight years and never seen a house-flame do that. My Lady turned toward a door last night. Your aunt's lamp just showed me two people standing in one." She looked at Arenya steadily. "I don't know what that means. I won't pretend I do, in your mother's house. But I know it isn't nothing."

Arenya got her coffee-stained notebook out of her bag and set it on her knee without opening it. Tonight it was the wrong tool, but holding it was still better than holding nothing.

"I'm going to start with the part that sounds worst," she said. "Eight weeks ago people started dying. They froze mid-sentence, stared at someone, and dropped. I found nine. Last night I dreamed the whole thing. A net, four men, light pulled out of people like thread." She turned her right hand over on her knee. "And this morning a man in a cardigan walked into my mother's house and looked at this hand like a price tag."

She breathed out. "Nana, you said two people in one door. Kino, you said a door. If that's me and him, then the lamp's telling us something we'll have to work out together. Not tonight. But I want it said out loud, in front of my mother, so nobody finds out later that we hid it." She looked at his back. "And Kino. Whatever's on your arm. You don't have to show me. But I'd like to know if it's cold."

He turned from the window. It took a moment, and when he did his face was plain and unguarded, the face he'd have at a trailhead at four in the morning with nobody there to see. He crossed the carpet and stopped an arm's length from the sofa, where Amma could see him, and pushed the black sleeve to the elbow.

The mark was a pale rosette, a thumbprint pressed into brown skin. It looked colder than the room.

"Since the dawn watch," he said. "Since the mountain turned toward a door. I thought it meant she'd turned from me." He didn't look at Nana. "I've dreamed it for weeks. She rolls away and I slide off her shoulder. I didn't tell anyone until this morning. It hurt. It still does, a little. I'm not going to pretend it doesn't, in front of your mother." He lowered the sleeve. "But it's cold when you're near. It's cold when the lamp leans. Not at you. *Toward* something. I think we're both pointing at the same door, Arenya. I'd rather stand beside it than argue with it." His mouth moved, tired. "The training says I'm supposed to be the one who walks through. I'm trying to learn the other job."

She didn't say anything clever. She was out of those. She unbuttoned her left cuff, then her right, pushed the sleeve past the wrist and held the arm out between them under the lamp, so Kino, Nana and her mother could all see. The coin-mark sat on the pulse point, small and round, the grey of an old nickel. Under it, in ballpoint, a plate number and *SORRY.*

"Same cold. Since the minute she died. I've been calling it a pinched nerve." She looked at Amma and kept her arm steady. "That's the lie. That's all of it. Everything else, tonight." And to Kino, quieter: "Beside, not through. I can do beside."

Amma looked at the boy's sleeve, at her daughter's open hand, at the receipt pressed to her chest. She got up off the floor the way mothers do, one hand on the sofa arm, all at once and with no help.

"Then nobody goes home tonight." It wasn't an offer. "Itzel-amma, you take Susmithaa's room. The sheets are clean. She changed them Sunday." Her voice caught on *Sunday* and she walked straight through it. "Joaquín, the sofa. There's a blanket in the cupboard. If your dog wants the porch, he can have the porch." She looked at Arenya last. "And you start from the beginning, kanna. The nine people. All of it. I'll make tea."

She went into the kitchen. The freezer hummed. The pot of pozole ticked on its lowest blue ring.

Then, from Arenya's zipped bag on the floor, a sound none of them had heard all afternoon: a short, polite double buzz. Through the canvas, Susmithaa's phone lit up blue.

The bag's mesh pocket was thin enough to show the lock screen. Arenya didn't have to open it.

At 2:47 in the morning the house had the particular silence of people pretending to sleep.

Amma's breathing from the armchair was too even. Appa's door was shut, but a line of light lay under it. Nana snored in Susmithaa Aunty's room with her shoes still on, and the lamp burned low and steady between the couch and the floor, where Kino lay on two folded blankets.

Then the flame straightened, all at once, like a dog lifting its head.

Arenya was sitting up before she knew why. Something cold came through the back screen door without opening it. Down the hall the kitchen tiles went grey with frost, tile by tile, in a line toward the front room, and then the line stopped, because Ix stepped out of it and shook the frost off like water. The dog lay down across Kino's shins, heavy and trembling, and pushed its muzzle into his palm.

Kino closed his hand around the muzzle the slow way, the way you hold a hurt animal, and breathed with it. Four in, hold, four out. Frost melted off its fur and soaked into his jeans. He didn't seem to notice. His eyes had gone somewhere else, and she watched his face take the pictures one at a time.

"Don't pet him yet," she whispered, flat and low, so that Amma's fake breathing could stay fake. "Tell me in order. What's cold, what's warm, what's new."

The notebook was under the pillow. She uncapped the pen with her teeth.

"Serramonte," Kino said, barely sound. "Second floor. The periodical racks are strung. Frost thread, rack to rack, ankle height, like a snare for rabbits. There's a ring of chairs by the window, too many to count. Every one is cold except one." He swallowed. "That one's warm. Like somebody just stood up."

She wrote it left-handed, ugly: *Serramonte, 2nd fl. Strung racks. Circle of chairs. One warm.*

"And Ix smelled something he's never smelled before," Kino said. "Wet stone. Ink. Something old that's still keeping its accounts."

"That's not Hale," she said. "Hale smells like a hotel lobby, I'd bet money. Somebody's keeping *books*."

The lamp flame bent hard toward the dog. Then, for the first time since the funeral home, it bent *away* from him—away from Kino, away from Ix—and leaned toward her open right hand.

Kino saw it. She watched him see it. It went into his face like a splinter under a nail, and he let it hurt for exactly as long as it took to breathe once, and then she watched him put it somewhere and shut the lid.

"Later," he said, to no one.

Her palm was tingling. Not a burn; a fingertip, going slow, as if someone were writing on her to see whether she'd flinch.

*Brooke told me not to flinch once,* she thought. *Funny what sticks.*

She didn't close the hand. She turned it up to the lamp and watched it the way you watch a phone that's about to ring.

A stroke, straight down. A hook, a curve, a flat base. Then another figure beside it. Then a long pause, long enough to breathe in. And one last stroke dragged under the pair from heel to fingers, ruled and straight, like the line under a sum.

*12.*

"Twelve," she said. Her voice came out clipped and small. "Underlined." The cold wasn't on her skin. It was in the lines of her palm, as if someone had cut them a little deeper. "It's not writing on me, Kino. It's *filing* me."

The lamp stood up tall and white at its tip. The warmth in her hand rose to meet the cold. It didn't wipe the number out. It pushed, and the two of them sat under her skin like ice in tea that hadn't decided yet.

In the kitchen the freezer ticked. Ticked again. A thin rime crept out along the seal of the door, an inch, as if whatever was inside wanted to be closer.

*Arenya. Love. Don't flinch, you're doing ever so well.*

Abernathy was on the bond, close and low, the cockney pitched too high and too cheerful. *Twelve, guv, grey ink, proper tidy, ruled underneath. And it's not from the freezer or Serramonte. It's coming up from* under. *Under the floor, under the bulk, somewhere wet and stony. Never been there, me. Don't fancy it.*

*Keep the hand open. Don't clench. A neat closed fist is just what they'd want for the ledger.*

Then, with the patter going thin and something older showing through for half a word: *And there's something else coming up under the twelve. Faint. Like a watermark in paper. A little circle with rays on it. A sun, like a kiddie draws. Gone quick.*

*I know that shape, love. Don't ask me from where. It's made my coat go cold. Write it down if you must. Just don't show the boy yet.*

*Also: someone's opened Appa's door a crack. Not a spirit. A frightened man in socks. Mention it gently.*

She didn't turn her head. Kino's hand slid across the carpet and stopped an inch short of hers, palm open, and she slid hers the rest of the way until the cold from his mark and the warm from her palm had to argue it out in the gap. Then he closed the inch, two fingers over hers, brief, and let go.

"Thank you," he breathed. "For not taking it from me. It stung. Still does. But *used* is a better word than I had."

"I think it's pointing at what's been used," she said. "Not who it likes."

He put his forehead against Ix's brow and asked the only way he knew, no words, just the memory of the kitchen tiles held out like a plate. *¿Te siguieron?* She watched the lamp. He watched the door.

The answer was no. Nothing had walked behind the dog. Then Kino's mouth went tight, because there was a second picture, the one Ix was ashamed of: the edge of Colma on the way home, low wrought-iron gates in fog, a stone yard full of blank headstones waiting to be cut. As Ix had run past, something there had *noticed* it. It hadn't chased. It hadn't even turned. It had made a small tidy mark, the way a toll booth logs a plate, and let the dog go on.

"Not followed," Kino said. "*Logged.* Whoever keeps the books already knows our dog's route home."

Down the hall, the door opened the rest of the way.

"Appa," she said, without turning around. "If you're going to stand there, come sit. There's room on the couch."

He came in barefoot, in the old Cal sweatshirt he slept in, reading glasses pushed up into his hair. He was holding a long white envelope against his chest with both hands, the way you'd hold a hymnbook. He didn't look at Kino, or at the dog he couldn't see. He looked at the lamp first, and his jaw worked. Then he looked at her open palm and sat down at the far end of the couch very carefully, as if it might break.

"Meridian Mutual," he said. It wasn't a question. "I heard the phone. Earlier."

Across the flap, in Susmithaa Aunty's round handwriting: ***Ravi — only if they call.***

"She made me witness something in March," Appa said. "At a notary in Colma, by the flower shop. I signed where she pointed. She wouldn't let me read it. I told your Amma it was a will." His thumb rested on the seal and didn't break it. "Kanna, I lied to everyone in this house for seven months, and I am very bad at it." He looked up. "They called. So. Do I open it, or do you?"

From the armchair, Amma's breathing stopped being even.

"Appa." Her voice had gone soft, and she hated how young it sounded. "You're not bad at lying. You're bad at lying to *us*. That's different." She looked at the seal. *Only if they call.* "They called. Twice. Amma, you're awake. Come sit. If this is a letter, it's for all of us. Aunty's lamp is standing straight up and I'm not doing this alone."

Kino had gotten off the floor without a sound. He was behind the couch now, between the hall and the kitchen, not guarding Appa so much as walling him in. Ix pressed against his leg. "Mr. Sowri," he said, in the voice he used on old men on cold trails. "You did right to bring it out where the lamp can see. Open it slow. If the flame ducks, stop, and tell me. I'll be right here."

"I'll open it," she said. "Not because it's mine. Because you've carried it seven months and your hands are shaking. Give me the weight."

She slid a thumb under the flap, slowly, giving anyone in the room one more second to say stop.

Nobody did. The flap gave with a small dry sound, like a breath let out.

The lamp didn't duck. It leaned in a little, the way someone leans to read over your shoulder. Amma got up and didn't take the couch. She sat on the carpet at Appa's feet with her back against his shins and one hand around his ankle, and said nothing at all, which was worse than anything she could have said.

There were three things inside.

The first was a letter on heavy cream stock, folded in thirds, blue-and-silver letterhead: *MERIDIAN MUTUAL LIFE · Beneficiary Services.* Dated March 9. *Welcome to the Meridian family. We are pleased to confirm that your policy is in force.* Below, a little grid in typewriter font:

> INSURED: SOWRI, SUSMITHAA ............ **11**
> BENEFICIARY: SOWRI, ARENYA (NIECE) .... **12**

No premium. No face value. One line at the bottom: *A representative will contact the beneficiary in person to verify.* And across the whole page, in Aunty's blue ballpoint, pressed so hard the paper had torn at the O: **NO.**

The second was two pages of legal paper with a raised notary's seal she could feel with her thumb. *Declaration of Susmithaa Sowri.* Plain, almost dull. She had purchased no policy from Meridian Mutual Life or any affiliate. She named no beneficiary. Her niece was party to no contract made in her name. Signed by her, witnessed by *RAVI SOWRI* in Appa's careful engineer's capitals, stamped *Colma, California.*

Appa looked at his own signature as if someone else had forged it. "She told me not to read it," he whispered.

She laid the first two flat on the coffee table, side by side, the way she'd lay out sources. Precise was the only thing she had that didn't shake.

"Okay. March ninth. *Welcome to the family.* That's ten days before Hale says she walked into Colma with cash. So his story's backwards. Nobody bought anything. They sent her a receipt for something she never ordered." Her finger slid to the grid. "And they numbered us. Eleven. Twelve. In a typewriter font, like a utility bill." She touched the torn O, very lightly, as if it might still be hot. "She went through the paper."

"Printed in March," Kino said quietly, from behind the couch. "Before anyone died. They had the numbers written down long before they said them out loud. And she tore the paper saying no. That's not a woman who got sold something, Mr. Sowri. That's a woman who got *counted* and said no to its face."

"And then she went to a notary," she said, "and swore under seal she never bought it. With you as the witness, Appa, because you're the one person on earth who'd sign where she pointed and not read." Her voice broke for exactly one word. "That's not you being bad at lying. That's her trusting you to be good at not knowing."

She wrote it, small: *3/9 Meridian letter, unsolicited. 3/19 Colma notary, a declaration, not a purchase. She didn't buy a door. She was trying to shut one.*

The third paper was folded small, torn from the cheap spiral pad that had lived by Aunty's kettle for as long as there'd been a kettle. The ragged edge was still on. The round handwriting showed backward through the page, crowding to the margins, and at the bottom, where a name should have been, the ink went heavy. Even folded, even reversed, she could make out the shape. A little circle. A crown of quick rays.

A sun. It was how Aunty had signed every birthday card she'd ever gotten, since before she could read. They were in a drawer in her apartment right now, every one. She had never once asked why a sun.

*Before you read, love,* Abernathy said, quick and quiet. *Keep your face still. That sun's not ink. It's lit, the colour of the lamp, soaking through the folds. Same mark that came up under the twelve. Not a copy. Your aunt didn't just sign with it, she* sent *it.*

*I don't think it's a trap. The cold in your hand aches when you touch it, but your light doesn't flinch. It leans in.*

*And I'll tell you something true, since I said I'd never lie to you about danger. Looking at it, I smelled hot brass and lamp-smoke, and I heard a voice say,* Mark it so it can be found again. *Low. Patient. Mine, and not mine. I don't know who it was talking to. Don't ask me yet, guv. I've got a headache I haven't got a head for.*

*Read it. Slow. I'm here.*

She put her numbered hand flat on the back of Amma's, so that they were a chain: her, Amma, Appa. Kino at their back. "Kino," she said, rough. "Come where you can see. She says Itzel knows the rest. I think you get to hear this too."

He came around the couch and knelt on the carpet beside Amma, his shoulder against her knee, Ix settling across his feet. He kept his eyes half-lidded on the freezer seam in the dark glass of the window. He didn't lean in to read. "It's hers," he said. "I'll hear the room while you do."

She unfolded the page with her left hand and read it aloud from the top, in Aunty's voice as best she could, the brisk one with a little lift at the end of every sentence, as if each line were a question already answered. It was the way Aunty used to read her the newspaper when she was seven and pretending she could already.

*Ari kanna,*

*If Ravi has given you this, then they've called and I'm not there to pick up. I'm sorry. I wanted to be the one who picked up.*

*In March they sent me the letter you're holding. I never bought anything. That is how they say* we have found you. *Men who count don't knock. They send paperwork.*

*Listen to me.* **Writing is not keeping.** *They wrote me down in March and it took them seven months to come with their net, and when they came I had already decided what they could have, which was nothing important. If your palm has been written on, don't scrub it and don't hide it. Just don't go and* sign. *Don't verify. Don't say your full name to them in a room they chose. A thing gets kept when you agree to it.*

*The paper Ravi signed is my no. Keep it near the lamp. Itzel knows what a no is worth to the one under Colma. Make her tell you all of it this time; she leaves out the parts that frighten her.*

*If Itzel's boy is there, the cold one, then the lamp has already decided something, and you can stop arguing with it. Feed him. He doesn't eat when he's worried.*

*Tell your Amma I'm sorry about the will. Tell your Appa he lied very well for once and I'm proud of him.*

*Not too much ghee in the lamp. It smokes.*

And then the sun.

Appa broke first. It was quiet. His shoulders went, and he put his big hand over his mouth and kept it there. Amma turned her hand over under Arenya's palm and gripped hard, numbered side and all, and said to the carpet in Tamil, "*Stubborn. Stubborn, stubborn woman,*" in the voice people use for *I love you* when they can't manage the words.

In her palm the warmth stopped pushing against the cold twelve. It *settled*, the way someone sits down next to you on a bench and makes it clear she isn't getting up. The number was still there. It just wasn't alone with her anymore. The lamp burned lower and steadier, and its flame tipped once toward the back of the couch, toward Kino, like a nod.

A floorboard creaked in the hall.

Nana Itzel stood at the edge of the lamplight with her shoes still on and her braid undone, one hand on the wall. Nobody knew how long she'd been there. Her eyes were wet and very hard.

"*Ay, Susmita,*" she said softly. "Telling on me from the grave." She looked at the notary seal, at Arenya's palm, at her grandson. "Bueno. The parts that frighten me." She pulled Aunty's reading chair toward the lamp and sat and folded her hands. "Somebody put the kettle on. It isn't a short story, and I'm not telling it twice."

Arenya folded the kettle-pad sheet along its soft creases and tucked it into the back of her notebook, against the spine, where she kept the things she couldn't afford to lose. She didn't write anything down. For once.

"*Don't sign. Don't verify. Don't say your full name in a room they chose,*" she said. "Noon at Serramonte is a room they chose. Strung racks. A ring of chairs, one still warm. So I'm not going. Not as Arenya Sowri, anyway."

She filled the kettle herself, because Aunty had said *feed him* and because her hands needed a job. The freezer seam was pale with rime six feet away. She didn't look at it. She looked at Nana.

"She said you leave out the parts that frighten you. So start with those. Who's the one under Colma? Why does a *no* matter to it? Why does it keep books?"

She put the first mug into Kino's hands before he could refuse. "Drink that. Aunty's orders."

He drank. Hot enough to hurt; he looked glad of it. Then he said something to his grandmother in Spanish, low, and Arenya caught *todo* and *miedo* and understood the rest from his face.

Nana took the second mug, didn't drink from it, and held it in her lap like a candle.

"In nineteen-twelve," she said, "San Francisco decided the dead were bad for property values. So they dug them up. A hundred and fifty thousand, maybe more, and carted them south over the hill to Colma on wagons and the streetcar at night. My grandmother's people cut stone down there. They said you can't move that many dead without someone *counting*. Every box written down: who, from where, to which plot. Clerks did it for years, in ledgers, by lamplight, in a shed by the stone yard that always smelled of wet granite and ink." She looked at Kino. "Ix smelled the shed."

"The clerks died. The counting didn't stop. The stonecutters called it *el Contador*. Not a devil. Not a saint. A clerk. It writes down who is dead in that valley and who is going to be. Very careful, very fair, and in a hundred years it has never made a mistake in its sums. Nobody prays to it. You just don't lie on paper near Colma." She took a breath. "And somebody has learned to *read its books*. They wear grey and they say 'beneficiary.' Whatever it writes, they come and collect."

Her hands tightened on the mug.

"Here is the part that frightens me. In March, Susmita came to my kitchen with that welcome letter and she was *shaking*. I had never seen her shake. We went down together. She did the notary, all very correct, and then we walked to the stone yard after closing and she put a copy of her *no* under the shed door, with salt and a candle, the old way. I stood witness." Her mouth worked. "Something answered. Not a voice. The candle went out, and the salt on top of the paper was arranged, after, into a line beneath it. Like a sum."

"*A no buys time, not freedom.* My grandmother used to say that, and I didn't say it to Susmita, because she had already decided. It bought her seven months. I think she spent every day of them getting ready to give you the light instead of giving it to them."

Her eyes went to Arenya's open palm, then to her grandson's cold forearm, and stayed.

"And there was one more thing in the salt. A second mark, beside the line. Two small strokes, close together, side by side. I told myself it was the wind. Then tonight the lamp split in two." She looked at both of them. "I think the next *no* takes two people to say it. Don't ask me how. That's the end of what I know, *mijo*. I swear it on her lamp."

Arenya wrote it down, because a story like that was a list of facts, and facts were what she had. *1912. 150,000 moved. Ledgers. Shed: wet granite + ink. El Contador. Doesn't err. Somebody reads its books. March: salt, candle, her NO under the shed door. Answered with a line. Second mark: two strokes, side by side.*

"A clerk," she said. "Not a monster. A clerk that's very fair." The pen stopped. "That's worse, you know. Monsters you can fight. Clerks you have to *dispute*."

"Then twelve isn't a threat. It's a line item. And the men in grey aren't deciding anything. They're just the ones who learned to read the ledger." She looked at the torn **NO**. "Which means what Aunty wrote wasn't magic. It was a formal objection. And it got entered. The salt proves it." She turned. "Nana, the two strokes. Did they look like a tally? Two ones? Or two people standing next to each other?"

Then, gentler, to Kino, eyes still on his grandmother: "And whatever the next no takes, nobody decides that tonight. I'm not signing you up for anything because a pile of salt looked like a pair." She breathed. "But I'm not saying it alone, either. If it comes to that, I'd want it to be you."

She let it sit. She didn't take it back.

Kino set the mug down on the carpet. For a moment he said nothing at all, and she understood, a beat too late, that she had said something he had waited a very long time to hear, and that he'd probably imagined it coming from a mountain.

"Okay," he said. It was rough, and he left it rough. "Not tonight, like you said. But I heard you, and I'm not going to pretend I didn't." He looked at her straight, like a man across a trail junction. "Two strokes side by side. If it's two people, then the lamp wasn't picking between us. It was *pairing* us. And I spent three days thinking it was a verdict."

Nana got up slowly and took the pen out of Arenya's hand without asking. Under the left-handed notes she drew a long horizontal line, and beside it two short upright strokes, almost touching.

"Not ones. My grandmother had a ledger page from the shed, in the old clerks' hand. When a body came over the hill and somebody *disputed* the name—wrong family, wrong plot—the clerk didn't cross it out. They don't cross out. He drew two strokes in the margin. *Dos testigos.* Two witnesses. It means: *this entry is contested, and it stays open until two stand for it.*" She tapped them. "So. People. Standing next to each other."

She handed the pen back.

"Does it know your no is coming, *mijo*? It doesn't. It isn't God. It *left room*. A clerk leaves a space on the line when he's waiting for a signature. That's all those two strokes are. A space." Her mouth went thin. "And the grey ones can't unwrite a no. Nobody can. But they can come early. They lean on the one who said it, and lean, and lean, until that person walks into a room they chose and says their whole name just to make it stop. That's how they make it expensive. Susmita knew. That's why she never answered the phone."

On the table, Susmithaa's phone lay dark.

Arenya copied the two strokes into her notebook under Nana's line, exactly, and boxed them. *Dos testigos. Contested. Stays open until two stand.* Under it: *They can't unwrite a no. They can only make you say your name to stop the phone ringing.*

"That's what Hale is," she said. "Not a hunter. A pressure campaign with a nice voice. Fourteen missed calls and a polite threat. He wants me to walk into a room and say it all on my own." She looked at the dark phone. "So we don't answer. Not him, not the landline. Appa, unplug it before breakfast?"

Appa nodded, grateful for a job. Then he cleared his throat, eyes still red, and said, a little too loud, practising being brave: "Then it's settled. Nobody verifies anything. Everybody eats breakfast." Amma didn't laugh, but her shoulders came down a quarter inch.

In her palm, beside the cold twelve, there was a faint new pressure, like the place where a second pen would rest. She let it be.

"Breakfast, then Colma, in daylight, with a plan," she said. "Kino's rule: nobody signs. And the shed, if it's still there, is *our* room to choose. Not theirs."

"I walk the stone yard first," Kino said. "With Ix, in daylight, and you at the gate. A witness doesn't have to be the first one through the door. She has to be the one still standing at the end."

She leaned her head back against the couch. The window had gone slate, not dawn yet, but the idea of it.

*Abernathy,* she said into the bond, where only one person could hear. *The voice you heard. "Mark it so it can be found again." I'm not going to push. But I'd like to know whose it was before somebody else tells me. And thank you for the warning about the sun. It helped to know first.*

She closed her eyes for exactly one minute, palm open, shoulder against Kino's, between her parents. It was the first time in two days she'd let herself.

On the bond, the answer came after a long moment.

*I went looking, love. Honest as I can be. Nothing. Not fog, fog I'd know, fog's got something behind it. It's a clean wall. Smooth as a page nobody wrote on. The smell's gone. The voice's gone. All I've got is that I* heard *it, tonight, and now I can't.*

*Whose it was, I don't know. I won't make one up for you.*

A pause, and the cockney went thin as paper. *Doesn't feel like forgetting, though. Feels like somebody tidied up after me. I don't care for that.*

*Now shut your eyes, your Ladyship. Your minute's nearly spent. I'll keep count of the rest, and I won't let it be anyone's number but mine.*

Across the room the little sun had sunk back under her skin. Her palm was only a palm, with a cold twelve in it and a warm place beside it.

The minute passed, and then a few more that nobody counted.

Appa got down on his knees behind the side table and pulled the landline cord out of the wall, gently, like taking a splinter from a child. Amma fetched the good blanket and put it over both of them on the couch without asking whose knees went where. Nana Itzel stayed in Susmithaa's reading chair with her shoes on and didn't sleep, watching the lamp the way her grandmother had watched the stone yard. Kino kept the door. Ix's breathing slowed against his feet.

Outside, the slate went slowly to pewter over the hill. Somewhere south, in a valley with more dead than living, a shed that smelled of wet granite and ink waited with a line left open and two small strokes in the margin.

The frost on the freezer seal held. So did the lamp.

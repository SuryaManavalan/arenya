Seton's emergency entrance hummed like a refrigerator. The automatic doors opened half a second too slowly, as if they were thinking it over.

Past triage, the family room was a box of beige chairs and a dying ficus. A wall-mounted TV ran a muted infomercial for a knife that could cut through a shoe. Amma sat under the fluorescent lights in her nightdress and Appa's PG&E fleece, her hair half out of its plait, a paper cup of water sweating untouched in her hands. Appa stood at the window with his back to the room. He hadn't sat down, and Arenya understood from the set of his shoulders that he wasn't going to.

When Amma saw her she made a sound that wasn't a word and held out one arm. Only one. The other hand wouldn't let go of the cup.

On the chair beside her, wrapped in a hospital blanket the way you'd wrap a child, was Susmithaa Aunty's brass lamp. A crack ran through the glass chimney, fine as a hair.

"They let me keep it," Amma said into Arenya's shoulder. "I wouldn't give it to them. Is that stupid? I wouldn't give it to them."

"It's not stupid." Arenya's throat closed on the second word, and she waited it out. "You keep it. Nobody's taking it."

Past her mother's shoulder, through the open door, she could see the nurses' station. A man in a dark wool coat leaned on the counter. He was tall and very still and much too awake for four in the morning. He held a lanyard ID face-down under two fingers, the way you'd hold a card you didn't want read. The night nurse was shaking her head at him. Arenya couldn't hear a word, but she watched the nurse's eyes slide down the hall toward this room and then away. It was the glance of someone who had just been asked a name.

Amma pulled back and looked at her properly for the first time. "Kanna. Your wrist. Why are you holding your wrist?"

She hadn't known she was. The cold spot on the inside of her right wrist was the size of a coin and hadn't warmed since she woke. Her left hand had been clamped over it the entire drive.

"Steering wheel," Arenya said. It came out too fast, so she said it again, slower, the way you fix a typo. "I gripped it wrong the whole way. Pinched a nerve, probably. It's nothing, Amma."

She made herself let go. The cold stayed anyway, a coin pressed into the skin. She tucked the hand into the crook of her mother's elbow, inside the fleece, where nobody could see it. The right hand stayed shut. She hugged Amma with her forearms and her chin, like someone carrying groceries, and Amma either didn't notice or didn't care.

*Don't look, love.*

The voice arrived close against her ear, forced cockney with the edges worn thin. Abernathy stood at her shoulder, shabby coat not quite moving with the air-conditioning, flickering at the margins.

*Don't look at the nurses' station. Look at your mum. That bloke in the coat, guv. He's got no light on him at all. Not hidden, not shuttered. Nothing. Like a hole in a fence you could put your hand through.* A pause, and something older crept under the next words before he caught it. *I've never seen a living man like that. And I don't like how it itches that I might have.*

She did not look. She looked at her mother's hair.

*Left hand off the wrist, but easy, like you're rubbing it cause it's cold. Say it's the air-con. If he's asking after a niece he'll be down that corridor in a minute, and you want to be a girl crying on her mum, not a girl with a mark.* The flicker of him moved to the chair, to the blanketed shape on it. *And that lamp. It's not out, Arenya. It's listening. Keep it close. And keep your fist shut, yeah? Whatever you do with it. Please.*

It was the *please* that landed. Abernathy didn't say please.

"Amma." She said it into the hair, barely moving her lips. "Is that the man? The one who asked about the niece?"

Amma went stiff in her arms, which was an answer. "He was here when they brought me in. Very polite. He said *niece*, kanna, not *daughter*. Like he knew which word to use." Her fingers found Arenya's sleeve. "Appa told him we have no niece. Appa is a bad liar."

At the window, Appa's shoulders rose and fell once. He didn't turn around.

Arenya got the man in pieces, the way you read a menu over someone's shoulder. Early forties. Clean-shaven, but only since yesterday; the jaw was paler than the cheeks. Black oxfords with a white line of winter salt along the welt, which meant not from here. His hands were the strange part. They lay perfectly still on the counter, no tapping, no phone, the hands of a man who had been taught to keep them where people could see. His coat hung heavier on the left side.

The nurse said something short and final. He nodded as though she'd given him exactly what he came for, tucked the lanyard into his breast pocket without once turning it over, and straightened.

Then he looked down the hall. Not at the ficus. Not at the TV. At the open door of the family room, at the lamp in its blanket on the beige chair, and at her.

He started walking. He was in no hurry. On the TV, the knife went through another shoe.

---

He stopped two paces inside the doorway, which was as far as courtesy allowed, and kept his hands where the family could see them. He looked at the lamp a moment longer than he looked at anyone. Then at Appa's back. Then, last, at Arenya.

"Mrs. Sowri. Mr. Sowri. I'm sorry for your loss, and I'm sorry about the hour." The voice was low and even. "My name is Shawani. Department of Energy." He lifted the lanyard out of his pocket and showed the card for exactly as long as it took to read, and put it away. "I'm not here to add to anything tonight. I asked the nurse about your sister-in-law because her name came up in a review I'm part of. I should have waited until morning. That was a mistake."

His eyes came back to Arenya. They were too clear. They rested on her clamped left hand and the wrist hidden under her mother's elbow, and they did not linger, and he said nothing about it.

"Ms. Sowri. We've met, in a way. I won't ask you anything in front of your mother. I'll be in the corridor by the vending machines for as long as you need." A beat. "When you're ready, there are two or three things I think you'd rather hear from me than from the people who come after me."

Arenya didn't let go of her mother. She turned only her head, and her voice came out low and flat, the one she kept for strangers who called her *hon*.

"You were in my apartment on Friday. You told me to stop. Now you're at my aunt's deathbed with a lanyard." She glanced at Amma, at the lamp, back to him. "Say 'review' one more time and I'll ask that nurse what kind of Department of Energy works the ER at four a.m."

Her right fist stayed shut against Amma's back. The coin on her wrist ached.

"Not in front of them. Not in the corridor either, where you can watch the door. Give me ten minutes with my mother. If you're still here after, you can buy me a terrible coffee and tell me who 'the people who come after you' are. Names would be nice. I take notes." She tightened her arm. "Stay where I can see you, Mr. Shawani. And don't come any closer to that lamp."

"Ten minutes." He didn't argue the terms. He took one pace back, out of the doorway, and stayed in her line of sight. "The coffee here is worse than you're imagining. I'll buy it anyway." His gaze touched the lamp once more and moved off, as if he had noticed it and decided not to say so. His voice dropped for her alone. "Names, then. I'll give you one tonight, and you can decide what it's worth. And you're right about the nurse. Ask her. She'll tell you I showed her a card, and that's all I gave her."

He turned to Appa's back and inclined his head, the small bow of a man leaving a house. "Mr. Sowri. I'm sorry."

Then he was in the corridor by the vending machines, hands visible, facing the family-room door and not the exit. The hum of the building covered whatever else he was thinking.

---

Arenya watched until the vending machines swallowed his outline. Then she did the thing she'd actually been asked to do. She sat Amma down in the beige chair beside the lamp, crouched in front of her, and took the paper cup out of her hands before it crushed. Left hand. The right stayed closed.

"Amma. Drink some of this, or I'll make you eat the ficus." It was a bad joke, and Amma almost laughed, which was the point. "Tell me what Aunty said last. Anything. At dinner, on the phone. Don't edit it, even the small stuff."

She pulled the coffee-stained notebook out of her bag one-handed and didn't open it. It sat on her knee, a prop and a promise. "Appa," she said, softly, to the window. "Sit with us. Please. Ten minutes."

Out of the corner of her eye: the corridor, the coat, the hands in plain view. She was counting. Ten minutes was not a figure of speech.

Amma drank half the water in small swallows, like a child taking medicine. The almost-laugh came back as a hiccup.

"Dinner was Sunday. She made too much rasam, as always." Amma's eyes went to the blanketed lamp. "She was happy. Silly-happy. She said her Thursday friend was teaching her to make tamales and she was very bad at it. The grandmother in Daly City. Itzel." She sounded the name out carefully, the way Aunty must have. "She said, *If I die first, Itzel will come with pozole, and you must eat it and not be rude about the chili.* I told her not to say such things at the table." Her mouth worked. "That was the last thing. *Don't be rude about the chili.*"

Behind them a chair scraped.

Appa had turned from the window. His face was grey and dry. He sat two chairs away, not beside anyone, big hands loose between his knees, and spoke to the floor.

"In March," he said. "She called me at work. She never called me at work. She said, *Ravi, if an American ever comes asking about my niece, you tell him there is no niece.* I asked her, which niece? She has only one." He lifted his eyes to Arenya. "She said, *Exactly.* And she hung up." A breath. "I thought she was being dramatic. Tonight I told him there is no niece." His voice cracked on the last word, quietly, the way the chimney had. "I am a very bad liar, kanna."

Arenya didn't write it down, because Amma would see. She said it back to herself instead, twice, the way she memorized quotes. *If an American ever comes asking about my niece. Exactly.* March. Itzel. Pozole. *Don't be rude about the chili.*

She moved to the chair beside him, close enough that her shoulder pressed his arm, and took his hand in her left. "You're not a bad liar, Appa. You're a bad liar because you love somebody. It's the only kind of bad I'd want." She squeezed once. "You told him the right thing. Keep telling him no. Anyone asks, they ask me first, and I'll tell you what to say."

His hand closed on hers, too hard, the way a man grips a railing on a boat, and then he remembered himself and eased. He nodded at the floor, twice.

"Amma. Do you know where Itzel lives? I think Aunty would want her called." That was true. It was also a thread, and Arenya knew it, and hated a little how fast she'd reached for it.

"Address, no." Amma patted her purse, where a plastic hospital bag had been stuffed. "They gave me her things. Her phone is in here. The nurse said it kept buzzing at three o'clock and nobody answered." She didn't open it. "I couldn't look. You look, later." Then, with the certainty of a woman reciting scripture: "But she said Itzel will come. So she will come. We will be at the house. We will have to clean the house." The idea, a job, straightened her spine an inch.

Arenya's knee had come to rest against the lamp's blanketed base, and she hadn't moved it. The brass was warm. Not body-warm, exactly; warm the way a chair is when someone has been sitting in it a long time and just stood up. For a second, less, she would have sworn the warmth shifted. It didn't spread. It gathered, the way a cat resettles against you, toward the side of the lamp that faced her closed right fist.

Then the quiet stretched out, a family in a row of beige chairs, and it was good. It was the first good thing all night.

Her phone said nine minutes.

In the corridor, Mr. Shawani had bought two coffees. He held one in each hand, visibly, and hadn't taken a sip of either. He wasn't looking at his watch. He was looking at the door, patient as furniture.

Arenya let the last minute sit, because it was the only gift she had left to give. Then she stood, kissed her mother's hair, and took the plastic bag out of the purse with two fingers of her left hand. She didn't open it. She tucked it into her own bag, under the notebook. "I'll keep Aunty's phone safe. Nobody touches it but me."

"Ten minutes. I'm going to go be rude to a man about coffee. Appa, you stay with Amma. The lamp doesn't leave this room. If anybody who isn't family walks in, you call my name, loud. Loud, Appa. Be a bad liar at the top of your lungs."

She waited for his nod. Then she laid her left palm on the blanket over the lamp for a second, not sure why, and walked out.

---

She stopped at arm's length and took the nearer coffee without thanking him. She didn't drink it.

"Names," she said. "You promised one. Start with who sent you to my aunt."

"Nobody sent me to your aunt." Malachi said it quietly, back against the wall, and didn't touch the second cup. "Her name was on a list I keep. People who travel to odd places and keep odd company. I put it there in September. I never knocked on her door. That's mine to carry, and I'll carry it."

His eyes stayed on the family-room door, not on her.

"The name is Quinn. Adonis Quinn, who died at Tartine. He worked for Monolith, and Monolith's founder is his cousin. I don't know that they're the ones doing this. I know your wall has a man in a grey jacket on it, and I know my people have three more like him. I also know somebody reached this hospital before your family did, and it wasn't me. I got here second."

A pause. Then, almost reluctantly: "My card says Energy. It's a courtesy, and I'd rather not say what it's a courtesy for. Not here, not yet. You'll have to take that as the price of my having told you the rest."

He looked at her then, at the left hand clamped over the wrist.

"Now one for one. What happened to your arm, Ms. Sowri? Don't say the steering wheel. I watched you drive in."

"Wow. You watched me drive in." Her mouth did something that wasn't a smile. "That's either surveillance or a crush, and I'm not sure which is worse."

She kept the coffee in her right hand, so the fist stayed closed around paper, and took the left off her wrist for the first time since the car. On the inside of the wrist was a pale circle the size of a coin, a little greyer than the skin around it. No bruise. No mark from a nail.

"I woke up with it at 3:19. It's cold. It's been cold for an hour, and I'm told it isn't frostbite." She turned the wrist so he could see, but didn't hold it out. "That's your one. Here's my price. Adonis Quinn I already had. I want what's *not* on my wall. Who's 'your people'? And the polite American in the suit: do you know his face? Or did you only see the nurse's face when she looked at me?"

Malachi looked at the wrist for exactly as long as it took to memorize it, and he didn't lean in. His face gave nothing away, but he'd stopped holding his coffee like a prop.

"I haven't seen that before," he said. "I'd tell you if I had. I'm not going to touch it, and I'd ask you not to let a doctor photograph it. Or anyone with a clipboard." A beat. "It's a bad habit of mine. If I don't understand a mark, I assume somebody else does."

He answered in the order she'd asked.

"My people are three analysts and a phone. That's all. A cell, not a department, and nobody upstairs would sign for it if I fell off the earth tonight. As for the man in the suit, no. I never saw his face. The night nurse said a man asked for your aunt by her full name at about twenty to four, before the ambulance paperwork was even filed. Polite. Signed nothing. He knew to say *niece*."

His gaze dropped to the cup crushed slightly in her right fist.

"You're holding that very carefully. I won't ask. But whoever's counting the people your aunt left behind, I'd rather it was me who noticed first. Is there anywhere you can be, the next few days, where you're not alone and not at your apartment?"

She looked at the cup, saw the white of her knuckles, and loosened exactly one finger so the paper wouldn't crumple. A small concession. She hated that he'd seen the need.

"Three analysts and a phone. So you're me with a lanyard. Great. Bronze medal." She set the coffee on top of the vending machine and put the left hand back over the wrist. "I'll be at my parents' house all week. It's Tamil, so there'll be forty aunties and a rice cooker the size of a car. Nobody gets near me without being interrogated about their marital status. You will not be coming to that house. If my father sees you again he'll tell you there is no niece, and he'll cry, and I'll never forgive you. You can reach me by phone."

She tore a corner from a clean page of the notebook and wrote the number of her burner. She held it out and didn't let go when he reached.

"One more. 'Nine names on a list.' Is Susmithaa Sowri the first of them to die, or the first you *noticed* die? Think before you answer. I'll know the difference by your hands."

His hands stayed where they were, open and still, and he let her read them as long as she liked.

"I've mixed two numbers, and that was careless of me," he said. "Nine are the dead. Freeze deaths, confirmed by my team. None of those nine were on any list of mine beforehand, and I think that's the whole problem. The other list is people my analysts call unusual. Your aunt was on it. She's the first name on that list to die." A pause. "I don't know if she's the first to be killed. Only the first I noticed."

His eyes went to the family-room door and back.

"And she didn't freeze. The nurse says she was found sitting by a tree in her cardigan. No fixed stare, no seizure, nothing like the others. Whoever did this either changed method or did something I haven't seen. I don't like either answer."

He took the paper by the edge she was offering, gently, and waited until she let go. He folded it once and slid it into an inner pocket without looking at it.

"I won't come to the house. I'll call once, tomorrow, and I'll say only 'it's me' and a time. If you don't answer, I won't call again. Ms. Sowri, I'm sorry about your aunt. I mean that more than I'd like to."

She watched his hands the whole time. They didn't move. She hated that it was the first thing about him she believed.

"Nine dead. A list of 'unusual.' And my aunt, who kept a lamp in a window for thirty-one years, lands on the wrong list and dies the wrong way." She said it flatly, a headline she was trying to make fit. It didn't. "She didn't freeze. Write that down, Mr. Shawani. If I find out that's the reason you stopped looking at the freezes, I'm going to be very unpleasant."

She shifted the bag on her shoulder, where Aunty's phone sat beneath the notebook.

"Tomorrow. 'It's me' and a time. If I don't answer, it's because I'm burying somebody, not because I'm done." Quieter: "Thank you for saying sorry. I didn't think a man with a lanyard knew how."

She didn't offer her hand. She turned back toward the family room, left hand over her wrist, right fist closed. In the doorway she stopped, where she could see Amma and Appa and the lamp in its blanket, and said over her shoulder without turning: "Don't follow me in. And if you see a polite American, don't be polite."

---

He didn't follow. When she glanced back from the beige chair a minute later, the corridor held two coffees on top of a vending machine and nobody else. She hadn't heard him go.

Amma had fallen asleep sitting up, her head on Appa's shoulder. Appa was awake and looking at nothing, his big hand over hers. Arenya sat on the other side of the lamp and put her left palm back on the blanket.

Warm. Still warm.

At 5:51 the window behind Appa went from black to the colour of weak tea. Somewhere in her bag, under the notebook, Aunty's phone buzzed once, a single short pulse like a knock, and went quiet.

She didn't take it out. Not yet. But her right fist, for the first time since the garden, uncurled a quarter-inch on its own. It loosened the way a hand does when it has decided it is safe enough to sleep.

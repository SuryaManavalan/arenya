Colma, by daylight, was a town of fifteen hundred living people and a million and a half dead, and it had arranged itself accordingly. Cemetery gates alternated with florists. Every block had a place to buy a bucket of roses and a place to put them down.

Nana drove. The 1998 Corolla smelled of marigolds and WD-40, and the three papers rode in a Safeway bag on the dash, under the lamp's spare wick, like a family that had agreed not to discuss something. Breakfast had been idli from the one shelf of the freezer the frost hadn't touched, plus Nana's leftover pozole, reheated. Nobody had said it was a strange combination. Amma had watched Kino push his bowl around, and then she'd watched me watch him, and then she'd gone to find her purse.

Fog was burning off Mission Road in long white strips. **ESPOSO MONUMENT CO. — SINCE 1961** was a chain-link lot of uncut granite blanks standing in rows, a waiting room for people who hadn't arrived yet. Somewhere a radio played Tagalog talk. A polishing wheel whined in the open-front shop. At the very back, past the last row, stood a corrugated-tin shed that hadn't been painted in Nana's lifetime, its door turned away from the street as if it were embarrassed to be seen.

An older man in a dust-white apron came to the gate wiping his hands, safety glasses pushed up on his forehead. "You here for a stone? Mama. Nice to see you again." He nodded at Nana, followed her eyes to the back of the lot, and his face did a small, practiced nothing. "Ah. That. City says it's on our lot. We don't go in. You go, you go. Don't touch my blanks."

"Gracias," Kino said. "We won't touch anything."

He meant it for the whole yard. I watched him mean it.

He stepped through first, as agreed, and stopped two paces inside. His weight settled. His breath went slow. His thumb found the birthmark through his sleeve and pressed it, the way you'd press a bruise to check it was still real, and under his breath he said the old words Nana had taught him for entering a place that wasn't his. *Con permiso. Vengo a ver, no a tomar.* With permission. I've come to see, not to take.

Then he called Ix up out of the dark behind his ribs, and not in front of him, either. At heel. Low, shoulder against his knee.

I stayed at the gate, Nana's hand on my elbow, and did not step past the post.

My palm was the problem. The 12 on it didn't hurt. It *itched*, the way a word you can't remember itches, the word right there behind your teeth, wearing a hat. I closed my fingers over it. Then I made myself open them again. Not hiding it. Just watching.

"Nana," I said, low, "if the line stops at the gate for me the way it did for Ix, tell me. I don't want to be the reason it opens."

"It will stop for you, mija." She didn't take her eyes off the boy. "It stops for everyone at the gate. The gate is where they *ask*." Her thumb moved once on my elbow. "If it opens, I'll pull you back by the hair. I've done it before. Ask your tía."

I took out the notebook and wrote the time, because facts first. *10:14. Grey line, knee height, every stone. Apron man, practiced nothing.* When the pen lifted I saw that the faint printed rules of my cheap notebook had drifted. They ran level with the line on the granite, perfectly parallel, as if the page had decided to match the yard.

Nana saw it too. "Facts are fine," she murmured. "Facts it already has. Just don't sign the bottom."

*Puzzled,* I'd told Ix on the bond. *I'll take puzzled. Puzzled is a clerk who can't find your line, which is how I'd like to stay.* He'd answered with the thing I'd asked for, out loud, right away: the brass smell, half a second, hot and gone, the same as last night. *Saying it so you've got it on the record, love.* And the line that had been ruled by his feet at the gate and left blank, a clerk who dipped the pen, looked at him, and set it down. *Not yet.* He sounded almost pleased with himself, and then he didn't. *A clerk that's puzzled by Ix is a clerk that'll be less puzzled by you. Keep your name behind your teeth, Ladyship.*

I kept it there. It was getting crowded back there.

---

Every blank in every row carried a faint pencil-grey line at exactly the same height, level from stone to stone, all the way back to the shed. The yard was ruled like a page. Kino walked it the way I'd watched him talk about walking Tam at night: on the gravel, never where the shadow of the line would fall across his path, hands in his hoodie pocket, eyes at the height of the line. Halfway down he crouched to sight along it toward the shed door, the way you'd sight a trail. Then he stayed there a while, and I understood he was reading it, and that whatever it said, it was saying it plainly.

"Twelve rows," he said, when he stood. His voice carried back down the gravel, low and flat, a report from a ridge. "The shed's the heading. Where the thirteenth would be. There's a file of narrow stones along the left fence, one at the head of each row. That's the margin." He swallowed. "Row eleven has a salt stroke in the margin stone. Old. Chisel. The line dips through it, like somebody argued there. That's Susmithaa. That's March."

I wrote it down. My hand was steady, which surprised me.

"Row twelve's clean. The margin stone's polished, the only finished face in the yard. One pencil stroke on it. Low. One, not two." He didn't say whose. "It's waiting for a second."

Under the 12 in my palm, just beneath the little rule, the itch settled into a single point, like a finger putting itself on a map.

"Kino." I kept my voice level, the voice you'd use on someone on a ledge. "Row twelve is one stroke, and my palm just put its finger on the empty spot for the second. I'm not saying that so you'll step on it. I'm saying it so you know that stone is *mine*, and nobody gets to be brave on it before I say so."

He looked at the clean stone and not at me. His hand went still in his pocket. "Somebody's been in and out of that shed lately," he said. "They were careful. The tin over the door's been wiped. There's a crust of salt under it and nobody's scuffed it." He tilted his head. "I'm going to circle wide and listen at the wall. Not the door. Ix stays at heel. If my hand goes up flat, you pull her back, Nana."

"If you hear a pen," I said, "or a voice counting, or anyone you don't know, you walk back. You don't answer it. And don't say my name. Not even to the dog." I tightened my grip on the notebook and kept my other palm closed. "Nana, if my hand starts doing anything on its own, pull. I'll forgive the hair."

Nana made a sound that, in someone less formidable, would have been a laugh.

He went. Gravel, then weeds, then the cracked concrete pad the shed stood on. Behind him the polishing wheel cut off mid-whine, for no reason the shop would ever give, and the Tagalog radio went with it. Colma at ten in the morning was suddenly the quietest place I'd ever stood, and I had once stood in a library during finals.

I couldn't hear what he heard. I could see him. I watched him lean toward the tin wall without touching it, so close his breath must have fogged it, and I watched the exact moment it reached him. His shoulders didn't jump. They *stopped*. The way a held pen stops.

My closed palm went warm. Under the cold of the 12, a little sun-heat gathered in the place beside it, where the second stroke would go. Nana's grip on my elbow tightened until I felt it in my back teeth.

I learned later what he'd heard: a real pen, dip and scratch, unhurried, the patience of something that had been doing this since 1912, and under it the voice from Hale's voicemail, dry as old paper, not speaking to anyone. Reading back. *"Once, entered. Once, contested. Sostenido por una."* A pause. *"Doce. Testigo: una. Falta una."*

And then the pen stopped completely, and the attention came through the tin like cold through a window and settled on the ache in his arm, and it asked him, without sound, in the pause of a long counter, with the clerk waiting and the pen held over the line:

*¿Y tú?*

From forty yards off I saw his face change.

---

He told me afterward what it cost. I'm putting it here because he never would have.

Twenty-six years of fasting and night-walks and dreaming on the mountain, all of it waiting for something old to wake and turn and ask for *him*. And here it was, through a tin wall in Colma, in a clerk's patient silence. The want came up in him so fast and so clean it shamed him. He could say *sí*. It would be the easiest word of his life.

I didn't know any of that then. I knew only that his jaw worked once. That he did not nod, and he did not shake his head, because either one was an answer, and Nana had said you don't lie near Colma. That slowly, so whatever was listening could follow it, he turned his head away from the shed and looked back down twelve rows of granite to the gate. To me, with my closed hand and Nana's fist on my elbow.

He held the look. It said *not mine to give.*

"Kino!" It came out sharper than I meant. "Don't answer it. Not a word, not a nod. Not yes and not no either. Tell it you came to see, not to take. Then walk back to me."

He was already walking back. One step, two, three, off the pad and into the weeds, never turning his back on the door, the way you leave a lion's ground. Ix moved with him, shoulder to knee. Only when his heels hit gravel did he turn and take the long way around by the fence. He passed row twelve without looking at the clean stone. He didn't trust himself to.

I made myself breathe out. "It's asking you because you're the one standing closest," I said, quieter, though he was too far to hear. "That isn't the same as you being the one it wants. We said not tonight. Say it with your feet."

He said it with his feet. Then, at the last row, he lifted two fingers: *coming back. Don't move.* His voice, when it finally arrived, was rough as the gravel.

"It's reading. *Doce, testigo una, falta una.* It asked me." A swallow. "I didn't answer. Get her off the gate, Nana. We talk in the car."

And I felt it go. All three of us did. Something followed his look down twelve rows, along the pencil line, past the blanks and the careful gravel, to the gate post and the girl standing at it with her hand shut. It rested on me for the length of a held pen. It did not feel hostile. It felt like being *read*, a line at a time, by someone with all the time in the world.

Inside the shed, the pen dipped once and made a small sound, too short to be a word. A tick.

The polishing wheel started up mid-whine, as if it had never stopped. The radio laughed at something.

Tito Boy Esposo stood aside to let Kino through the gate and didn't ask. He only said to Nana, with his eyes on the back of the lot, "Next time, Mama, call first. It's been busy this week." A pause, long enough to be an opinion. "Lot of visitors."

---

In the Corolla I finally opened my hand.

The 12 was there, cold. The rule was under it. Beside the rule, where the second stroke would go, there was no stroke. There was a dot of pencil-grey, small and exact, the kind a clerk leaves beside a line to say *come back to this.*

"Referred," Nana said, not looking. She'd seen it in the mirror. "It does not say yes. It says *later.* It is the most dangerous word they have."

"Great," I said. "I've been referred. Like a bad knee."

Kino sat with his hood up and both hands under his thighs and didn't say anything, and Ix was a weight in the car I could feel more than see. I wanted to tell him it was fine. It wasn't. I wanted to tell him I'd seen his face and knew what he'd given up in that second, and I couldn't find a sentence that wouldn't make it smaller. So I wrote it in the notebook instead, in the margin, where the printed rules still ran level with a stone I could no longer see. *K: didn't say yes.*

Nana pulled onto Mission Road. Across the street, in front of the florist with the bucket roses, a beige Camry was parked at the curb. Empty. The plate began **7K**. On the dash lay a parking-validation slip, and I could read the logo from where I sat: **Colma Notary & Mail**, two doors down.

*Oi,* said Abernathy in my ear, very quietly, with none of the accent. *Ladyship. That's a lot of visitors.*

"Nana," I said. "Slow down."

She was already slowing.

Daly City at twenty to seven was fog and wet stucco. The houses climbed the hill like teeth, and every porch light was still burning against a sky that hadn't decided to be day.

Nana's door was unlocked. It always was when she was expecting someone.

Kino toed off his boots on the newspaper she kept there for mud, side by side, the way he had since he was six. The kitchen smelled of rice and ginger and the burnt-sugar edge of a kettle she never let go cold. Two cups sat on the oilcloth. One was full and steaming. The other was upside down on its saucer, the way you set a place for someone who hasn't come yet.

Or someone who isn't coming back.

Nana sat with her back to the window and her hands around her own cup. She was smaller than he remembered. She always was. Her eyes went to his face, then to his left side, where the birthmark lay under the cotton, as if she could read it through the shirt.

"Sit," she said. "You walked down a mountain with something on your tongue all night. I can hear it rattling in your teeth." She nudged the full cup toward him with one finger. "Drink first. Then tell me. Then I'll tell you whose cup that is."

She did not look at the upside-down one. She was very carefully not looking at it.

The chair was the same chair. It creaked in the same place. He drank the way she had taught him, with both hands, all of it, not talking. It was too hot. He drank it anyway. He set the cup down and turned it once so the chip faced away from her, and kept his eyes off the overturned cup too. He knew she saw him doing it.

"Nana." He got that far and stopped. His thumb found the birthmark through his sleeve and pressed, checking it was still there. "It wasn't only tonight."

He made himself look at her.

"Since September. Every time I dream on her, she's lying the way she always lies, and then she rolls. Slow. Like somebody in bed turning toward a door they heard open. And I'm on her shoulder, and I slide off, and she doesn't notice." His voice stayed level, the levelness of a man holding something heavy out at arm's length. "Five, six weeks. I didn't tell you."

The kettle ticked on the stove.

"I told myself it was so you wouldn't worry. *Mentira.*" Quieter. "I didn't want you to look at me the way you're looking at me right now. Like you already knew." His jaw worked. "*¿Ya sabías, verdad?* You set two cups."

At last he let his eyes go to the overturned one, and kept them there.

"Okay. I told you. Now tell me whose cup."

Nana let him sit inside what he'd said. She had done the same on the mountain when he was small, leaving him an hour longer than he wanted. She refilled his cup without asking. Then she reached across and turned the chip back to face her, where it belonged.

"*Sí, ya sabía.*" No apology in it. "Not the dreams. You hid those good. But you stopped eating my tamales on Sundays and started eating them standing up, like a man who's late." She sniffed. "Five weeks. *Ay, mijo.* You think you're the only one in this family who can count?"

She laid her hand flat on the overturned cup, the way you'd rest a hand on a sleeping dog.

"Hers. Thursdays, eleven years. A Tamil lady from down the hill, the cottage behind the blue house on Hillcrest. Susmithaa. She took her tea with too much sugar, and she brought me this terrible sweet that tastes like a candle." The corner of her mouth moved. "She kept a lamp in her window the way I keep the kettle on, so you know what she was. And you smelled her this morning, *verdad?* Butter and church. That's ghee and camphor. That was her, going." Her thumb tapped the porcelain twice. "I woke at a quarter to three and turned this over. I didn't need anybody to call me."

Something in Kino's chest went very still. He had spent half the night on a mountain chasing that smell, and it had been a woman he'd passed on Mission Street a dozen times without knowing her name.

Nana leaned in. Her voice changed into the one she used for teaching.

"Now listen, because you have it backwards and it's been eating you. When you're asleep and you hear the door, who do you turn away from? Nobody. You turn *toward the door.* The Lady didn't roll off you, Joaquín. Since September she's been listening to something. Last night it opened a crack, and a light went through." She let that land. "And the light didn't go into the ground with Susmithaa. It went to her brother's girl. The niece. The one who doesn't believe in anything." A beat. "Susmithaa worried about that one more than she worried about dying."

She sat back and wrapped both hands around her cup. She was watching him the way he watched hikers.

"So. You told me your thing. That's mine. Now you tell me what a champion does when his Lady is looking at a door."

For a while he said nothing. He looked at the chip, now turned the right way.

"Twenty years." It came out flat. "Fasting on the ridge. Sitting in the fog till I couldn't feel my hands. You waking me at two to go ask the mountain if I could walk on her." He laughed once through his nose, with no humor in it. "And she opens the door for somebody who doesn't believe in *anything*."

He heard himself. His mouth tightened, and he pressed the heel of his hand against the birthmark, hard, as if he could push the cold out of it.

"*Perdón.* Your friend died. She drank coffee at your table for eleven years and I'm sitting here sulking over her cup." His voice dropped. "That's not what you raised."

He breathed the way she'd taught him: in for four, hold, out slow. When he looked up his eyes were wet, but his voice held.

"What a champion does." He turned the question over like a stone from a creek bed. "He doesn't stand in front of the door. He doesn't block it because he's jealous of what came through." A pause. "He stands *beside* it. He watches who comes in, and he makes sure nothing else follows. Not the thing that's been eating the old ones."

He swallowed.

"The niece. Does she know? About the light, any of it?" His hand came off the birthmark and lay flat on the oilcloth, near the overturned cup but not touching it. "Somebody's freezing hosts in this city, and the Lady just handed the brightest one there is to a girl who'll think it's a migraine." He shook his head. "She's going to get herself killed, Nana. Unless somebody shows her how to hold it."

Then, quieter, almost to himself:

"Even if it should've been me."

"Tea," Nana said. "She drank tea. Coffee made her talk too fast." It was the only correction she made. She left the rest alone, because he had already done that work.

She rose slowly, one hand on the table, and went to the stove. She kept her back to him. She always had for the hard parts, so he could cry without being watched.

"Does she know. No. Last night she knew her Amma was crying on the phone, and maybe she dreamed something she's already telling herself was cheese." She lit the burner under the big pot. "Susmithaa told her little things over the years. The girl laughed. Susmithaa laughed too, so the girl wouldn't feel bad." The spoon scraped. "That was her mistake. She was too kind to frighten anybody."

She turned and pointed the spoon at him.

"*Should've been you.* Listen to me. The Lady doesn't hand out lights, *mijo.* That one was never hers to give. It belongs to that family the way the dark belongs to ours. What she gave *you* is eyes that work at night. Who do you think a lamp needs?" She turned back to the pot. "Somebody who can see what's standing just outside the circle it throws."

He didn't answer. He didn't need to.

She chopped an onion for a minute. Then, in the voice of someone discussing errands:

"The family will be back from Seton by noon. After that the house fills with people and food for days, and somebody keeps a lamp burning. I'm taking *pozole* down the hill at two. Old women bring food; nobody asks an old woman why she came." She glanced at his ranger greens. "You'll carry the pot. Wear the black shirt, not that. And you don't say *champion*, you don't say *Lady*, you don't say *light*. You say *lo siento mucho*, you stand where I put you, and you *watch*." The knife stopped. "If anything came through that door behind her, it'll be in that house before the week is out. I'd rather it found you there first."

"Tea," Kino said, nodding, filing it the way he filed trail markers. "Too much sugar. Okay."

He watched her back, the knife, the onion coming apart. His shoulders let go a notch. He didn't say thank you. In this kitchen you didn't. You did the thing she asked.

"Black shirt. Pot. Mouth shut." He almost smiled. "That I can do. I've been practicing my whole life."

He got up, pushed in his chair, and crossed to the stove. He took the spoon out of her hand as if it were nothing, and she let him. His other hand settled over hers on the rim of the pot, calloused over knuckles she'd known since they were small.

"I'll stir. You sit." Then, lower: "And Nana, if something did follow her through, and it's already watching that house, it'll see a pot and an old woman's grandson. It won't see what I am." He glanced at the overturned cup. "Ix comes with me. Quiet. He'll stay out past the porch light, where the fog is."

He started stirring, slow and steady, eyes on the pot.

"What do I look for? Tell me what wrong looks like in a house full of people crying."

Nana sat. She did it the way she never did, right away, without arguing. It told him more about how tired she was than anything she'd said all morning.

"Crying is fine," she said. "Crying is people working. Look for the ones who aren't working." She held up a finger. "One. Somebody nobody can remember. Everybody thinks a cousin brought him, the cousin thinks the neighbor did, and when you look straight at him your eyes want to go somewhere else. That kind of forgettable doesn't happen by accident, *mijo.* It's made." A second finger. "Two. The lamp. They'll keep one burning for her. If the flame leans when somebody walks past, and there's no draft, you look at who walked past." A third. "Three. The girl. Watch who watches *her*. Not the aunties; aunties watch everybody. The ones who look at her the way you look at a deer by the road. Like they're working out how heavy she is."

She lowered her hand.

"And if Ix goes quiet out there, not lying-down quiet, *hiding* quiet, you take the pot and you bring me home. No heroics in a house of mourning."

Steam rose off the pozole and fogged the window over the sink. Beyond it, down the hill past the rooftops and the wet palms of Mission Street, a blue house on Hillcrest had every light on at seven in the morning.

The kettle's tick turned into a thin whistle, and Amma lifted it off the flame before it could scream.

"Ravi's birthday," she said, from the kitchen, without turning around. She was answering a question Arenya hadn't meant her to hear. "Day, month, year. She said it was the only date in this family she never forgot." The cups clinked. "Don't tell him. He'll cry again."

Nana didn't stand. She held out one hand, palm up, the way you'd ask a child for something they shouldn't be holding. "Give it a minute, *mija*. The message is already inside. It came down with the ringing." She tapped the back of the phone with a fingernail. "Whoever calls like this is listening for the phone to *answer*. Not for a voice. So we close the door before we open the letter. No signal. No little blue map." Her glance went to the freezer, and the dry ghost of a smile crossed her face. "Not in there. One cold thing in my towel is enough."

On the shrine shelf the lamp flame stood perfectly straight. It wasn't leaning toward anyone. It waited.

The phone was warm against Arenya's thigh, warmer than a phone had any right to be after a day in a bag. Maybe that was just her right hand.

She thumbed on airplane mode and watched the little plane appear like a promise. "Then the door," she said to Kino. "Would you?"

He was already moving. He drew the front curtain, then the one in the hall, and the room went the color of weak tea. Beyond the porch light the fog leaned on the glass. He shot the deadbolt and put his back to the door, then crouched, looked at the gap beneath it, and scuffed the mat across the thin gray tongue of fog working at the threshold.

"Check the icon yourself," he said quietly. "Don't take my word. Don't take anybody's."

She checked. She sat on the arm of the couch with the phone cupped in both hands, right palm sweating against the case, and opened her notebook on her knee. Habit. If she was going to be afraid, she was going to be afraid with a pen.

Four digits, then the year. Appa's birthday. She did it slowly, the way you'd turn a key in a lock you suspected was wired.

The screen hesitated, and opened. In the doorway Appa made a small sound, like someone had stepped on his foot. The wallpaper was a photograph of the lamp, taken from below, so that the flame looked enormous.

Nana pulled a dining chair up until her knees almost touched the couch. "Listen once," she said. "Then you turn it off. All the way off."

Arenya pressed play.

The voice was pleasant and unhurried. American, with no region in it anywhere, the kind of voice that had been trained flat, like a lawn.

*"Good evening. This message is for the household of Ms. Susmithaa Sowri."* He said the name right. *Sow-ri*, the *r* tapped against the roof of the mouth. Nobody outside this family had said it that way all week. *"My name is Daniel Hale, beneficiary services at Meridian Mutual Life. First, I'm so sorry for your loss. I understand Ms. Sowri passed early Sunday, at 3:19 a.m."* A sympathetic pause. *"In March, Ms. Sowri took out a policy with us and named a single beneficiary. Policy terms require me to verify that beneficiary in person. It's a short meeting. It won't take more than a few minutes of her time. Your claim reference is M-M-zero-three-one-nine-four-four. Please call me back at this number at your earliest convenience. We'd like to settle this quickly, for everyone's sake. Thank you."*

A soft click.

Amma's hands had gone tight around the brass kettle handle. "Three nineteen," she said. "The hospital told us *around* three thirty. Nobody told us three nineteen."

Arenya held the power button until the screen went black. Then she wrote, in block capitals, fast: *3:19. DANIEL HALE. MERIDIAN MUTUAL. MM-031944. POLICY IN MARCH. SINGLE BENEFICIARY. "IN PERSON."* She underlined *in person* twice, hard enough to dent the page beneath.

"Three-nineteen is the minute she died," she said. Her voice was flat. "Abernathy—" She stopped. Wrong room. Wrong audience. "Somebody I trust told me the same minute. A hospital doesn't have it. An insurance clerk has it, on a Sunday, by Tuesday?" It had come out clipped, the scared kind of clipped. She turned to the kitchen. "Amma, did she ever mention a policy? Ever?"

Amma shook her head slowly. "Insurance? Your aunty didn't trust the bank with her gold. She kept it in a Horlicks tin." She looked at Appa, and Appa looked at the floor.

"Did either of you see that?" Arenya asked. "The lamp. It went down."

"It ducked." Kino hadn't looked away from the wick. It was climbing back now, slowly, as if it wasn't sure it was allowed. "Not on *beneficiary*. On *her*. 'A few minutes of *her* time.' It went small and blue at the root, like somebody opened a freezer door." He rubbed his thumb once over his left forearm. "The corner went dark too, by the shoes. And my arm answered it."

"He said her name right," Arenya said. "The tapped *r*."

"And *March*," Kino said, very quietly. "The receipt in your book. The temple. A prayer in your name. Same month she buys a policy for one person who has to show up in the flesh." He didn't finish. She could do that math faster than he could, and he knew it, and she hated that he was right.

Nobody calls that number back, she thought. Nobody meets anybody *in person*.

"The back door," Appa said, too quickly, and went. They heard the bolt slide, then the chain, then a second chain nobody had known the house owned.

Nana took the dead phone out of Arenya's hands and folded it into the pot towel like a tamale. "She told me at the Safeway," she said. "By the onions. *'Itzel, I bought a door, and I put Ari's name on it.'* I thought she meant the temple." She tucked a corner of the towel tight. "Maybe she meant both. A door can be locked from either side, *mija*. Whose side is this man on? That's the question."

Kino had gone to the front window. He stood to one side of the curtain, not in front of it, hands loose at his sides, and let his eyes go soft. Arenya watched him do it: the stillness that came over him, animal and patient, the way a deer stops being a deer-shaped thing and becomes part of the hedge. Somewhere out in the fog, she realized, Ix was lending him eyes.

He stood there a long time. When he let the curtain fall back to its finger's-width gap he breathed out through his nose, and she knew before he spoke that the dark hadn't given him anything.

"A door can be locked from either side," he repeated to Nana. "Then we don't open it from ours."

Then, at the far end of the block, an engine turned over.

No headlights. Tires peeled softly off wet asphalt, and a car rolled away dark, and by the time Arenya had turned her head toward the sound it was gone around the corner. Kino's shoulders came down a quarter inch. Out front, something let go of a long breath, the sound a dog makes when it has decided not to bark *yet*.

In the kitchen, the landline rang.

It was the old beige phone on the wall by the fridge, the one Amma kept "for emergencies and Periamma in Chennai." Nobody called it. Amma half rose. Appa froze in the hallway with one hand still on the chain.

It rang again.

"Amma. Don't." Arenya was across the room before the third ring, one hand up, palm out, like she was stopping traffic. Her right hand was hot enough to feel in her teeth. "Let the machine take it. Please. Nobody says a word. Nobody says *hello*. Nobody says our name."

She stopped at the kitchen doorway, close enough to hear, not close enough to touch. A finger to her lips. Appa. Amma. Nana. She flipped the notebook to a clean page and uncapped the pen. Whatever he left on that tape, she was getting every word: his phrasing, his pauses, whatever lived under the voice.

Kino arrived behind her shoulder on the balls of his feet. She felt him there before she heard him, a warmth at her back. "Let it ring out," he breathed. "Everyone off the linoleum by the phone."

Fourth ring. The machine clicked, and Appa's voice from 2009 filled the kitchen, younger and cheerful, a TV going behind him: *"You have reached the Sowri residence, please leave—"*

The beep cut him off.

A pause. Then the same flat, friendly voice.

*"Good evening again. Daniel Hale, Meridian Mutual. I see the other handset has gone quiet. That's all right. People grieve differently."* Paper rustled, too deliberately, like he wanted them to hear him reading. *"I believe this one is for Arenya."*

Amma's hand flew to her mouth.

*"The policy is real, Ms. Sowri. Your aunt was very careful. She paid in cash, in person, at our Colma office, on the nineteenth of March. It covers more than money. Tomorrow at noon, the Serramonte Library, second floor, by the periodicals. Bring identification. If noon doesn't suit, I'm happy to come to you."* He let that sit. *"Though I'd hate to bring paperwork to your mother's door during the mourning period. And Ms. Sowri? Keep that hand warm. Goodnight."*

Click. Then the dial tone, droning on into the tape until the machine gave up.

Arenya looked down. The page was nearly full, her handwriting steady all the way down. Nearly. In the margin, beside the line where he'd said *bring identification*, there were two words she had no memory of writing.

*Eleven, twelve.*

She read them twice. Her handwriting. Her pen. She hadn't heard it, not consciously, and that was the part that turned her stomach over.

"He said *hand*," she said. It came out very level, which was how she knew she was scared. "I never told anyone about my hand. Mr. Bell saw it. Mr. Bell *reported* it." She turned the notebook toward Kino. "Somebody was counting under him. I wrote it down without hearing it. Did you?"

"I heard it." His eyes had gone to the freezer. "Behind his breath. Someone else on the line. Slow, like counting steps in the dark. *Eleven. Twelve.* It stopped when he said *hand*."

She followed his look. Along the seam of the freezer door, where a phone and a card lay wrapped in a pot towel, a thin white rime had grown. It had not been there a minute ago. The fridge hummed on, indifferent. On the shelf behind her the lamp climbed back to gold now that the line was dead, and it leaned hard toward her, the whole flame bent, as if it were trying to put itself between her and the kitchen.

"Counting what?" Kino asked Nana. "Fourteen missed calls, and now numbers. Is it counting us? Is Arenya twelve?"

Arenya made herself look at Amma, at the fingers pressed to her mouth, and made her voice go soft. "Amma. He's not coming here. I won't let him. He said 'mourning period' on purpose, to make you afraid." She breathed. "But he gave me a time and a place. A public library, with a librarian and cameras. That's a tell. Men who want a body don't book the periodicals."

"It's a room on their terms," Kino said. "With a paper trail that says you came willingly. But a library has a second floor and a back stairwell, and nobody picks the ground but the one who's already stood on it." He flexed the cold forearm once. "If you go, you don't go as the one he booked. Not alone. Not tomorrow at noon on his say-so. I'll walk Serramonte tonight and see it before he does. And Ix goes where I can't."

"Don't go alone." It came out sharper than she meant. "Not tonight. If they're counting us, a scout walking their library in the dark is just a bigger number." She shut the notebook on the pen. "Nana. You said Aunty told you she bought a door. Before anyone walks anywhere, I want every word of that Safeway conversation. Then I decide what Serramonte gets from us." And Colma, she thought, and a cash counter, and a clerk who had seen a woman's face on the nineteenth of March. That, at least, was a thing she knew how to do. Find the desk. Find the person behind it.

She went back to the couch and sat by the lamp, and let it lean on her. It was the first thing in two days that had felt like a hand on her shoulder.

Nana didn't answer right away. She got up, crossed to the freezer, and laid her flat palm on the white seam, the way you'd test a child's forehead. The rime softened under her hand, then stopped softening.

"Not your age, *mija*," she said at last. "Your number." She wiped her palm on her skirt. "Your aunty told me once, years ago, when we were both drunk on that terrible Christmas wine. She said they count the ones who carry. While they are still hunting, they don't say the number out loud. When they say it out loud, they are done looking. They have started collecting." Her eyes found the margin of the notebook. "Eleven was somebody. Twelve is you."

No one said anything. The lamp leaned, gold and steady.

Then Nana turned and pointed one finger at Kino, like at a nephew who had been caught climbing the fence. "And no. You don't walk Serramonte tonight. Your shadow can go. Tell him to look and come home. You sleep on this floor where the flame can see you." She clapped her hands once. "Amma, the good blankets. Not the polyester."

Amma moved at last, because now there was something to do with her hands. Appa locked the back door a third time. Nobody told him he already had. Out in the fog, Ix let go of Kino's attention like a dog slipping its leash, and was gone toward Serramonte, low and fast.

Near midnight the house was quiet. Four people, one lamp, a freezer humming a little too cold. On the coffee table, in the margin of a notebook, someone else's count sat in Arenya's handwriting, waiting for its next number.

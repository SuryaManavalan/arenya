Nana Itzel would not drive past the Camry.

She went the long way instead, down El Camino, along the Italian cemetery's wall of cypress, then a U-turn at the gas station on Serramonte Boulevard that made the Corolla's suspension groan like a man getting out of a low chair. "If he is watching the road," she said, "he watches an old lady who is lost. Very believable." She turned the radio to a mariachi station and left it low, which in Nana's language meant *talk, I am not listening.*

So Arenya sat in the back seat with her right hand closed on her knee and her eyes on the dashboard clock, 11:09, and said what she'd been rehearsing since the shed.

"Say it fast, Kino, before I talk myself out of listening. The voice at the shed. Was it Hale?"

Kino sat beside her with the dog-shaped weight of Ix against his shin, his hands flat on his knees as if he'd had to tell them to stay there. "Same voice," he said. "Same dry count as the voicemail. But older than Hale. It doesn't hurry." He nodded once. "It was the same one."

"Okay." She opened the notebook, pencil ready, because a notebook was a thing to do with her hands. "And I'm going to say this once. I'm not answering it today. Neither are you. If it comes down to two strokes, we decide it in daylight, with Nana, and no clerk in the room. Deal?"

He was quiet long enough that she looked at him. His eyes were on the mirror, on the strip of Nana's face in it.

"I wanted to say yes," he said, low, under the trumpets. "At the shed. It's the first thing I've wanted that badly that wasn't the mountain. That's what scares me. Not the clerk. Me." He breathed out. "I didn't answer because it wasn't mine to give. But if I'd been alone, I don't know."

Arenya didn't have a joke for that. She had the dot in her palm, the little pencil stop beside the ruled line, itching like a scab you were forbidden to pick. "Bronze medal for honesty," she said finally, and it came out gentler than she meant. "Deal?"

"Deal." He tipped his chin at her fist. "That dot is yours to decide on. I'll stand wherever you put me. But in there, we don't sign, we don't say your whole name, we don't say yes to anything with a pen on it. We go in to look. If the man offers you a form, you hand it to me and I read every line twice." He took the door handle as Nana swung into the far side of the strip mall's lot, nose out, like a getaway she was pretending not to plan. "Together. Me a half step behind your left. I'm learning that side."

*Colma Notary & Mail* sat two doors down from *Flores de la Paz*, between the florist and a shuttered tax-prep office. Vinyl letters peeled at the corners of the window: NOTARY · LIVE SCAN · APOSTILLE · SE HABLA ESPAÑOL · TAGALOG. Across Mission Road, in the gap between the monument showroom and the florist's van, the beige Camry sat where it had sat all morning. Still empty.

Nana killed the engine. "I stay with the car. If the gray man comes back, I honk twice. If I honk once, it is because a lady cut me off. Don't confuse them." She looked at Arenya's hand, then away. "Remember what she wrote. Not your full name. Not in a room they chose."

"Not in a room they chose," Arenya said. She pulled her sleeve down over the cold wrist and got out.

Kino gave Ix a low word at the car door, *quédate con Nana*, and the weight left his knee. Then he was behind her left, hood down, hands where anyone could see them. The cousin who doesn't talk much. He had the face for it.

"Abernathy," she said, barely moving her lips, passing the roses. "If the brass smell comes back, say it out loud. Every time."

*Every time, love,* said the voice at her ear, close and unpleased. *Don't push the door yet. Let me look.* And then, as she did anyway: *Right, that's wrong, that is. All them little doors shut tight as held breaths, and one hangin' just ajar. Like a ledger left open at a page. Somebody wants it noticed. Or it's waitin' to be filled in.*

The bell over the door was a real one, brass, on a curl of spring. It gave one flat note. Kino caught the door before it could swing back.

One long room. A wall of brass PO boxes with tiny glass windows ran down the left, and on the right a counter held a laminator, a scale, and a styrofoam cup of sinigang still steaming. Behind it a man in a pressed guayabera was eating the soup with a plastic spoon: sixty or so, reading glasses pushed up into grey hair, a notary seal on a lanyard like a saint's medal. The name plate said R. PANGANIBAN. Halfway down the wall, at hip height, Box 112 hung open half an inch. The only door in the room that wasn't flush.

"Good morning, good morning." He set the spoon down at a precise right angle to the cup. "Mailbox, fingerprints, or notary? If notary, I need ID, you sign my journal, and you give me your thumb." He tapped a fat green ledger at his elbow, bound like an old hymnal. "State law. Everybody gives me their thumb."

Then he looked at her properly, and stopped.

His eyes went to her face, then her sleeve, then her face again, slower. "Ah," he said, quietly, to himself. "You have her chin."

He didn't say whose. He slid the green journal four inches to his left, away from them, and laid his palm flat on its cover. "That one is closed today. Twice this morning people want to look in my book." He nodded at a clipboard by the register. The top sheet was a *Request for Copy of Notary Journal Entry*, filled out in neat block capitals and signed. From the counter she could read the date requested. 03/19. "I tell him the law: you want a line from my book, you write a request, you sign, I mail you a copy in some days. He signed." He picked the spoon back up. "Everybody signs for me. That's the job."

Arenya read the date once and looked away, the way you look away from a menu you've already decided not to order from. She gave him the smile she used on Teddy when she needed a day off.

"Hi, sorry to interrupt your lunch. I'm just curious whether that open box is free." She tipped her head at the wall without pointing. "Seems like a sign. And I'm curious about the lady with my chin, Mr. Panganiban. I'm not here to sign anything, and I'm not asking for your book. You said you keep the law and I respect that." She folded her arms so the closed hand tucked under her elbow. "Scarves? Silver rings? Did she come in here? And is that how you leave 112, or did someone come this morning?"

Then the smell arrived, and she knew it from Abernathy's silence before he spoke. It was the silence of a man at a stove.

*Brass,* he said. *Hot brass, out loud, like you asked. Comin' from 112, the open half-inch, steady as a burner someone's just left. Not the bell. Not the seal on his neck. The box.* A pause. *And there's ink under it, wet, and I nearly know it, love. I nearly—* The coat at the edge of her vision flickered, two places for a frame, then one. *And the green book just went* tick. *Pencil set down on a desk. Something's bein' marked. Don't let that thumb-ledger near your hand. If he offers you the book, the answer's no.*

"Smells like a stove in here," she said, flat and low, to the soup and the room.

Mr. Panganiban's spoon stopped halfway to his mouth. He put it down again, at its angle, and looked past her at the brass wall, the way a man checks a window he's certain he locked.

"Scarves," he said. "Three. In March, in this weather, three scarves, and rings on every finger like she's going to punch somebody." The corner of his mouth moved. "She brought me champorado in a jar because she said my soup was sad. She was not wrong." He wiped his fingers on a napkin, one at a time. "I notarized for her. A declaration. Her brother witnessed, very nervous man, sweats through his shirt. She read every line out loud to me before she signed. Nobody does that. She said, *Rodel, if anybody comes asking about this day, you make them write it down.*" He tapped the clipboard without looking at it. "So I make them write it down."

Kino spoke then, for the first time, to the man and not to her. Quiet, even. "Mr. Panganiban. Has anyone besides you had a hand in that box today?"

"Nobody." Rodel lowered his voice, though there was no one else in the room. "Same day, one hour after the lady, a man comes in. Nice suit. Very polite, asks about the weather. He rents a box. One-twelve. Business name *Meridian Mutual Services.* A year in advance, cash, all twenties, new ones." He shook his head. "I didn't put it together until she was gone. Ay. I should have." He nodded at the wall. "He comes Fridays, seven-forty, before I open. He has a key for the front. Always he closes it. Always. Today is Wednesday, and I came in at eight and it was like that. A finger's width. Like somebody wants me to see."

She had been avoiding looking at it. Now she looked.

Through the little glass window in the box's door stood one thing: a cream envelope, upright against the back. Heavy stock, a printed window, the same stock as the Meridian letter folded in her bag. Where a name would go, three typed words.

**TO THE BENEFICIARY**

And beneath, smaller: *To be collected in person. Identification required.*

"Hija," Rodel said, very gently, and took his reading glasses down from his hair, as if he'd rather see it less clearly. "I don't ask your name. Don't tell me. But if you take that out of the box, you are the one who took it. That is how the post works. And that is how *they* work too, I think."

She did not reach for the box. She took one step back from it, which felt like the smartest thing she'd done all week, and raised her phone. No flash. The window, the three words, the number, the gap. She lowered it before he could object, and he looked at the phone, and at her, and decided not to.

"I'm not taking it," she said. "*In person. Identification required.* That's a door with my name already on the other side. I'm not walking through because somebody left it open." She breathed. "One question, and you can say no. Her declaration, March nineteenth. Not the words. The gist. And the man who signed your request this morning. Did he put down a name?"

"The gist." Rodel folded the napkin into a square, then a smaller one. "She bought nothing. She named nobody. And her niece is party to no contract. *No contract, no agreement, no understanding.* She made me write it three ways. I said, ma'am, one way is enough for the State of California. She said, *It's not for California.*" He set the napkin down. Under his palm the green journal was still, but his hand pressed harder, the way you hold a lid on a pot.

He turned the clipboard around with one finger, so it faced them, without sliding it any closer. The block capitals read **W. BELL**. Under *Purpose of request* the same patient hand had written: *Verification of beneficiary.* A PO box in Daly City. And the signature, *Bell*, trailing off into a long flat stroke, ruler-straight, like a line drawn under a sum.

"He was nice also," Rodel said. "Grey sweater. I shook his hand because I'm polite." He rubbed his palm slowly on his trousers, unhappily. "Still cold."

Her phone was warm in her hand. She turned it over, though she'd promised herself she wouldn't, and the photo had finished loading: the brass door, the half-inch, the cream envelope through the glass. And in the printed window, where the glass in front of her said TO THE BENEFICIARY, the photograph said something else. Typed. Centered. Her whole name. The real one. The one Susmithaa had told her never to say in a room they chose.

She did not say it. Her stomach went down a stairwell without her.

Outside, the Corolla honked. Once. Then, after a deliberate count of two, again.

She turned the phone face-down against her chest. "Mr. Panganiban, thank you. For the champorado. For writing it down three ways." Her voice came out steadier than she was. "Please don't tell Mr. Bell you saw me. And please don't let anyone sign for that envelope." She was already turning. "Kino. Car. Walk, don't run."

At the door she stopped, one second, not looking back. "Leave the box open," she said to the room. "Let it wait."

The bell gave its flat note behind them. Cold water and roses, the sidewalk slick. Kino took the street side without being asked, so he was between her and Mission Road, and she heard him say, low, "Phone in your pocket, face down. Don't read it again out here." He never looked at the Camry. He looked at the alley mouth and the florist's van and every window that might hold a grey sweater. At the Corolla he opened her door, checked the street once more, and folded in beside her. Ix pressed against his knee at once, and his hand went to its head.

"Nana, go. Not past the Camry. Whichever way you like, as long as it's slow and boring."

"My specialty," said Nana, and had the engine going before the door shut.

Arenya's hand wouldn't stop shaking, so she put it flat on her knee until it did. "It's Bell," she said. "Grey sweater, cold hands. He filed a request for Aunty's notary entry from March nineteenth, *verification of beneficiary.* And there's an envelope in a box in there with my name on it. My whole name, typed. It wasn't on the glass. It was on the photo." She swallowed. "So it's only my name if somebody looks at it through a lens. I don't know what that means yet."

She wrote it down because writing was something that ended: *Rodel Panganiban. Box 112. Meridian Mutual Services, cash, March 19. Collected Fridays 7:40. Bell: PO box Daly City.* She looked up at the mirror, at Nana's eyes. "Hale's noon at the library is in twenty minutes. I'm not going. But Friday at 7:40, somebody comes to that box, and I want to know who, and I want to know it from a car, not a counter. Take us home."

Nana signaled, the most law-abiding signal in the history of San Mateo County, and pulled out away from the Camry, toward the cemeteries.

In the side mirror, the Camry was not empty anymore. Mr. Bell sat behind the wheel in his grey cardigan with a small notebook open against the steering wheel, writing without looking down. He didn't turn his head as they passed. He didn't have to. Then he closed the notebook and got out and crossed the street with the unhurried walk of a man with an appointment. At the florist's buckets he stopped long enough to choose a single white chrysanthemum, and paid in coins.

Then he went two doors down and opened the door of Colma Notary & Mail.

The bell was a real bell, brass, on a curl of spring. She watched it move. She didn't hear it ring.

"He went in," Nana said, to the windshield. Her knuckles were pale on the wheel. "Rodel is a stubborn man. Stubborn men are good at paper." A long breath. "*Mija.* Whatever is on your phone, don't show me in the car. Show me in my kitchen, with the door shut and the pozole on."

"Pozole, door shut. Yes, Nana." It came out smaller than she'd have liked. She pressed her closed hand between her knees. "He bought a flower for Rodel, didn't he? That's what you do before you ask a stubborn man for something."

Nobody answered. Mission Road unrolled, cemetery after cemetery, green lawns and white stones in their patient rows. She didn't open the notebook again. She counted cemeteries instead, one, two, three, and caught herself at it, and kept counting anyway.

"Kino." She kept her eyes on the lawns. "In the kitchen, I want you to read what's on my phone first. Not me. Read it twice, like you said. If it says my name, I want one person who isn't scared of it to tell me what it says after."

Beside her he didn't say *yes.* He only turned his left forearm over on his knee, the jaguar-spotted one, and left it there, open, between them.

Under her fingers, faint as a held breath, the pencil dot beside the rule was still waiting.

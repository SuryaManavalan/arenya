The first thing was the smell: burnt hair and hot plastic, rising from the top of his own skull.

The second was that he was sitting. Front passenger seat of the grey sedan, belt across his chest, hands open on his knees. Fog had beaded on the windshield. Half a block up, through the wet, a porch light snapped on at a stucco house, and someone inside was screaming a name he didn't know. *Aunty*, maybe. The word went into him like a nail.

At his feet the canister ticked as it cooled. Its little status light was green, which meant *full*. Through the inspection glass there was only a grey drift of ash.

His right hand was inside his jacket. He didn't remember putting it there. It held a pen, and from the pen a cold thread ran up his forearm like a fishing line with something small and alive on the far end. Somewhere to the south-east it pulled. A woman. *Awake.*

Dorsey sat behind the wheel with his chin on his chest. Pruitt and Hale were slumped in the back. All three had their eyes open. Nobody was blinking.

The Crown crackled and his molars buzzed with it. *"—ive, status. Team Four, sta—"* Shepherd, cut to pieces. *"—canister reads full. Confirm col—"*

In the back seat Hale blinked. His head turned, slowly and evenly, the way a security camera turns, until it was pointed at Caleb.

Fourteen months of habit knew exactly what his face should be doing. For the first time, it wasn't doing it.

*Face. Do the face.*

He had done it ten thousand times without thinking, and now he had to think, which was like trying to remember how to swallow. Jaw slack. Eyes soft, aimed at a point eighteen inches past the glass. He breathed the way the others breathed, slow and even, while his heart hammered under it like somebody locked in a trunk.

He didn't look at Hale. He looked at the porch light.

"Lead," he said. The voice came out flat. Good. Flat was easy; flat was fourteen months. "Canister reads full. Collection confirmed. Team Four standing down."

His left hand stayed in his jacket. Beneath the fabric his fingers let go of the pen and closed around the two Vesper doses. Not because he wanted one. Because he needed to know they were there. His right hand slid off his knee, slow as a sleeper turning over, and came to rest beside the seatbelt buckle. His thumb found the release and did not press.

The Crown swallowed his words and gave back static. Then Shepherd, unhurried as a dentist: *"—copy, Four. Full canister. Good wo—"* The buzz in his teeth changed pitch, as if a finger had been laid on a wire. *"—Strand. Your filament's still out. Why is your filament still out?"*

It was. The cold line ran up his arm, south-east, to whoever was awake on the other end. He'd never reeled it in. In the back seat Hale's camera-head stayed on him, and Hale's lips parted a little, as though about to repeat whatever he'd been told to repeat.

Dorsey's fingers twitched on his thigh. He was coming up.

"Residual tether," Caleb said. Bored. A man reading a gauge. "Target went down hard. Lift was messy. Something kicked back through the line. I'm reeling it in."

He meant it to sound like procedure, so he did it as procedure. Under the jacket his left hand left the Vesper and found the pen again. He pinched the thread where it crossed his wrist and *folded* it, the way you'd fold a wet paper chain, then pushed the fold back along the line toward the woman and shut it like a fist. He didn't snap it. He didn't want to hurt her, and he didn't want Shepherd to feel a snap. He only wanted it quiet, a spent line.

His eyes never left the porch light. Hale could look all he liked. Strand was boring. Strand had always been boring.

"Filament's closing. Give it ten seconds. Four's ready to roll on your word."

The fold took. He felt it travel down the line like a knot in a hose, away from him, and close somewhere warm. The pull dwindled to a whisper, the ghost of a pulse he'd only notice by holding perfectly still. Not cut. Not gone. Somewhere a woman wore a cold coin on her skin that only he could find.

It cost him. Something behind his left eye cramped like a hand that had held a pen too long, and for one second the fog on the windshield had edges, and faces in it. Then it was fog again. The Vesper he hadn't taken, sending its first bill.

*"—copy. Tether clo—"* Shepherd sounded satisfied, or as close as he got. *"—Four, proceed to Drop Two. Canister goes hand to hand. No stops."* The Crown clicked and the buzz in his teeth flattened to the old hum, only thinner, a station half tuned out of.

Hale blinked once more and turned back to his window. He'd bought it, or he'd stopped looking. With Hale there was no telling the difference.

Dorsey sat up, started the engine without a word, and put on the wipers. He signalled on an empty street at three in the morning. The sedan rolled past the stucco house with its porch light and its screaming, and turned toward the freeway.

Caleb rode the first two lights with his hands where they had always been, and counted. Eleven seconds of red at the first. Nine at the second. He watched Dorsey's right hand on the wheel and Hale's reflection in the side mirror, and the dashboard clock that wouldn't move fast enough.

"Canister's mine till the handover," he said in the old Lead voice. He bent, lifted it from the footwell and set it on his lap, both hands around it like a man holding a sleeping child. It was warm. Ash shifted inside. He made himself not think about whose. "Drop Two gets it from me, not off the floor."

Nobody argued. Nobody ever argued with Lead.

The on-ramp light came up long and red, an empty intersection around it, fog haloing the lamps. His thumb found the release. His other hand slid beneath the canister and found the door handle.

*Not for me,* he thought. *For her. For whoever's in here.*

Dorsey braked the way he did everything, smooth and on time. The sedan settled on its springs.

*Click.* In a car this quiet, the buckle sounded like a rifle bolt.

Three heads came up together.

He didn't think. Fourteen months of someone else's reflexes were still in his legs, and for once he let them work for him. Belt off, door open on cold fog and wet asphalt, canister clamped to his ribs before the click had finished echoing. One shoe on the road, then the other. Left, away from the freeway, toward the black gap between two dark houses.

Behind him someone said "*Lead*" in a voice with no feeling in it at all. He didn't turn. He ran.

They didn't chase him. That was the worst of it.

The sedan's doors stayed shut. The engine idled. The light changed to green and the car sat through it, three men waiting for instructions the way a television waits for a channel. He went over a low wall and through a side yard: wet ivy, a recycling bin, a dog two houses down that didn't wake. Then he was in the black slot between two garages with his back against stucco, and the canister was warm against his ribs like something breathing.

Through the fog, the sedan's indicator ticked. Then it pulled away toward the freeway, unhurried. Going to report.

The Crown hummed in his teeth. Thinner, but there. And then, for the first time in fourteen months, the voice in it wasn't giving a status call.

*"Caleb."*

Shepherd said it softly and patiently, like a man calling a cat in off a road. Nobody had said that name since the day the Crown went in. He had forgotten it had a sound.

*"That canister's empty, son. You know it, I know it. Whatever you think you're carrying, it's ash. Come in on your own and this stays a malfunction. We'll fix the Crown, and you won't remember being scared."* A pause. *"You've got the tether folded, too. Clever. It'll unfold. They always do."*

On his forearm the thread gave one faint pulse, south-east. She was still out there. So was the leash in his skull.

Caleb slid down the stucco until he was crouched in the wet dark with the canister in his lap, and he said it out loud, because he no longer knew how to say anything inside his own head.

"My name's Caleb." It came out cracked. Too loud; he dropped it. "You said it like you own it. You don't. You don't get to say it again."

He waited. The Crown hummed and Shepherd breathed, and he could feel the man smiling in the static.

"It's not empty," he said, low. "Her name was Susmithaa. I remember the sound she made. I'm not coming in, Shepherd. Not to get fixed, and not to forget. I'd rather be scared."

Then he stopped talking to the voice and started thinking like Lead, because the worst of it was that the voice was right: they'd track the Crown. He had a hand-length of time before the sedan reported. And the thread on his arm was a trail he mustn't follow, because if he walked to that woman, he walked the team to her door. He patted his pockets: dead burner, knife, keys, three hundred and eighty dollars. Water, he thought. Metal. Mass. Anything to muffle a signal.

*"Susmithaa,"* Shepherd said, tasting the name like a wine he hadn't ordered. *"Noted. Be seeing you, Caleb."* He hung up, if that was the word. The hum stayed, and that was how Caleb knew he was listening.

At the far end of the slot the yards fell away into a cut in the hill: a concrete drainage channel, black water at the bottom, and a storm culvert mouth big enough to crouch in. Old corrugated steel, rusted to lace at the lip, running under the street. The water was ankle-deep and so cold his feet went numb at once.

Three steps in, the hum in his teeth thinned. Five, and it was a mosquito in another room. Ten, and it was gone.

Silence. The first real silence in fourteen months, with nobody in his skull. Only dripping, his own breath, and the canister warm against his chest.

Then the silence filled with everything the Crown had been holding back. His hands began to shake and wouldn't stop. Behind his eyes the cramp spread like spilled ink, and the dark of the culvert grew grain, faces in the rust. His left pocket felt heavier than two little doses had any right to. And he remembered. All of it. Rooms, nets, names, the sounds people made. Fourteen months.

He sat down in the water because his legs decided to. It felt like a hand around each ankle. He set the canister on his knees and for a long time did nothing with his shaking hands but hold it.

Then, because a soldier does an inventory when everything else has failed, he started one. Out loud, flat and quiet, so the steel could hear.

"Tartine. The old man with the scarf. That was us. Yuri something." His jaw worked. "Tonight. Susmithaa. Daly City." The names kept coming, and each one landed in his chest like a round he couldn't un-fire. "Dorsey. Pruitt. Hale. I never learned your real names. Fourteen months and I never asked."

His voice went, and came back too loud. It slammed off the corrugated walls and he flinched and clamped a hand over his own mouth. He was laughing, or something near it, with no air in it. "*Gallows,*" he whispered into his palm. "Good. Still got that."

He wiped his face with the back of his wrist and stared at the inspection glass, at the grey drift behind it, the ash of a woman's whole life of light. The green status light pulsed on it, patient.

"I don't know where you go from here," he told it. "I don't know who to give you to. But I'm not handing you back." He drew the canister into his chest and rested his chin on the lid. "Not till somebody who can use you tells me what you are. Somebody not them."

He left the Vesper in his left pocket. The pocket stayed heavy. He kept his hand away from it and counted drips, waiting for the shaking to stop, or for him to stop noticing it.

It was 3:14 in the morning.

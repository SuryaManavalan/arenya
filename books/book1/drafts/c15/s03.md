At three in the morning the fog lay over the Bay like a sheet over furniture, and Kino Balam sat on the cold rock above the East Peak fire road and asked.

He had been asking since midnight, the way Nana had taught him: breath low, eyes soft, no demand in it. Below him the bridge towers stuck up out of the white, and the Sutro mast, and nothing else. The city was a glow under the milk.

Ix woke in him before he knew why. It was the feeling of a cat standing up on your chest, weight shifting, claws not quite out. The jaguar was looking south.

Then the mountain moved.

Nothing shifted. The manzanita didn't stir and not one pebble rolled. But he felt it through his palms and up through his tailbone, slow and enormous, a sleeper turning over in a bed she had lain in for ten thousand years. She turned her face toward the city, toward something lit down there under the fog, and her back came around to him.

The mark on his left forearm went cold.

Ix made a sound Kino had never heard from him, low and unhappy, almost a whine.

"Easy," Kino said. He laid the forearm across his knee and looked at the Lady's thumbprint, grey as river stone, a bruise with no heat in it. "I felt it."

He pressed his palms flat to the rock again and asked, not for anything, only *who*. He kept his breath slow. He didn't let the question curdle into *why not me*, though it tried. Under his tongue the Spanish was ready, the words he only ever said to Nana: *no me dejes*. He swallowed them.

South, then. He marked the line against the Sutro mast: Inner Sunset, Daly City, somewhere along that glow. He took out his phone. It was three in the morning and she would be awake anyway. His thumb hovered over her name.

He did not tell her about the dreams. But he called.

At 5:48 Ava Lindqvist woke with her face wet and both hands raised in front of her, cupped, as if she were holding something that would go out if she breathed on it.

There was nothing in them. The radiator ticked. Her roommate's door was shut, as it always was. Grey light, fog on the window.

She'd dreamed of a woman made of lamps, standing in a garden in the sky. Four men pulled a net, and the lamps went out one by one until there was only one left, in somebody else's hand. She knew it was a dream. She had a literature review on dreams. But her palms were warm, just the palms, the way they are after a mug, and she hadn't held a mug.

"Okay," she said, very low, to no one. "Case report. Subject: female, twenty-five. Woke at 5:48. Bilateral palmar warmth, no stimulus. Tearful, cause unknown. Dream content: lamps, net, four men." Her voice wobbled. "Four men. Great. Very specific. Very *not* a coincidence, brain, thank you."

She checked for fever with the back of her fingers and her wrist. None. The warmth sat in her palms, steady, and it smelled faintly of camphor and ghee. She decided that was a smell she was inventing. She wrote it all in the dream log with the time, because that was what you did, and added: *Coffee maker reads 12:00. (Power dip? CHECK.)*

In the kitchen the coffee maker blinked 12:00, 12:00, 12:00.

"That's electricity," she told the room. "I love electricity."

---

On the mountain, the rock gave him *where* the way a stove gives heat in the dark. South and low, under the fog: a small, steady warmth like a candle in a window. For one breath he smelled something that didn't belong on Tam, burnt butter and something sharp and medicinal, like a church that wasn't his. Ix's ears went flat. He didn't hate it. He respected it, the way he respected the bigger cats in his dreams.

Nana picked up before the first ring finished. He heard her kettle, and the radio murmuring in Spanish, and then the long pause of a woman sitting down.

"Mijo. I felt it. Like the bed tipped." Another pause. "Listen to me. She didn't turn her *back*. You're thinking like a boy whose mother looked at the baby. She turned her *face*. When the old ones look at something, it's because something new came into the house. A big fire. Old. Older than us, maybe. Not from here." The kettle began to scream and she ignored it. "The land doesn't stare at what it isn't worried about or curious about. So which is it? That's your work. Not *why not me*. I can hear you thinking it from Novato."

He pressed his palms down and tried to narrow the warmth. It blurred. South, low, under the fog, and then only fog and his own pulse. He lost it.

"Face, not back," he said, and breathed out something close to a laugh. "Okay. I hear you." Ix pressed the inside of his ribs, wary and curious. "There was a smell. Burnt butter, and something like a church. Not ours. Ix doesn't hate it. I can't tell you if it's worried or curious. I lost it in the fog."

"You didn't lose it," Nana said. The scrape of a chair came down the line, pulled out for him. "You were *shown* it. There's a difference, and you'll learn it." Then, drier: "And bring the thing you're not saying. It fits on my table too."

He didn't answer that. "Pon agua, Nana. The kettle. I'm coming down at first light. Put the good bread out. I'll be there before the sun is."

He hung up before she could hear his voice change.

In the Inner Sunset the microwave did not blink. It read **2:41**, steady. So did the oven. Not counting, not flashing: the clocks had lost power and then decided to stop instead of reset. Her phone said 5:51, two percent, nothing overnight.

The oven door was black glass. Ava stood in front of it in her sleep shirt with her reflection faint in the grey light, and in the reflection her hands were cupped at her chest.

She looked down. Her hands hung at her sides.

She looked back. The woman in the glass held a single wick-flame, gold, the kind that sits on a brass lamp, and it lit the underside of her reflected chin. It did not flicker when the radiator ticked. The camphor was very strong now.

Her first instinct was to look away. She made herself not.

"Okay," she said, in a small flat voice. "Okay okay okay. Hypotheses." She raised one finger; in the glass the reflected finger rose. That much matched. "One: sleep deprivation, hypnopompic imagery, common. Two: me. I'm what's wrong. Three…" She didn't say three. Her mother's kitchen rose up in her head and she shoved it back down. *Not now. Document.*

She thumbed the camera on at two percent and filmed the oven door, narrating like a protocol because her voice held steady when it had a job. "5:51 a.m. Oven clock reads 2:41, static. Microwave reads 2:41. Reflection shows a lit flame in the hands. My hands, direct observation, are empty."

Then she did what she'd tell any participant to do: she moved. She stepped left, and the flame stayed in its cupped hands. She crouched, so it should have left the frame, and it didn't. She counted seconds, real hands, glass, real hands, glass. A hallucination would drift or lag.

It didn't drift. It was perfectly, insultingly consistent.

She held her breath. In the glass the flame guttered, as if her breath had been feeding it. When she let the air go, it stood up again, steady and gold.

"Hi," she whispered. "If you're a symptom, you're a very *consistent* one."

And past her reflected shoulder the window behind her was not her window. There was a brass lamp on a low shelf. There were marigolds, browning at the edges. There was an old woman's hand, a ring on the smallest finger, reaching for the wick to pinch it out.

The hand stopped. It did not pinch.

"I know you," Ava said, barely a sound. "Not you. The lamp. I don't know why I know the lamp."

The oven clock went from 2:41 to **5:52**. The microwave beeped once, like something waking. In the glass, the hands were empty. So were hers. The camphor stayed.

She plugged the phone in beside the toaster and didn't watch the footage. She opened her notes and typed with unsteady thumbs: *5:52 — flame responds to breath. Second scene in reflection: brass lamp, marigolds, old woman's hand, ring on little finger. Hand stopped. Did not extinguish. Smell: camphor. Clocks reset.* Then, because the protocol said to: *Subjective state: scared. Not psychotic-scared. Different scared. (Flag: is that a thing I can tell?)*

She set the phone face-up on the counter and, very carefully, cupped her hands again before the glass.

"Don't go out," she said. "Whoever you are. I've got you."

---

Kino stood a moment longer with his back to the Lady and his face to the city glow, his forearm bare to the fog. It wasn't a prayer. He didn't trust himself to pray, not tonight. He said it quietly, to the mountain and not to Nana.

"I'll go where you're looking. You don't have to turn around for me."

Then he started down the fire road, in the dark, without a light, the way he walked on patrol: silent, one hand loose at his side. The gravel was pale under him. Past the water tank the wind off the ridge changed. It stopped hitting his back and came level with his shoulder, the way someone falls into step beside you.

He didn't look up and she didn't turn. But for the length of one switchback the cold in his forearm eased, as if a thumb had pressed there in passing.

Below him, at its eastern edge, the fog had begun to go pink.

---

In the kitchen the glass showed nothing now. No flame, no lamp, no window that wasn't hers. Only a tired woman in a sleep shirt holding nothing.

But her palms were warm. It wasn't a heat she could feel on the skin; it was under the skin, steady, about the size of a candle flame. It stayed when she breathed and it stayed when she didn't. It didn't need her breath anymore. It had only wanted to know that she would hold it.

On the counter the phone buzzed once. A calendar reminder she hadn't set: title blank, time 3:20 a.m., already past. When she tapped it there was nothing there.

Outside, the N-Judah ground by on Irving Street, the first run of the morning, and the camphor finally left the room the way a guest leaves: slowly, and looking back.

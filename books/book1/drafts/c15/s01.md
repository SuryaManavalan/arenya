Two days of soft, buffering nonsense, and then the buffering stopped.

Arenya was standing in her parents' backyard in Daly City, and the backyard had too many directions. She could see the fence from the front and from behind at the same time. She could see the curry-leaf tree from inside its own growing. Fog poured along the ground, and it wasn't weather. It moved like a tide that had an opinion, thick and dark and patient, and it was breathing.

At the bottom of the garden stood Susmithaa Aunty's cottage, except the cottage window *was* the brass lamp, the kuthu vilakku, enlarged to the size of a temple, and every one of its wicks was burning.

Her aunt stood barefoot at the center of it, and she was not her aunt. Or she was, the way a sunrise is a light bulb. She was a woman made of lamps: a thousand small flames in the shape of a woman, braids of fire with tiny silver charms ringing in them. Far beneath her, the way you see the bottom of a pool, Arenya could make out a small woman in a cardigan standing in the real garden, perfectly still, staring at the street.

Half a step behind the burning woman, a lanky figure flickered in and out of the frame. Shabby long coat. A light swinging on a chain like a lantern. He was swearing steadily in something that might have been cockney.

Four men stood around them. Arenya couldn't hold on to their faces. The faces slid off her attention like rain off glass, smudged thumbprints, each man wearing a halo of grey static. Between them a net was being drawn tight, humming filaments that *pulled* like magnets, dragging flame after flame toward the center and snuffing it. One of the men wore a light grey jacket.

She had seen that jacket before. It had been on her wall.

*This is a dream*, she told herself. *This is the cheese-before-bed dream. You took the wall down and your brain is putting it back up in Amma's garden.* She said it twice. It didn't help, because her feet were cold, and you don't feel cold feet in dreams.

The flames were going out one by one. Susmithaa turned her head through an angle that wasn't an angle and looked straight at her.

"Kanna." It was the most frightened Arenya had ever heard her. "You came. Of course you came. *Listen to me.*"

"Aunty." It came out small, like she was six years old and lost in the Serramonte mall. She swallowed and tried again, sharper, the voice she used when she was scared. "Okay. I'm listening. I'm *listening*. Tell me what to do."

She didn't wait for permission. She reached, the way you'd reach for someone slipping on a wet step, toward the nearest flame in her aunt's burning hand. And as she reached she made herself look at the man in the grey jacket. Not at his face, because her eyes kept skidding off it. At the jacket. The collar, the seam at the shoulder, the way it hung. *Details. You can always hold on to details.* She tried to pin him the way she'd pin a still frame. *Tartine. Corner table. You.*

"I know you," she said, and her voice shook. "I've got you on a wall."

The flame came to her hand and did not burn. It felt like Aunty's palm on her forehead when she'd had a fever at eight, dry and warm, smelling faintly of camphor and ghee. One silver charm rang against her knuckle, a bell no bigger than a grain of rice. For half a heartbeat the net's pull slackened around that single wick, as if it had to work out where the wick belonged now.

Susmithaa saw it. Something crossed her burning face that wasn't relief. It was a decision.

"Good girl," she breathed. "Hold that. Don't let go of that."

Then the man in the grey jacket turned toward Arenya.

He didn't turn his head. The static around him turned, the whole smear of him shifting like a satellite dish finding a signal. The collar was there, and the shoulder seam, and the way the jacket hung slightly long on the left, as if something heavy sat in that pocket. Then the details began to slide, the way a word goes strange when you say it forty times. The net's hum dropped lower. One filament peeled away from the lamp-woman and drifted across the fog toward her. It didn't hurry. It was curious.

"—bleedin' *wire*, bleedin' *magnets*, who brings *magnets* to a—"

The lanky man had heard her voice. For one frame he was in two places at once, behind Susmithaa and at the edge of the lamplight near Arenya, coat flapping in a wind that wasn't blowing. Then he was only in the second place, slope-shouldered and wrong, standing between her and the man in grey.

"No no no no. *Oi.* Your Ladyship. Don't you *talk* to 'im." The patter was going too fast and sounded thin, a man whistling past a graveyard. "You don't *know* 'im, you've never *met* 'im, you're a nice girl 'avin' a cheese dream, ain't that right, lads? Nothin' to see!" He jerked his chin at the four smudges, grinning with too many teeth, then dropped his voice for her alone. "They count what they can see, love. Don't let 'em count *you*."

He swung the lantern on its chain, hard and overhand, and brought it down on the nearest humming filament where it bit into a cluster of Susmithaa's flames. He was trying to burn it, or snap it, or at the least get his own light into the knot so a few wicks could slip free. With his other hand, the one that never quite had the right number of fingers, he cupped the air around the flame Arenya held, like a man sheltering a match.

"Take that one. Go on. Just the one, *hold* it, don't let go of it whatever she says." Halfway through the sentence his voice slipped, and for half a word it wasn't cockney at all. It came out low and old and very sure. *"—keep it lit, child—"*

Then he caught it, and the grin was back, wobbling. "Union rules. Hold the light. *Madam!*" He was shouting over his shoulder now. "Madam, they're closin' the east side, I can't hold the east side and the kid, pick one, *pick one*—"

Arenya closed her fist around the flame, hard, the way you hold a phone in a crowd. With her other hand she was already writing in the air, because her hand didn't know it wasn't holding the coffee-stained notebook. *Collar. Seam. Hangs long on the left. Left pocket. Something heavy in the left pocket.*

"Left pocket," she said out loud, to herself, to the flickering man, to anyone. "Jacket hangs long on the left. Left pocket. Left pocket. Left—" The words were already going strange in her mouth. She hung on to the shape of them like a rail.

The lantern came down on the filament with a sound like a struck tuning fork. For an instant the light on the chain flared white and the wire *glowed*, cherry-red along an arm's length of it. Three wicks in the knot shuddered and leaned toward freedom.

Then the net did what magnets do. It took the heat and pulled it in. The glow ran down the filament like water down a drain, out of the lantern and into the knot, and the three wicks went out together, *pff pff pff*, little bright deaths. The lantern guttered to an ember on its chain. Two of the smudged men turned their static toward the lanky man. There was no anger in it. Only inventory.

But the flame he was cupping stayed lit. Between his wrong-fingered hand and Arenya's closed one it burned steady as a pilot light, and the curious filament slowed. It couldn't quite see past him.

Susmithaa heard. In the center of the fire she stopped fighting for the east side. She simply let it go, a whole flank of her flames snuffed in one long exhale, so that she could turn fully toward the two of them. Down in the real garden the small woman in the cardigan swayed on her feet.

"I pick," she said. "*The kid.* Always the kid, Abernathy. Stand there. Don't move from there."

*Abernathy*, Arenya thought, and the name landed somewhere familiar and somewhere not.

The filament drifted closer. She did not step back. She leaned away from it, a little, and kept the flame tucked against her chest. "Don't let go. Got it. I'm not letting go." And then, quieter, through her teeth, to the man in the static: "Whatever you are, I'm going to remember you."

*Left pocket.* She had the words. The picture was already leaving her. The collar went first, then the seam, then the long left hang, all smearing into grey the way a dream face does when you try to describe it over breakfast. She was holding a sentence with nothing left inside it.

He didn't lose her, though. The curious filament found the edge of Abernathy's cupped hand, slipped around it like a cat around a doorframe, and touched the inside of her wrist. It was cold. There was a small, precise *click*, like a turnstile, like a barcode reader.

*Counted.*

"No," Susmithaa said, and it was the voice from the engagement dinner, low and certain, sandalwood and silver rings. "Not her. You don't get her."

She stopped fighting. Every flame left in her, a few hundred little lights, turned at once the way a flock turns, and *poured*. It came down her burning arm and across the impossible distance, into the one wick in Arenya's fist. It wasn't heat. It was weight, the weight of every lamp lit in that window for every year Arenya had been alive. Her hand was full of morning. The net clenched shut on the place where the woman had been, and caught nothing. It caught ash, and the faint ring of silver charms falling.

The last of her face was a few flames wide, close enough to kiss Arenya's forehead, and she did.

"You reached, kanna. That's all it ever was, reaching." The voice was fraying. "They aren't the war. They're people who think they can win it before it starts. Something old is waking. The doors are going to open, and every lamp will matter. Keep it lit. Go with all the light you can carry." A ghost of the old mischief crossed what was left of her. "And look after that one. He'll say he's looking after you. *He's lying.*"

Then she was gone.

Abernathy's coat tore loose from the place where she'd stood, as if a hook in him had been unpicked and set into someone else, and he was dragged across the fog to Arenya's side whether he liked it or not. Far below, in the real garden, the small woman in the cardigan folded down onto the wet grass beside the curry-leaf tree. She fell gently, like someone sitting down to rest.

The four men stood around an empty net. Three of them were already turning, the way crews turn at the end of a shift. The one in grey didn't. His static had gone very still, the stillness of a man taking out a pen.

Arenya woke in her own bed in the dark.

Her right hand was clenched so tight that her nails had cut half-moons into the palm, and the palm was warm, warm the way a cup is warm after the coffee's gone. On the inside of her wrist was a cold spot the size of a coin. The clock said 3:19.

At 3:20 her phone lit up on the nightstand. **Amma.**

And from the kitchen, through the half-open door, she heard her kettle being filled, and somebody swearing at it under his breath, in cockney.

She didn't look toward the kitchen. She couldn't, not yet. The kettle and the swearing went into the same drawer in her head as the garden and the fog and the lamp-woman, and she slammed the drawer with her whole body. Her left hand found the phone. The right stayed a fist, and wouldn't open. She put the phone to her ear and heard her own pulse in it before anything else.

"Amma?" A croak. She cleared her throat and tried again, lower. "Amma, it's three in the morning. What happened?"

She already knew.

In the kitchen doorway the kettle clicked off and the swearing stopped. Nothing else in the flat made a sound. He stood very still, one shoulder against the frame, the coat hanging wrong even at rest. In his hands he held the lantern, down to a dull orange ember, the way you'd hold a sparrow.

*Don't answer that straight off, love*, said his voice, thin and close, in a place that wasn't quite her ear. *Let it ring one more. Let her get the news out herself. It's better comin' from her than from your face.* A pause. *That's your aunt's light in your fist. All of it, near enough. Keep that hand shut till it settles, and don't go showin' it to anyone, because it'll still be warm tomorrow and it oughtn't be.* He tried the grin, and it wouldn't hold. *Kettle's on. I can't drink it, I can't pour it, and I filled it anyway. Muscle memory. Don't ask whose.*

He looked at the coin on her wrist, and the patter stopped.

*That cold spot. Somethin' counted you. Don't scratch it. Don't look at it too long, neither. We'll talk about it after your mum.* And then, quieter, not meeting her eyes: *She said somethin' at the end. About me. You 'eard it. Everyone 'ears it.* The cockney went thin for a breath. *I don't know what she meant. I'd tell you if I did. I'm tellin' you now so it's said before you start wonderin' alone. I don't know.*

On the line there was only breathing: wet, ragged, very far from the phone, as if Amma had forgotten she was holding it. Behind her a television was going in an empty room. A man's voice, Appa's, said *okay, okay, okay* to nobody.

"Kanna." Amma's voice was shaking. "It's Aunty. Appa went out because the dog next door would not stop. She was in the garden. Only in her cardigan, in this cold, sitting by the curry-leaf tree like she was waiting for somebody. He thought she was sleeping." A sound came out of her that wasn't a word. "They took her to Seton. They are saying... they did everything, they are saying."

Then came the part that frightened Arenya more than grief would have. She heard it in the way her mother's voice dropped, the way Amma lowered her voice in temples and at the DMV.

"And the lamp went out. Her lamp in the window. Thirty-one years, kanna, I never once saw it out. Appa went to look and there was oil still in it." A breath. "And there is a man here at the hospital. American, in a suit, very polite. He was here *before* us. He asked the nurse for Susmithaa Sowri by her full name, and he asked me..." She stopped. "He asked if she has a niece. Why would he ask that? Kanna, just come. Please come."

Arenya sat in the dark with her nails biting her palm, the warm weight of a dead woman's morning shut inside her fist, and the cold coin on her wrist, and said the only thing there was to say.

Behind her, in the kitchen, the kettle ticked as it cooled.

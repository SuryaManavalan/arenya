Nana had added Susmithaa's name to the parish list in her own handwriting, at the bottom, and the priest said it carefully and correctly and went on.

Amma's hand closed on Appa's sleeve. It stayed there through the whole Mass.

* * *

Holy Cross at five-forty was a city lying down. White stone ran up the hill into the fog, and every third grave had marigolds. Someone's speaker played Vicente Fernández low, and Arenya hated how well it fit.

Nana knelt at the Lucero plot, near the top of section L, and pulled the grass from the edge of Ernesto's marker with her fingers. *Esposo y Padre.* "Go say hello to your friends," she said, without turning. "Take Joaquín."

The friend was forty yards down the row: a newer black stone, no flowers, one grey pebble holding down a square of printer paper. ADONIS QUINN.

Arenya pulled her cuff over her fingers and slid the page out with her body between it and the hill. It wasn't for her. A trace on a faint grid, LANTERN · BENCH 3, a slow line like a heartbeat with all the hurry taken out. Three peaks bracketed in blue ballpoint. *11.0 s.* The third bracket was pressed so hard the pen had nearly gone through.

On the back, a loose hand, written on a knee. *It's eleven seconds. It was always eleven, wasn't it. You wrote WHERE FROM and I thought you meant the money. I'm sorry I didn't come in May.*

Kino's hand had gone to his forearm. "It doesn't point at the stone," he said. "The stone's just where somebody put it down."

She folded the page on its own creases and put it back under the pebble, squared to the dry patch of grass. "I'm sorry," she told the granite. "We'll find where it comes from."

Ava wrote the numbers on her palm. A groundskeeper went by on a cart and said a man in a good coat had sat on this wet grass at six-thirty that morning for an hour. He hadn't brought flowers. He'd brought a pebble from the parking lot.

"Some people don't know what to bring," the old man said. "I don't tell them."

On the exhale, every flame on the hillside leaned toward the Bay. Up the row, Nana looked down and waited until she was sure she'd been seen.

* * *

Amma didn't look at the canister. She looked at Arenya the way she looked at a pot she wasn't sure about, as if the trouble might be under the lid.

"On the tenth day the ashes go to the water," she said. "My mother took my father to the sea at Visakhapatnam and came home and slept for the first time in eleven days. We are past the tenth day. I did not take her." Her thumb moved once on the white cloth. "Your father says the water is wrong. He doesn't say how."

Appa said nothing. His left hand was in his pocket, where the stain was.

"So I ask you, because you know these things now. Don't tell me you don't, kanna. I have watched you put salt on windowsills." Her voice didn't break. It narrowed. "If I give her to the water, is it the water that takes her? Or is it the other thing?"

Then she turned her chin toward the Lucero stone. "Joaquín's grandmother says her husband has room at his feet. Not forever. *Prestado.*" She said it slowly, like a coin she wasn't sure was real. "You decide. I have tried since Sunday and I can't."

Arenya had counted the fog's breath in her teeth all afternoon. *Nine. Ten. Eleven.*

It didn't come. Twelve. Every candle on the hill stood straight up. Thirteen. Then the breath came all at once and bent every flame toward the water, and the white cloth on the canister lifted at one corner and pointed the same way.

Amma held it down with her hand.

She had wanted, once, to find the cracks where the truth leaked out. Here was one on a hill in Colma, and she'd have given anything to be ranking poodles.

"I don't know, Amma," she said. "That's the true answer, and you asked for true. But I saw that cloth. The breath came two seconds late, and when it came, it came for *her*. If something's waiting in the water, it's waiting for her by name. I won't hand it a name it's asking for."

She crouched at the edge of the grass, not on it. "Prestado," she said. "Borrowed. Here, at his feet, until the water's clean. And I'm the one who finds out when that is. That's my job now. When it's clean, I'll drive you to the sea myself. Visakhapatnam, if it has to be. And you'll sleep after."

Her voice wanted to go small. She put an edge on it. "She won't mind the stranger. She laughed at people out of frame. She'd have liked a man who was good with guests." She dipped her head to Nana. "Thank you for the room. We'll come back for her. I'm saying it out loud so it counts."

* * *

Nana opened a hand-sized hollow with a trowel and set the cut turf beside it like a lid. Amma knelt, sari in the wet grass, and set the canister down. Nobody handed it to anybody. They laid the turf over it and covered it with marigolds.

The breath stopped.

Every candle stood dead straight. The speaker by the gate played a chord into nothing. Then the fog came over the western ridge, not rolling but pouring, and where it touched section L the grass went black and seawater beaded in the carved letters of every name on every stone.

At the bottom of the row, something stepped out on legs too long for the hill. A heron, seven feet at the shoulder, grey as a parking-garage ceiling. Each step left a tideline across the graves. Its eye was a flat white coin. Down the slope a little boy pointed and said *big bird*, and his mother went very quiet.

The promise went off in Arenya's chest like a struck match.

Every candle flared white. Under the turf the canister glowed through the soil like a lamp under a blanket. And the hill, the whole old dark land from the ridge to the Bay, took one slow breath on *her* count.

Light by blood. Dark by birthplace. Both answered at once.

She stepped out of herself.

* * *

The bulk over Colma was a city of the dead in lamplight, every grave a lit window, every row a street. The fog was a real sea down here, black and cold, and the heron stood knee-deep in it at the foot of the stair. Far out past the ridge, something the size of a coastline lifted its head.

Her hands were full. In the right, light: lamp-oil and dawn and her sister's laugh, warm as a hand on her back. In the left, dark: fog and soil and patience. Neither asked permission.

Abernathy was already on the first step, waving both arms like a man flagging a bus. "Eyes front, love! Don't you look at the lady." The white coin swung to him. The heron bowed, a short dip of the beak, the nod of a clerk to a man he has filed before, and made a sound like a turn signal. *Tick.* For one frame Abernathy was solid, in a coat that wasn't his.

She didn't think. If she thought she'd start footnoting.

Left hand first: two fingers at the long grey neck, and a thumbprint of night settled under the white eye. *You count things? Count this.* Then the right. Susmithaa laughing at someone out of frame. Amma sleeping after Visakhapatnam. She threw all of it down the row past Abernathy's ear and over the jaguar's spotted back, into the open throat.

"You don't get her," she said, and her voice came out too loud and very much hers over the whole lit city. "She's *borrowed*."

The heron screamed like a tide going out too fast over stones.

Then it bent and drank the black water at its feet, and the stair tilted toward the sea. A lamp no bigger than a cupped hand came loose from Ernesto's window and began to slide down the steps, a brass flame, small and bright.

*Tick,* said the heron.

* * *

She never could say afterward how long it lasted. The undertow took Kino and dragged the cat down the stair, and he fetched up with his flank against the stone and the lamp beside his paw. It went through Abernathy like wind through a coat on a hook, and the lamp stopped against his shin the way a dog stops against the one person at the party it knows. Gold ran along his edges, a high collar, brass buttons, a servant's livery from a house older than the country.

On the hill, her body stood with its eyes on nothing, and the foam-hands went for the wrist.

The jerk came like a hand on her collar, hauling her down out of the sky. Cold, and hers. She planted her feet on the top step of a city made of windows and leaned the other way, into the dark hand, the patient one, the one that had held this land since before anyone drew a county line on it.

*No. I'm not leaving this hill until she's off it.*

It held. She sent one thin thought down through the bulk, to a man with coffee on his sleeve. *Wrist. Cuff. Off.*

Abernathy flicked a coin of gold across the lit city. It landed warm in her palm. "Spend it on whatever you throw next. Don't waste it on a speech."

She spent it on the bird's cracked eye, one line, the mark and the light and the squire's word all in the same place. The coin of white split like a dinner plate. Seawater ran down the grey face. The heron's head jerked back, and it was hurt now the way animals get hurt, honestly, with nothing filed about it.

It inhaled. The whole sea behind it went out with it, and far past the ridge the coastline-sized thing breathed *with* it. The cold that came up the stair was older than January. The brass lamp lifted off the step.

She held. She put her free hand out under it, palm up, not touching, and Abernathy leaned his whole self into the nothing in front of the flame and the nothing held. *Stay. You're with the Sowri, and the Sowri stay.* Kino went off the last step into the black shallows, ears under, rosettes going dark one by one.

Somewhere above, on the real grass, Nana threw a fistful of salt over Arenya's frozen wrist instead of over the hole in her husband's grave. *Los vivos primero.*

Then the leash went slack. The lamp stood up straight on its step, waiting. Abernathy walked backwards up the stair saying *follow the coat, Your Lamp*, and it followed him like a duckling, and settled into her open hand.

It weighed nothing. It was warm as a cup of tea someone else had made for her. And she heard it laugh, short and delighted, at something out of frame.

The heron saw it go into her hand. It stopped. It looked at her, not through her, and it wasn't a clerk's look. It was the look of a thing doing a sum and not liking the answer.

Kino rose out of the water behind it. The cat went up the grey neck like night up a wall, and the sound that came out of the bird was a breakwater giving way. *Esta no es tu agua. Go home.*

* * *

It was nine steps from the sea and broken, lowering itself into the black water a step at a time. Her right hand was already up. The Lance sat in her wrist, warm and easy, and the grey back was half turned. That would be clean. She'd never find out what it was.

She lowered the hand.

"You don't kill the only witness," she said, mostly to herself. She came down two steps, past Abernathy, until the cracked eye couldn't pretend she wasn't there.

"Hey. You count things. So take this to whoever does your filing. Susmithaa Sowri is an open entry. Two people stood for her tonight, her mother and me. She's borrowed, at Ernesto Lucero's feet, until the water's clean. And when it's clean, I bring her down to it. Not you." Her throat hurt. "If your master wants to talk about the balance, it can come and ask. Properly. With manners. Nana leaves salt for company."

She spent the last of what she had on a fine bright outline across its back, to watch it home.

The heron shook, the way a wet bird shakes, and the light came off it in drops.

It would not be watched. But the drops lay on the black water and did not go out, and the film they made began to move. Not west toward the open ocean. North and east, against the wind, around the shoulder of the hill, toward the Bay. Oil on a current running the wrong way. For three seconds she followed it, a bright thread toward the place where the city's lights ended and old industrial dark began.

Then it thinned and was gone, and the heron looked at her once more and bowed. The short dip. The nod you give a man you've filed.

*Tick.*

It walked into the sea, and the sea closed over it without a ripple.

* * *

She came back into her body like stepping off a bus that hadn't quite stopped.

Her wrist was wrapped in Ava's scarf. Caleb's palms were on her arm, and there was coffee down her sleeve. Her right hand was closed. When she opened it there was nothing in it, but the canister under Amma's arms was warm as a teacup, and every candle on the hill burned straight up.

She didn't stand. She let Caleb rub three more breaths into her arm. "Thank you," she said, hoarse, and looked at his hands, and didn't finish. Then she crawled the three feet to Amma and laid her palm between her mother's shoulder blades, the way Amma had done for her on Sunday.

"It's over. Feel the tin. She's warm, and she's staying."

*The lamp's laughing,* Abernathy said in the bond. *Tell your mum.* And then, plainer: *It bowed to me. Twice. And I didn't put the coat on. It was just there, and it fit.*

Arenya said it to her mother. Not the part about the coat. Only the laugh.

Amma lifted her cheek out of the marigolds. "Laughing," she said, as if confirming an order. "Yes. She always laughed at the wrong time."

Down the row, Caleb stood at the edge of the candles, facing the road, the crushed coffee cup in his fist. "Northeast," he said to nobody. "Bay shore, industrial. Drop Two is a Peninsula lot with a roll-up door. I don't know that it's the same place. I'm telling you now, before you decide whether to believe me."

Arenya borrowed Ava's pen and wrote *NE · bay shore · pulling* on the inside of her wrist, small, where the sleeve would cover it.

Appa took off his glasses and wiped them on his shirt. His left hand came out of his pocket. In the candlelight the stain on his palm had shrunk to a crescent no wider than a fingernail paring.

They walked down section L together in the dusk, the living first. Behind them the lit candles stood straight. Across Mission Road the fog breathed on its count, eleven seconds, slow, like a big animal after a run, and stayed on its own side of the street.

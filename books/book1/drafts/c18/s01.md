"Eleven again," Arenya said to the kitchen. "Nobody say it's a coincidence. I'll pay you a dollar."

Nobody took the dollar.

It was 7:40 on Monday morning and the Heron Thing was on her phone for the third time. Two million views by now. A jogger's shaky footage of the Candlestick mudflat, and a bird that was too tall by half, standing in four inches of water with its neck folded like a coat on a door. It turned its whole body toward Hunters Point. The water around its legs went glass-flat.

She counted. Eleven seconds. Then it was a heron again and it lifted away, embarrassed, as if it had been caught.

She screenshotted it. She time-stamped it. She wrote nothing down.

Across the table Kino stood with his coffee going cold and his left sleeve pulled down. "Six fifty-two," he told Ava, who had her pen out before she'd finished asking. "Warm like a cup somebody just set on it. It didn't fade when the bird lifted. It faded when the water moved again."

Ava wrote *6:52–6:53, L forearm, offset to water, not to bird* and underlined *not* twice.

"It turned the way I'd turn if I were told where to go," Kino said, to nobody. "It's not hiding. It's counting out loud."

On the counter by the toaster, Abernathy sat very still, and for half a beat he was also in the doorway. *Same stroke,* he said, in the voice that only she could hear. *The waterline one. I know that stroke, love. I can't tell you from where, and I'd thank the universe to stop making me try.*

She didn't answer him. She reached for the toast.

* * *

Nana took the salt question over a bowl of corn husks, without looking up.

"Manners," she said. "You leave salt for *company*, mija. Now it knows it's welcome." A husk split clean down the middle. "Don't leave more unless you mean it."

Kino set the full shaker on the windowsill. He didn't open it.

Appa came in from the garage at 8:20 holding his phone away from his face as if it were hot. A man he'd trained at PG&E in '09 had texted from the Hudson substation. *Meters on Hudson Ave all ticked back 11 sec at 6:52. also your block. sorry to bother you sir.* Appa took the envelope off the fridge, the one with *11* on the back, and wrote *6:52 HUDSON + HOME* underneath in his engineer's capitals. He pinned it up again with the magnet.

He didn't say anything to Malachi. He only kept the envelope.

Malachi's phone had come back on at 8:05. Three missed calls from a 703 number. A CHP auto-reply on his query: *RECORD CLOSED: LAST CONTACT NONE.* A memo from someone named Corinne: *Bring the girl's file or bring a reason.*

"Someone was quick," he said to the table, level, "or someone is tidy. Either way, they saw me ask before they buried it." He turned the phone face-down beside his plate. "Tuesday at one I meet my handler. I'll tell her nothing that gets this house on a list. If I'm late, or I stop answering, assume I chose it. Assume the reason was good."

"Keep those numbers, Appa," he added. "Every one. They're evidence of something. I'd like it to be yours."

At 8:50 her work email chimed twice.

The first was from Elias Khatri. *Mr. Quinn asked me to find you. He'd like the coffee. Crissy Field, the warming hut, Wednesday 6:40 a.m. He'll be the one who's early.*

The second came a minute later, from a personal Gmail with no name. *I'd bring someone. — E.*

She read it three times. *I'd bring someone.* A man who says that knows the someone is a precaution, and he's telling you he knows. She didn't reply from the work address. Everything typed there was findable.

* * *

Teddy's glass office smelled like toner and somebody's banana.

Legal had come and gone. The letter to the health department was answered; the cloned keycard had been "handled, don't ask." He rubbed his eyes with the heels of both hands.

"I'm not firing you," he said. "I'm doing worse. You're on the waterfront. Bayview, Hunters Point, the Heron Thing, whatever's making the Bay act like it drank something. Six hundred words, Tuesday, five o'clock. Say yes, or clean out the desk."

She had worn the blazer. She kept her chin up.

"Yes," she said. "And Teddy? Thank you for doing worse." A beat. "I'll still take the desk, though. For the poodles."

He didn't laugh. He slid a keycard across the blotter, white, no photo. Then he said, not looking at her, "Your old card pinged the Monolith lobby reader on Saturday. Twice." He shut the drawer, softly. "It was in here. The whole time."

The doorframe, when she put her hand flat against it on the way out, was cold and quiet.

She took that as the good outcome.

On the Montgomery platform she wrote to Elias from her own phone, after she'd texted Malachi first. *Yes. I'll bring someone.*

The answer arrived before the train.

*6:40. The warming hut. He'll be early. He's always early when he's scared. — E.*

* * *

At 11:58 Caleb was carrying the second folding chair into the front room, for the ofrenda, because he'd asked Nana if he could carry something. He stopped in the doorway with it.

The landline in the hall was ringing. The same tone as Saturday.

This time it quit on its own after four rings, and Nana's old answering machine clicked and whirred. A man's voice, thin, from a car with the window down. Colma traffic behind it.

*"Raghavan, it's Rodel. Rodel from the counter."* A long breath. *"I didn't sell anything of yours. I want you to know that. But the pandit came by the shop this morning with a man in a grey suit. The man paid him, and the pandit gave him a page out of the book. And then he bought a bag of oranges from the stand outside, like nothing happened."* The voice dropped. *"Oranges. Who buys oranges after that? I'm sorry. I'm sorry. Tell your guest."*

Click.

In the kitchen, Malachi's cup was halfway to his mouth.

Arenya got to the hall table before the machine finished rewinding. She didn't touch the tape. She laid her phone flat beside it and made Appa play it back, so that there would be a recording that was hers. Then she crossed to the kitchen, took the cup out of Malachi's hand, and set it down, because it was about to end up on the floor.

"Dalal sold a page," she said, low. "Yours. Or the blank one. Either way a man in a grey suit paid for it, and Rodel is scared enough to call a house he swore he'd never call." She looked at him, not unkindly. "Don't leave. Don't go find the pandit alone. You said you'd choose your risks out loud. Choose this one out loud."

He didn't drink. He put the cup back into the ring it had already left on the table, exactly, the way a man puts a thing back in a room he's searched.

"Eleven fifty-eight," he said, and checked the stove clock to be sure.

"Grey suit," Caleb said from the doorway, flat. He stopped. Went on anyway. "Hale wears grey suits. Dorsey doesn't. Pruitt doesn't. I don't know it's him. I know he'd be the one who pays a man and then buys fruit. He likes things to look ordinary." And to Malachi: "Whoever has that page doesn't need to find you. They need you to move. So don't move because of a phone call. Move when Kino says."

"Oranges," Malachi said slowly, "you bring when you've taken something from a priest. Whoever he is, he was raised properly. That's worse news than if he weren't." He nodded toward the hall. "I'll still drive to Colma. But I park a block down, and I'm the last one in the car and the first one out. If someone has bought my name, the cheapest place to use it is wherever I'm standing. I'd rather that not be next to her."

Ava, from the hall doorway, had written *11:58 x2* on her palm in pen. "Same minute my oven went east," she said. "I don't believe in that. So I'm writing it down so I don't have to." She photographed the machine's display with Appa's permission. *1 MESSAGE · MON 11:58.* Counter at 0047.

Kino knelt under the hall window and laid a line of salt, corner to corner, so thin it looked like light on the sill. He said something under his breath in Nana's old words. "Nobody here is selling you, Rodel," he told the dead tape. "We heard you."

Appa popped the cassette out with a thumbnail. He wrapped it in a clean dish towel and put it in the freezer behind the frozen peas, the way his mother had kept rupee notes. "Nobody records over anything," he said. On the envelope, under *6:52 HUDSON + HOME*, he printed *ORANGES* and pressed the magnet down hard.

*Eleven ticks,* Abernathy said, at her shoulder, so quietly she nearly missed it. *Under his voice. A turn signal, love. Eleven, and then the lane change. It was the only part of that call that wasn't scared.*

* * *

By one o'clock the ofrenda was built.

Marigolds from the Mission. Pan de muerto. A glass of water. Nana's comal lamp. And the photograph of Susmithaa at nineteen, laughing at someone just out of frame. The canister sat before it on a folded white cloth, set down and not handed, exactly as the rite said.

Arenya stood in the front-room door and looked at it for a long time.

The house went quiet the way houses do before a funeral. Outside, the fog breathed. Eleven seconds in, eleven seconds out. And the marigold petals stirred in time.

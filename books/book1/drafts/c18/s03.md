Low tide was at 7:31, and Kino knew it the way other people knew their own phone numbers.

He stood up in the middle of a sentence. He held his left forearm away from his body like he was carrying something hot. "She wants me at the water," he said, and nobody voted.

Ava drove, because hers was the only car with heat. Caleb sat in the back and didn't say a word about Hunters Point until they passed under it.

The lot at Candlestick was empty, with one light working out of six. Past the riprap the Bay had pulled back a quarter mile and left a plain of black mud with a moon on it.

That was normal. The direction wasn't. The water wasn't going *out*. It was sliding sideways, one long sheet of it, northeast along the shore toward the cranes, as though someone up there had a hand on the tablecloth.

Gulls stood on the mud and faced that way. The eelgrass lay combed flat.

Arenya sat on the riprap with Ava's scarf double-wrapped over her wrist. The skin under it had gone the waxy white of a candle. She looked at the heaped grey-green thing out on the flat, the size of a parked bus, bent over with both arms braced in the mud.

Every eleven seconds it lost an inch. The mud under it groaned.

"It's not pulling," she said. "It's holding. Something up at the cranes is pulling, and she's the only one leaning back."

She split the night the way she'd split a story. Ava kept the bodies, the phone, the stopwatch. Caleb took the road. "If anything white with a roll-up door shows up, you tell us loud."

"I'm at twenty-three, no prana," she told Kino. "I'm not fighting anything. I'm going to say hello with manners."

"Stay behind me on the stair," Kino said, in the voice he used for animals. "I'll be the jaguar. You be the one who talks."

"Abernathy," she said. "Don't bow to anything."

"Bow? Not a chance, your Ladyship." He was already over, hat in hand, the wind not touching his coat. "Though that one's got her heels dug in like a dockhand in a strike."

Ava read the first number aloud like a case report. "7:25. Waterline moving northeast, brisk walk. I'm logging it."

Arenya closed her eyes and stepped over.

* * *

The stone went cold, then went quiet.

On the other side, the riprap was a stair of drowned steps running down to a shore that wasn't the shore. The bay had been drawn back like a bedsheet, a quarter mile of mud lit from underneath, the blue of a phone screen in a dark room. Every eleven seconds the whole flat *flexed*, and every rivulet and eelgrass blade and gull bone slid an inch northeast.

The cranes were ledger-pens now. Tall, black, nibs down. Something under them was drinking.

The woman on the mud was the idea of one, from before the shipyards came: a barrow of oyster shell, eelgrass hair, the soft grey clay that used to be the whole south shore. Lines ran out of her. Dozens. They weren't rope. They were *straight* water, ruled flat like lines on a page, hooked into her shell-skin and drawn tight toward the pens.

She opened one eye. A tidepool with a crab in it.

*"Land's child. You came down the stair asking. Good boy."* The eye slid to Arenya's wrist. *"And the one who sent my brother home with manners. He came back bowing. I haven't seen a bow in a hundred years."*

Then it found Abernathy and stayed a beat too long.

*"…And a filed man, without his ledger."*

One line near her shoulder hummed like a plucked wire and snapped tighter. She hissed and slid. The mud tugged at Arenya's ankles too.

*"They're writing me down. Every tide, line by line. When they've written all of me, I go where the pens go."*

Arenya stopped a polite distance up the stair, where the mud couldn't take her, and did not bow. She tilted her head, the way you do to a witness who's been talking to the wrong people for a hundred years.

"Ma'am. Arenya Sowri. I'm the one who sent your brother home. I told him an entry's open, and open means asking." She held up the candle-white wrist so there was nothing hidden. "I'm not here to take anything off you. What's pulling you? Who's holding the pens? Names, a place, what they want. I'll write it down and I'll say it where it counts."

She lowered her voice. "And if you can't say it, tell me what you can. I'll work with a corner."

Kino didn't wait. He went down onto the mud beside the old woman's shoulder, set his back against the nearest taut line like he was bracing a door, and said something low in the old tongue Nana used for the Lady. *Aquí, abuela. Aquí estoy.*

He wasn't trying to cut anything. He was trying to buy her an inch.

For half a second the line bowed.

Then it *read* him.

The ruled water didn't snap. It turned, smooth as a page being flipped, and laid itself across his forearm, right over the warm place Nana had taught him to listen to. Kino made a sound Arenya had never heard from him. A thin straight line sat on the lit skin of his arm. Beside it, a second, fainter one began.

*"Off, child,"* the old woman said. *"They like a living hand. They've never had one that was land's."*

Then, because Arenya had asked properly and nobody had in a century, she answered.

*"Not a name. They don't give names, they take them. But a place. Under the dry dock where the ships used to lie down, there is a stone house that grinds. A mill. My rivers go in at the top and come out at the bottom as nothing."* The eelgrass hair stirred. *"The pens aren't the house. The pens are* stakes. *Iron, in the real mud, on your side. Men put them in at low water. A white door on wheels comes and stands by them and hums, and each tide another line is ruled. Pull a stake and a line comes loose. Pull enough and I can stand up."*

Arenya wrote it on her other palm in the astral equivalent of Ava's pen, under the old ink. *MILL under dry dock. STAKES. Low water. White door on wheels.*

The tidepool eye went to Abernathy again, and the voice dropped almost to gentle. *"That hand on the lines is older than the yard, filed man. Don't strain your eyes. Somebody tore your page on purpose."*

Abernathy said nothing. His hat came down over his face.

"Kino," Arenya said. "Let go. Now."

"Give me one more second." Through his teeth. He gripped the line with his other hand and wrenched. It held, patient the way paperwork is, and the second stroke started to draw.

"Oi! Page-turner! Over here!"

Abernathy slapped a thumbnail of lamplight across the thin place where the stroke began. He wasn't trying to cut it. He only wanted it to look at *him*.

The line stopped.

It didn't flinch or break. It *paused*, the way a clerk's pen hovers when a name on the page turns out to be one he knows. The ruled water lifted off Kino's arm and pointed, very slightly, at Abernathy. The nib hung in the air between them, considering.

Arenya didn't ask what it meant. She took Kino's good wrist in her candle-white hand and hauled, and the stair took them up the way it always did, and the mud let go of his ankles with a sound like a boot leaving clay.

Behind them the line whipped back and sank into the old woman. But in that one hesitation, she'd gained an inch. The first anyone had seen her win.

*"One more tide,"* she called after them. *"With manners."*

* * *

They came up into cold car air and dash glow and Ava's fingers on Arenya's pulse.

Two hundred yards up Harney, a box truck sat dark with its engine running.

"Right forearm," Ava said, flat. "Linear erythema, onset 7:29, no contact." She'd photographed it already. On Kino's arm, over the lit skin, one stroke ran straight and clean. It wasn't bleeding. She laid a ruler beside it. Four centimeters. Exactly.

Abernathy was perched on the dash like a bad air freshener, touching his hat brim. A thin wet line was ruled across it that hadn't been there before.

*That nib knew me,* he said in the bond, very quietly. *It hung there looking for my page. I'd have liked it better if it'd just bitten. Don't ask me yet, love.*

At 7:31 the cab doors opened together.

Two people got out. Hi-vis vests, no logo. Headlamps off. They weren't hurrying and they didn't look around the way people do when they've stopped somewhere they shouldn't. Ava's phone caught them at the edge of the one working streetlight. Grey steel-toe boots, laced high, both pairs. Clean.

One walked to the back and put a hand on the roll-up door. White. It lifted two feet, just enough to let something breathe out.

Caleb's hand went to the scar at his crown. "Warm," he said, barely moving his lips. "A hand, not a voice." His count had snapped exactly onto the tide. "Somebody set my watch."

"Stay in the car," Arenya whispered. "Nobody walks toward them. Nobody gets heroic."

The second one pulled a survey rod from the truck bed, grey fiberglass, a cable running back into the box. They walked past the mouth of the lot without breaking stride and went down the riprap with the feet of people who'd done it in the dark before. Kino's arm ticked once per pulse. "Left of the second piling," he whispered. "Mark it."

The rod went into the mud there. Every phone in the car flickered. The dash clock skipped a digit. Kino's forearm gave one hard tick, like a pen pressing through to the next page.

Out on the flat, where nobody was over to see it, an old woman lost the inch she'd just won.

Ava got the plate: California commercial, white on blue, *8ZK-41*. Under it, small, stenciled the way fleet trucks do it: **D2-07**.

Caleb's hand stopped shaking for about a second. That was worse.

On the way back, one of them stopped. He looked straight at the car. He didn't squint, because he already knew it was occupied. Forty, clean-shaven, a Safeway-manager face. He lifted a hand in a little wave, the kind you give a neighbor, and came over and stopped a polite six feet from Ava's window.

"Evening, folks. Sorry to bother you. This lot's port property after dusk, so you'll want to head out before security comes round." His eyes went once to Kino's sleeve. "And keep that arm warm, son. Tide's cold tonight."

He tapped the roof twice. The same two taps he'd given the tablet.

Behind him the roll-up door rattled down, and the warmth left Caleb's scar like a hand being lifted.

"Evening," Ava said through the crack, in the voice she used on men who wanted their drink free. "Thanks. We're leaving."

Arenya raised the inked hand from the dash and gave his back a small pleasant wave through the glass. She didn't let him see her face. "Drive. Lights on at Jamestown, not before. Let them watch us leave like people who got told."

Her voice shook and she let it. "He knew about the arm before he saw it. He's not security. He's a clerk."

"D2 is Drop Two," Caleb said to the windshield. "It's a fleet number. I told you it was a lot with a roll-up door." He pulled the grey jacket tighter. "That's a stamp, the roof. We've been entered. Go slow. Don't give him a reason to write a second line." A pause. "Tomorrow I tell Malachi everything I know about D2. Not the guess. What I saw."

In the mirror the truck sat idling with its lights off. Nobody got out to watch them go. That was the part that stayed with her. It didn't need to watch. It had already written the car down.

At Jamestown the headlights came on and the world was suddenly ordinary: a Walgreens sign, a 29 bus, a man in a Niners jacket walking a pit bull. Ava turned the heat up until the windows sweated.

Somewhere around the Daly City line, Caleb's count slipped half a beat off the tide and stayed there, ragged and a little wrong.

It was his again.

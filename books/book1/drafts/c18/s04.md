At 11:11 on Monday night, a weather nerd posted that every tide gauge in the Bay had read flat for eleven seconds.

*Sensor glitch??*

Ninety-one replies. Nobody had an answer. Arenya read all of them on Ava's couch, then put the phone face-down on her chest and slept anyway, which felt like a betrayal of something.

At 6:58 a.m., Ava opened her front door for the paper and said, "Okay, no."

A rope of bull kelp lay on the welcome mat. Wet. Tied in a careful, deliberate knot. No sand on the steps. No footprints. Nobody had walked it up.

Arenya crouched beside it in yesterday's sweatshirt and did not touch it. The knot moved.

*Tick.*

*Tick.*

*Tick.*

"Three," she said out loud, because counting was what they did now. "Not eleven."

Behind her ear, Abernathy had gone very still.

She photographed it next to her pen for scale. She slid a takeout menu under it and carried it to Ava's sink like a patient. "Ava, I'm sorry. Your doorstep is now a primary source."

* * *

*Brass, love,* said Abernathy, in the bond. *And paper. Old paper, oiled metal, like the inside of a clock in a reading room.* A pause. *Don't look at me like that. I'd rather it were regret.*

*What does that mean?*

*It smells like somewhere I've worked, guv. I'd swear it on me hat. I can't tell you where, and that's the bit I don't like.* The cockney had gone thin at the edges. *Salt and rot is the sea's. The brass and the library is somebody's shop. Somebody dressed that knot, or the sea borrowed it off somebody.*

*Friend or receipt?*

*Three ticks, not eleven. It ain't the ledger's count. Could be a friend. Could be someone wanting me to think so.* Then, briskly, to cover it: *Don't pick it up bare-handed. And eat something, your Ladyship. You're giving orders on an empty stomach.*

At the sink, Ava was already in dish gloves with a ruler. She tapped the counter twice. Waited a full minute by the stopwatch. The kelp did nothing.

Then she leaned over to write the result down, her gloved hand hovering above the knot, and it ticked three times, slow and even, like a clerk confirming a signature.

To her. Not to the table. Arenya's palm stayed cool.

"Data," Ava said, not lightly, and wrote it down. She bagged the kelp in a gallon Ziploc, labeled it *Tues 11/3, 6:58, front mat*, and put it in the fridge. Nana's rule: you don't throw out a gift. Inside the bag, the plastic fogged from the inside.

"It came to my door," Ava said. "Not yours. Whoever sent it knows which one of us drives."

Arenya texted Malachi from the couch: *Plate 8ZK-41, unit D2-07, two clerks in grey steel-toes, video from Ava. Stakes are iron, in real mud at Hunters Point, set at low water. Tell Corinne a fleet number is a paper trail. Eat something.*

Then she sat down at Ava's desk and wrote Teddy's six hundred words. The tide gauges that read flat for eleven seconds. The Hunters Point cranes. Port-property signs after dusk. An unmarked fleet truck, idling.

Facts and timestamps. She left out the kelp, the tide-woman, and Kino's arm.

Witnesses are people, not quotes.

* * *

The pho shop on Noriega had sweating windows and a TV over the register playing the Colma fog clip with the sound off.

Malachi took the seat facing the door. Corinne Abate was in the back booth with her coat on and a broth she wasn't eating, and when she saw Caleb come in behind him, her face did nothing at all. He found that he respected it.

"Thank you for the coat," he said. "It tells me you're staying for less than the broth." He laid his phone face-down and slid it an inch toward her. "Before anything else. Who said my name, and where? Exact words."

She didn't take out her phone. She had them by heart.

"Sunday night, a broker's board we pay to read. *True name, one asset, Bay Area, consular-adjacent, verified by a woman who keeps names. Sold. Buyer pays in water rights.*" She took off her glasses. "Water rights, Malachi. Nobody who means money writes that. And then your old file pinged. Your real one, from before us. Nobody here typed it."

Caleb spoke next, hands flat on the table, and marked every place where he stopped. The white roll-up door. The plate. The two men in grey steel-toes. The tap on the roof, twice, like a stamp. He set an envelope on the table with *D2-07* written down it forty times.

Corinne turned it face-down, the way you close a stranger's diary. Then she slid a folded printout across: a California filing. *Bayline Remediation & Survey, LLC.* Brisbane. Registered agent, a law firm on Sand Hill Road. One line highlighted: *subcontractor, environmental monitoring, Hunters Point lease parcel B. Prime: Monolith Systems.*

"Eleven box trucks. D-series. Your D2-07 is theirs. Their yard's off Tunnel Avenue, right on the county line. Roll-up doors." She looked at Caleb's hands, not his face. "Which you knew."

"Tunnel Avenue," Caleb said, flat. "Nose-out, so nobody reverses on a bad night. The doors hum on a tone. You feel it in your teeth, or in a Crown." A beat. "Drop Two's a Peninsula lot. Not that yard."

Then the price, delivered pleasantly. "Who's the girl? The one in the fog clip who isn't in the fog clip. Somebody bought your name the same week you stopped answering my calls. I don't believe in weeks like that." She put her glasses back on. "I can pull you tonight, a flight and a new badge. Or you give me her name and she goes in a file I control instead of one I don't. Those are the doors I have."

Malachi let the silence sit. On the TV, the hill, the candles, a white blur where a heron would be if cameras could see it.

"No flight. Somebody paid in water rights for my name the same week a Monolith sub started staking the Bay. If I get on a plane, they learn what my name buys. I would rather they learn it buys nothing."

He squared the envelope beside her printout. *D2-07* lined up next to *parcel B*.

"The girl isn't a girl, and she isn't yours to file. I'm running her as an access agent. Opened in March, recruited last week, and slow to paper, because the first file I wrote on this kind of thing came back telling me to forget it. You remember that file, Corinne. Or you know someone who does." He held her eyes. "You get her product. Not her name. Starting with this. She's the reason you have a fleet number at all."

Corinne read him from the bottom up, like a cable. Most of what he'd said was true, and the true parts carried the rest.

"Access agent." She tried the phrase. She tucked the printout under Caleb's envelope, so that both were his now. "Fine. Tuesday the tenth, a product on my desk: the yard, the lease, faces. If it's late, I pull you whether you say yes or not." She dropped a twenty for the broth. "And the ping on your old file didn't come from Brisbane. It came from a terminal with our own access. Until I know whose, don't call the 703 number." A paper napkin, a number in pencil, no name.

At the door she looked at Caleb last, at the left pocket he wasn't touching. "A wall is for keeping track of people who might come back," she said, not unkindly. "Don't."

Then she was gone into the fog on Judah.

* * *

Teddy read the six hundred words at 4:52 and sent back one line: *Clean. Running it 6 a.m. Who's the truck?*

*Fleet truck,* Arenya typed. *Bayline Remediation & Survey, Monolith's sub. More Thursday, I promise.* She switched the phone to silent, which was the closest thing she owned to a ward.

Low water at Hunters Point: 8:14 p.m., minus three-tenths of a foot. The lowest of the week. Nobody said out loud that pulling a stake set by a company with eleven trucks was a choice. It sat in the car anyway, like a fifth passenger.

Bolt cutters across her lap like a cello. Kino's pliers and pry bar behind her. Caleb's duct tape on the slippery handles. "If I reach for it tonight," he said, before they left the curb, "say my name." The kelp rode on the dash in its bag, ticking at nobody.

* * *

At 5:52, on 101 at Cesar Chavez, the dash clock read 5:52. Then 5:54. Then 5:58.

The tide table refreshed itself. *In 2h 03m.* Then *1h 56m.* Then *1h 41m.* Arenya's pulse jumped to match. Every heart in the car did it: *late, late, late.*

Then she saw it, and a beat later Kino felt it on the back of his neck. Not a car. The *lanes*. A shape the length of the interchange ran along the traffic like heat shimmer made of taillights, a hundred thousand commutes' worth of *I should have left ten minutes ago* poured into one long body. It had no head. It had a front, and the front was wherever the gap was.

It wanted the gap in front of Ava's bumper.

Ahead, a white Tacoma braked for nothing. Phantom brake lights bloomed across four lanes at once. On the dash the kelp ticked fast, five, six, seven, like a meter.

"No," Ava said to the car. "That's not a clock. That's a symptom."

She held fifty-one. She left the gap open and started her own stopwatch aloud, like a vitals check. "Elapsed, twelve minutes forty. The dash is lying. The tide table is lying. Your pulse is lying, Arenya, I can see it in your neck. Breathe on my count, not its count." She ticked two-second beats on the wheel with her thumb, slow and boring and stubborn. "We are exactly as late as we are. Not one second more."

In the back, Caleb put two fingers on his wrist and tapped just behind her thumb. "I'll hold the off-beat." The car had two slow rhythms now, and neither of them was hurry's. Kino cracked the window two inches and breathed out through his teeth, the sound he made for deer on the Panoramic Highway. "Easy, old one. Nobody's stealing your gap. Go around."

Over the roof, in the bulk, Abernathy stepped out into the queue.

It was an endless line of people in coats, each tapping a watch, each sure they were next. The brass at his collar went cold and officious. He didn't mean it to. "Evenin', ladies and gents. Ticket, please. No? Then you're not next." He tipped the hat. The nearest coats flinched. *Arenya, I'm giving you the clean lane. Look at the Tacoma and nothing past it. And don't ask me where I learned that. I'm not sayin' I like it.*

She let the lamp come up behind her eyes, and the freeway went see-through.

The front wasn't going south. All of it, every lane, was being *drawn*, the way water leans toward a drain. At the Bayshore split its front bent east, toward Hunters Point, toward a dark stack, and thin bright threads of hurry peeled off the traffic and ran downhill in the bulk, into the shipyard, like rain into a gutter.

"It doesn't want us," she said, flat and slow. "It wants the gap. Right lane, three cars up. The Tacoma's about to brake again. Don't fill the space. Let the gap be the bait."

The phantom lights went out all at once. The Tacoma sped up, embarrassed. The dash clock settled at 5:53, and the kelp ticked once, slow, like a meter shutting off.

"Something at the Mill isn't just drinking the tide," Arenya said. "It's drinking the city's hurry."

Nobody answered. Ava took the Third Street exit with time to spare.

"Seven-forty," she said. "Eight-fourteen is ours. We're not late. We're on the tide."

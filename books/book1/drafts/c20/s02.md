# The Envoy

Saturday, 6:12 a.m. The fog sat on Ocean Beach like a lid somebody had forgotten to lift. Salt in my jacket pockets. Ollie asleep in the back of the car under Kino's hoodie, his Crown pressed against the seat. The creek mouth ran the colour of weak tea.

The tide-woman stood where the water met the sand. She was thinner than yesterday. She didn't look at us. She looked at Caleb.

"It came up in the night," she said. Her voice was the creek, small stones rolling over each other. "Not one of mine. Not one of the sea's. An envoy, sent ahead of something that wants a door. It has been asking the kelp about the Gate-talker. It has been asking *whose name opens it*."

Out past the break, the kelp had stopped moving with the swell. Seven strands, pulled tight, like a hand held out.

Something walked up the waterline toward us and left no prints. It was tall and wet-dark, in a coat that was mostly tide. It stopped ten paces off and bowed, too deep, the way a courtier bows to a king he's already planning to replace.

"Gate-talker," it said, in a voice made of many small voices taking turns. "I carry an offer. I would like to be *invited*. Only that. I'll be so much less trouble invited."

It smiled with a mouth that was not quite where a mouth should be.

I kept my notebook shut in my bag and my hand on the strap, not on the pen.

"Invited to what?" I said. "Give me the terms in a sentence I can write down." I looked at the coat, at the place where the mouth should have been, and not at the mouth. "And you asked the kelp whose name. Not mine. So don't tell me it wants something from me. Tell me whose it is, and whether that person is standing on this beach."

* * *

Malachi stayed two paces behind my shoulder with his hands loose and in plain sight. He didn't look at the coat. He watched the bow, how long it held, how the thing rose from it.

"Invited is a word people use when they want to skip the part where they say what they're bringing," he said. Level. The voice he'd use across a table from someone who'd already decided the outcome. "You asked whose name opens the door. Arenya has already told you it isn't hers to give. So it's not her you want, and it's not Kino's inch. Which leaves the man who's been standing at the edge of this since Thursday."

He shifted his weight, so the envoy had to turn to keep track of him.

"Here's the only offer you'll get from me. Nobody at this creek signs anything, speaks anyone's name, or opens anything on the say-so of a tide. If you want a yes, you'll take it from me, in front of the Gate-talker, with the terms written down. Not from the water."

He didn't reach for his phone. He let the silence sit where the envoy would have to step into it.

Abernathy stood at my left shoulder, where the fog was thinnest. He kept his hands in his coat. I could tell by the set of his shoulders that he was looking at the envoy the way you look at a dog that hasn't decided yet.

"Oi, guv," he murmured, low, for me. "Spirits don't get *invited* to a door, not really. They get asked in, and then they work out how big the house is. Don't say yes to anything that bows that low."

The cuff on his wrist would be cold. I didn't have to look to know.

* * *

Kino didn't step forward. He stayed on the flat stone where the sand gave way, salt in his left fist, the right hand open at his side.

"Nobody invites a door they can't shut," he said. The voice he used across the ridge at dawn: flat, unhurried. "So I'll ask it the way Nana taught me. Invited to what? Where does the door stand, and whose house is on the other side? Give me the Mill's address, or the name of the one who signs for it. You asked the kelp for a name. Answer me with one."

He kept his eyes on the coat. "And you don't get Caleb. He's standing where I can see him. That's the only thing you're getting from this beach until I hear the terms."

Ava had her pen out, not writing. Her lips moved. I knew she was counting.

"Before anybody invites anything," she said, "I want to know what time it is in your house." She kept her eyes on the hem, where the tide soaked into the cloth and didn't come out. "My phone says 6:12. The clocks on this coast have been running three hours fast since Wednesday, and I've got the numbers on the back of my hand to prove it. So tell me which clock you're keeping. If you say twelve, I'm walking back to the car."

The envoy turned its head toward her, slowly, the way a tide turns. No single moment you could point to.

"The sea keeps one clock," it said. Each word arrived wet, with no breath behind it. "It is not yours, and it is not the Mill's. Yours runs fast because something is pulling the hours toward Sunday night. Eleven-forty. That is the hour I keep, too."

Then it faced me, and the bow came again, shallower.

"Not your name. Not the Gate-talker's inch. *His.*" The sleeve lifted toward the sand behind Kino, toward Caleb, and stopped a hand's width short of pointing. "No. Not that one. The one he walks behind. The man who crossed the platform. He carries the pitch the throat is missing. He can't say it into the dark himself. A name spoken by its owner is only a sound. It must be spoken *for* him, by someone holding a door."

Against Abernathy's wrist the cuff went cold as river stone. The hem dragged through the foam, and for one second the tide in it ran upstream, toward the city.

"Terms," the envoy said, to my notebook. "One door. One breath. One name, which I will give you, and you will not like who it belongs to. In return: the route of the box, and the hour the Chair's keeper looks away. Write it down. I will wait until the sun clears the ridge."

Far up the creek, something very large shifted its weight in the water.

I opened the notebook on my forearm. I didn't look at the water while I did it. My hand was steadier than I felt.

"Okay. Terms. I'll write them, because I'm the one who has to read them back." I clicked the pen. "One door, one breath, one name. I want the name before I write a word of the rest. And when I hold the breath, where does it come from? Out of me? Out of whoever's holding? I've been counting my own breaths all week. I'm not handing one over blind."

I wrote *ELEVEN-FORTY* in block capitals and underlined it twice. The pen didn't skip.

"The route of the box. The lane, the dock, the carrier. Not *the city*. And the hour the Chair's keeper looks away is a time, not a feeling. If you're keeping eleven-forty, tell me who's keeping the Chair at eleven-forty."

I didn't say yes. I didn't say no. I tapped the pen on the page and waited.

Abernathy leaned in, close, the way you'd give advice across a pub table. "Ask it what the breath costs *before* you write the lane down, love." Then, in his own voice, the one he uses when the patter's run out: "You said *he* carries the pitch. Which one's he? Don't point. Say the word."

* * *

Ava laid her hand flat on her notebook. "I'm the medic here, so I'm going to ask the boring questions. If somebody holds a door for one breath, what do I see on them? Heart rate, colour, whether they stay upright. Give me the failure mode. Is it a minute, or a year, or a person who stops being able to say their own name? I want numbers in a sentence. Not a feeling."

The envoy answered in order, because she had asked for order.

"Whose breath: the holder's. Drawn in at the door, and not let out until the name is spoken. A living pulse drops to a sleeper's. The lips go the colour of the inside of a shell. Afterward the holder cannot say their own name until the next tide turns. A dozen hours. No longer. I do not lie about cost. It is the only thing I own." A pause the length of a swell. "Your pulse is quick, medic. I hear it. It will be quicker."

To Malachi: "Not the Mill's hand. Not the Chair's. The one the creek runs *to*. It has a long way to come, and a short time to do it." To Abernathy it said nothing. The cuff, however, went colder.

To Kino: "The Lady's house is awake. I know. That is why I came down gently."

Then the sleeve lifted, and this time it did not stop. It pointed at Caleb.

"You asked what the door does once it is open. Good. It lets the walker *finish*. He has been crossing one platform for two days, and every crossing costs the city a few. You, who said a name at a fence and were answered: names come when you call them. I did not ask for you. I am telling you what you already are." The voice dropped, almost kind. "I will not give the name unless a holder is chosen. That is not cruelty. It is the rule of the water."

* * *

Caleb's hands were in his pockets because they wouldn't keep still. His left one was closed around the dropper. He didn't take it out.

The sleeve stopped a hand's width from him. He didn't step back. A cold line ran down the back of his neck, under the scar, and the scar answered with a warm pull he could feel in his teeth.

"Nobody holds a breath for me," he said. It came out hoarse, and he let it. "Not you. Not her. Not Arenya. I've had people hold things for me. I know what they cost by the time the bill comes."

He looked at the place on the coat where a face would be, and didn't look away.

"You say I carry the pitch. Fine. Then it's mine to say. You don't say it for me, in my voice, down a drain. If I can't say it myself, you don't get it at all. Take that back up the creek with your terms."

The tremor climbed his forearm and he let it show. He said, to me, without turning: "Don't write my name down. Write that I said no."

* * *

Kino's refusal came first, and it was the one that held. "No door," he'd said. "Not tonight, not at eleven-forty, not for a name you won't say yourself. My house doesn't hold breath for strangers. Whoever keeps that clock can walk down here and say the terms to my face."

The tide in the envoy's hem stopped running upstream.

Then it bowed to Caleb, all the way down, the coat folding like wet paper. It held the bow while the first edge of sun cleared the ridge and laid a copper line across the creek.

"Then write that he said no," it said to my notebook. "Write that *he* said it. Not the Gate-talker, not the Chair-seeker, not the medic with the quick heart. The water keeps that. The water keeps very little."

It straightened. The tide was draining out of the cloth already. The coat was only a coat, hanging on nothing, and then not even that: a drift of kelp and a smell of cold iron on the sand.

Up the creek the grey shoulder sank without a ripple. But the seven strands at the mouth stayed upright after the tide went slack, and while we watched, an eighth rose from the sand beside them and knotted itself, loosely, around nothing.

Abernathy's cuff was warm again. I looked down at the notebook. Under *ELEVEN-FORTY*, in a hand I didn't recognise, in a hand like running water, someone had written: *He will be at the Chair's door first. Choose who is there.*

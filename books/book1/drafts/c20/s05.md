Ava kept the log the way other people kept a rosary. *20:40, Ollie asleep. 21:15, Caleb eats half a sandwich. 22:02, oven light flickers once and holds.* At 22:31 Typhus's text landed on Caleb's phone, one line: *In. Role log open. Last entry is a signature field with a name pre-typed and no date. Checking whose.*

Then nothing, for twenty minutes, while the Mission studio ticked around us like a car engine cooling.

At 22:50 the receipt on my table went warm. I could see the heat in it. The thermal ink darkened the way it does when it's dying, and under the printed line a new row came up by itself, with no printer anywhere in the building.

**23:40. Side door. Someone holds it, or the chair keeps the hour.**

"Nobody holds a door on a clock somebody else wrote," Malachi said. He hadn't touched the paper. He stood at the window with his back to the room and watched the kerb in the glass. The streetlamp down there had gone the wrong colour. "The box moves in fifty minutes. We go look at the side of Building 12 with our eyes. Not our hands."

I lifted the receipt with a paper napkin by its very corner, like something off a crime-scene table in a true-crime podcast I'd make fun of. I folded it once and slid it into the back of my notebook, behind the page where I'd written *sign* and crossed it out.

"Fine," I said. "We look. But I'm not carrying this, and I'm not answering to my own name on it."

Abernathy was at my elbow before I reached the door, leaning on nothing, his coat not quite moving with the draught.

*Don't say yes to anything in that car, guv. Not the door, not the chair, not the bloke in the wrong-coloured light. I heard Malachi say "alternate." So did the cuff in L-3. It went warm on the word* sign *like a kettle that's heard its name.*

"You're very dramatic for a man who can't open a car door," I said.

*Pull your hand back if it goes toward your own name, love. I'll shout. You'll hate it.*

* * *

Kino took the stairwell with Ollie's back against his knee. Two fingers on the kid's wrist, counting out loud so Ava could write it down with the time.

The boy's pulse was thin and quick, like something small under a palm.

"Lucero," Kino said, through the stairwell door, to me. "Don't say it back, not even as a joke. Whoever reads a name like that aloud wants the room to hear it. Nobody in this room wants that."

Then, lower, the way you talk to a kid at dusk on a trail that's gone too quiet: "You did the thing you said you'd do. You told them no. Breathe with me. Four in."

He kept his phone face up. One text open to his grandmother, half typed. *¿Estás despierta?* He didn't send it. If the line went dark, he wanted her to be the first person he could still reach.

* * *

Pier 70 at night is a long shelf of drydock cranes and brick sheds, the bay black behind them. One sodium lamp over Illinois Street burned the same wrong colour as my kerb. Malachi took the car in nose-out beside a shuttered rigging yard and killed the lights.

Sixty metres away, Building 12's side wall. One grey steel door, a caged bulb above it, dark.

Two things were already there. A beige sedan against the far fence, engine dead, windscreen fogged from inside. And below the door, where the concrete dipped toward a drain, a thin line of water running uphill, toward the wall.

At 23:31 the bulb lit itself. Under the door a seam of paler light appeared. From somewhere beneath the pier, through the car's floor and the soles of my shoes, the drydock throat hummed a single note, then deliberately left a gap where the next one should go.

In the beige windscreen, a hand wiped a clear circle in the fog. Eyes. Looking at us.

I didn't touch the handle. I kept both hands on my knees, where the dome light couldn't find them, and I read the sedan the way I'd read a bad restaurant review: plate first.

Three characters legible through the fog. *-41.* I wrote it in block capitals with the time, then put a question mark after it and underlined the question mark twice. I didn't say the rest out loud.

"Dalal-ji sold one page to a man in a grey suit," I told Malachi, barely moving my mouth, "and then he bought oranges. I don't think he sold it to the chair. I think he sold it to someone who wanted to know whose door it opened."

"Arenya." His voice was level. "Eyes on the dash. Not the windscreen."

I looked at the dash. It was the hardest thing I'd done since Thursday.

Then, to the glass, very quietly, in the voice I use on dogs who've decided the couch is theirs: "No."

I didn't say my name. I noticed I was proud of that, and I hated that I noticed.

* * *

At 23:37 the sedan door opened. Both hands first, palms out, like a driver at a traffic stop. A woman in a rain jacket, mid-forties, the lanyard tucked inside her collar so only the cord showed. She held up a thermal receipt between two fingers. Even at sixty metres I could read the top line: *c/o Arriaga, I.*

"I'm not Lucero," she called across the dark. Not loud. To the car, not to anyone in it. "I've never been Lucero. Mine says *Arriaga*. It's been in my mailbox since Friday, and the clock on it is the same clock." She swallowed. "I was told I'd be the one to read it aloud. I got here at eight so I'd have time to decide not to."

Under the steel door the seam of light widened a finger. The uphill water reached the threshold and went under it like a tide taking one step. The throat's note sounded once and left its gap, and the gap leaned, like a door somebody was holding.

Inés lifted the receipt to her mouth. Her lips shaped a word, silently, and stopped.

At 23:40 the caged bulb went out. The steel door swung inward on its own, onto lit space. Nothing stood in it.

I wrote it down the way she'd done it. *23:37 INÉS, NOT LUCERO. "ARRIAGA." 23:39 SEAM WIDENS. 23:40 LIGHT, NO ONE IN THE DOOR.*

"She stopped on a word," I said. "Her mouth made one and didn't say it. Is that door open for whoever's on the receipt, or for whoever's brave enough to read it?"

Malachi didn't reach for the door. "Ms. Arriaga. Stay where you are. Keep both hands where they are." Then, to me: "What did you decide not to do?"

Inés didn't step closer. "I decided not to read the second line." She tilted the receipt to the lamp without lifting it past her chest. "The bottom is a name. It's not for the door. It's for the water under the pier. If I'd said it at the threshold at 23:40, someone inside would be *let past looking*, and the Chair's keeper would look away for one count of sixty." Her jaw worked. "I ran the register for that room six years. Nobody I know has ever seen it unwatched. I don't want to be the reason it is."

She nodded at the dock end. "Loading bay three. The Mill's truck, D2-04, engine warm. Whoever's driving it has until 23:42 to pull out, or it becomes the door's problem."

Down the long wall, a roll-up door began to lift. One amber signal blinked, once.

I wrote **D2-04. BAY 3. ENGINE WARM. 23:42** and boxed it twice.

"Ms. Arriaga, don't say the bottom line. Not to the threshold, not to the water, not to anyone." I held my palm toward her, the way you stop traffic. "Fold the receipt. Put it on the hood, step back three paces. I'll come and get it when it's safe, and I'll read it to nobody but myself."

* * *

The truck left at 23:42. Malachi turned the key one notch, lights off, and let it go. Two lengths back, never closer.

I stopped reading the road and read the truck. Its brake lights flicked at the Illinois light, not for a signal, and it didn't take the route the Mill's dispatch sheet would give it. Each turn was one nobody would need to make on the way to a Mill site.

"It's taking the long way round the old rail spur," I said. "The one with no lights and nothing on the maps. Left at the crane footing. Right after the tank farm. Somebody built that road on purpose."

Arenya's roll carried us eleven blocks of dark. Malachi's kept us from being made for ten of them. At the tank farm the truck's brake lights flared, held, and went out, and we were seen anyway. Malachi's headlights were the only light on the spur.

The cab door opened. A big man with a trimmed beard stepped into our high beams with both hands at shoulder height. Pruitt. He didn't squint. He looked like someone who had done the arithmetic and didn't like the answer.

He hauled up the rear door. No crates. A steel-framed chair bolted to the floor, facing the door. On its seat a grey lockbox the size of a loaf, stencilled **L-3**. A thermal receipt taped to its lid, the ink still wet.

Pruitt raised one hand and rapped the truck's flank. Tap, pause, tap-tap.

"Caleb," he said, to the glass. "Nobody's driving this. I just got paid to park it. He said you'd come."

I didn't get out. I put the notebook face-down on my knee and leaned toward the windshield so the high beams showed my open hands.

"Mr. Pruitt. Who paid you to park it? And don't read me the receipt. I've read one this weekend. I don't want to be the one who says the second line."

I wrote **L-3 — RECEIPT ON LID — CHAIR BOLTED TO FLOOR — FACING THE DOOR**, underlined once. Then, smaller, the thing I couldn't stop thinking: *Everyone who's been told to sit in that chair has been told to face the door.*

Malachi killed the engine, left the headlights on, and stepped out into them with his jacket open and both hands visible. He stayed on the car's side of the light, the bumper between him and the truck.

"Read me your instructions," he said. "Not the receipt. And you don't sign anything in front of me tonight. Not a sheet, not a box, not a name."

Pruitt's eyes went to Malachi's, not his mouth. "A phone. A voice I didn't know, no name. Cash in a Mill envelope under the seat in bay three. Park it. Raise the door. Say Caleb's name once. Walk away down the spur, don't look back." He swallowed. "Four's gone. I don't take orders from it. I took these from something that sounded like it."

* * *

In the back seat, Caleb kept his hands flat on his knees. They didn't stay still. He pressed harder, and that didn't help either.

"Ninety-one," he said, to Malachi, one word.

Then, to the windshield: "That's Pruitt. He was on my crew until Thursday, and the Crown still has him on a leash. Don't let him near the lid. Don't let him touch the tape." His jaw locked on the chair. He made himself keep looking at it, because someone in this car had to.

Pruitt lowered one hand just far enough to set a pair of truck keys on the bumper. Then he walked down the dead track toward the black water and didn't hurry and didn't turn. The dark closed over him.

Out on the bay a bell buoy tolled once. For the first time all weekend, the tide was going out the right way.

The truck ticked as it cooled. The chair faced the open back door, bolted and patient. Under the work light, the receipt on the lid was legible from the bumper.

**L-3 · HELD FOR M-OPS (VACANT) · SIGN BY MON 06:00 · c/o LUCERO, I.**

And under it, in a different hand, in pen rather than thermal ink: *Alternate accepted.*

I looked at my watch. 23:56. Six hours.

Nobody signed. Nobody said the name. Nobody touched the box.

*Right, love,* Abernathy said, very low, from somewhere behind my left shoulder, where no one else could see him. *That's the first honest thing that chair's ever done. It's told us where to sit.*

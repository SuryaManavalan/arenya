At 3:10 a.m. Ollie Baptiste sits up all at once, the way a puppet stands when someone pulls the strings tight.

I'm in the kitchen doorway with Nana's tea going cold in my hands. I don't go closer.

His eyes are flat as poured lead. "Four. Report."

Nobody answers. His head turns toward Caleb with no hurry in it at all.

"You took one of mine. Keep him. I don't want him back. I want what was taken from me. Pryce sits in my office. Bring me Pryce's chair, and I'll let go of the boy."

The voice is Ollie's throat. Someone else is choosing the pauses.

I say it level, the way I'd read a source's quote back to her before she could take it back. "Ollie Baptiste is not Four. He's not yours to report. You want a chair? Then you don't get to say what it costs. Say your name, if you've got one. Nobody else in this room is hiding theirs."

Then Ollie folds over the mop bucket and retches, and when he lifts his head he's small again. "...who was talking?"

Under the floor, the water has gone very still.

* * *

Malachi doesn't kneel. He stands where the lamp throws his shadow across the coffee table, so whatever's in Ollie can see his hands are empty.

"Malachi," he says. "The card in my pocket says something else. You don't need to hear it. You don't get to pick who sits in anybody's chair."

At 6:02 his government login had already thrown a grey page that said *Access ended*. At the federal building's side door, the guard told him the badge was dead before the reader could. By 10:50 he was in a Denny's in Daly City with Corinne Abate, who slid him a printout without taking her coat off. An Advanced Facilities Research task order. The contractor was Monolith. The sponsoring billet was a deputy slot two rungs above her, blacked out in her own chain of command, and it shared a signature block with a Mill role Malachi had seen only in the margins of other people's files.

"Don't go in Monday," she'd said. "Copy your notes tonight. And keep the access agent off every page you hand anyone."

She meant me. She didn't have to say it.

* * *

By dawn Ollie is lucid enough to be ashamed. His pulse is one-twelve and thready, and he keeps counting backward by sevens the way Ava asked him to, and getting it wrong on purpose so she'll correct him.

Caleb has gone to the bottom of the hill. He's sitting on the team car's back bumper with the window down, and he won't come up the steps until Ava calls. The tremor is in his teeth now. He told me the withdrawal had a number on it, and the number had won the morning.

* * *

Ava doesn't look at the thing in Ollie's face. She looks at his lips.

*Ollie. It's Ava. You're on the couch at Nana's. Count back from a hundred by sevens for me. Out loud, even if it's wrong.*

She takes the pulse, the sip count, the breath, and she writes them on a chart in the flat, clean hand she uses for patients who can't tell her what's wrong. She doesn't call it a miracle. She calls it a trend.

Her phone shows a text from Typhus, sent to Caleb, forwarded to her by mistake: *Box asked for M-Ops at 21:41 last night. Who asks a box for a role?*

No question mark. He's reading it, somewhere, the way he reads everything.

* * *

At noon I call Teddy from Nana's porch.

"You'll have it by eight," I tell him. "Not a seep. A permit, a lease, a Monolith subcontractor, and a trucking yard with a plate. All on paper. And Teddy, I'm coming in tonight to tell you about the keycard myself. I'd rather you heard it from me."

There's a long exhale on the other end. "Eight. Not eight-oh-one."

I hate it. I say yes anyway.

Abernathy is on the porch rail, swinging one heel against the wood. "Good girl," he says, in a voice that's older than his accent. "Now eat the sandwich."

I eat the sandwich.

* * *

At 5:30 I'm at the Folsom office, at my old desk, with the lamp off and the bag on my knees. I'm writing the Bayline piece. The photos are on the screen, the lease is in the attachment, the plate number is in the second paragraph where it can't be cut.

The Lamp is warm in my bag. I leave my ringer on for Ava and for Typhus.

At 5:41 the lamp goes cold in the middle of a sentence.

I don't open the bag. I put both hands flat on the desk, on the notebook, and look at the canvas seam. Through it, a doorway is lit that wasn't a doorway a minute ago. It's wrong the way a photo is wrong when someone has cropped the wrong thing out. The frame is right. The depth isn't.

It moves while I watch. Not toward me. Along.

"Nobody asked for you," I tell the empty office, quiet. "Stay in the bag."

To Abernathy, because I can't take my eyes off the seam: "Is that the Missing Man, or the thing that walks behind him? Tell me which one I'm looking at before I touch the zipper."

"Don't touch the zipper, guv. Not yet." His voice comes up strange, a half-word older than the cockney. He's standing in two places for a frame, once at my shoulder and once at the lip of the light. "The tall one's the gap. The crowd walks round him so he don't hold a face. Nothing in him to bargain with. The one that walks behind him is the doorway, and it ain't after you. It's after what's left in the bag." He stops. "And I know that walk, love. I don't know from where. I won't pass a guess off as a fact."

He moves in front of my hands, close enough to feel, not touching. "Walk to the window. Slow. You're not running from it. You're just leaving the room."

I do. Behind me the seam stays lit for thirty seconds, then dims to ordinary Lamp-glow.

On every phone in the office, sixty people are walking into the bay.

* * *

Malachi, on Tunnel Avenue, keeps his eyes on the sedan's side mirror and the gate beyond it. He doesn't look at the water. He's read enough of the freeze files to know what a man does when he looks where he's told to look.

Eleven drivers in the line turn toward the bay at once. Most turn back inside a count of five.

One doesn't. The driver of the lead D-series, the man he's been reading for an hour, keeps his head turned toward the mirror. Plate 8ZK-41. Malachi doesn't look at him directly. He lets him see the shape of him in the glass, and he holds still.

He texts Corinne one line, no name: *Lead unit at Bayline gate, plate 8ZK-41, driver not turning back. Don't put that on paper yet.*

Then he gets out, hands open where the gate camera can see them, and walks the fence line toward the creek, slow, away from the shore.

* * *

Kino stands on Nana's back steps and doesn't turn toward the bay. The gulls on the roofline all face east, and none of them makes a sound. He counts them the way his grandmother taught him to count owls on the ridge: once, twice, then the one that isn't there.

"Lady," he says, to the mountain at his back. "Hold your ground. Nothing walks on your side of the house tonight."

The night-jaguar in his chest comes up onto its feet. It keeps its eyes on the gap where the crowd walks around nothing. He doesn't follow it with his own. You don't stare at a thing that has no face to hold.

He goes down the steps to Caleb's car, not fast, and opens the passenger door where the streetlight shows his hands. "Keep naming what you see. Hydrant. Bus shelter. You're doing it right."

At dusk he walks the creek mouth with Caleb at his shoulder, on the flat rocks where the tide has pulled back. The inch is still owed. The tide-woman has waited this long.

* * *

The water goes flat as a plate.

They feel her before they see her: a cold along the mud, like a hand laid on the back of a neck. She doesn't come up. She speaks from the inch of water over the stones, in a voice like a drain.

"*Not tonight, jaguar's son. Tonight the gap has walked my shore, and I owe it nothing, and you nothing. But hear me. Something is coming up my river wanting a door. It asked me for a name. I told it I'd ask the one who owes me. Come at dawn. Bring your Gate-talker. Bring salt. And bring the man with the shaking hands, because it has already asked about him.*"

The water moves again. The heron lifts off the piling. Across the inlet, every Bayline lamp comes on at once.

Caleb doesn't reach for his dropper. He keeps his hands open at his sides and his eyes on Kino's back, and he says, very quietly, "Fine. That's my report."

* * *

Back in Nana's kitchen, the kelp's seventh fiber lies flat against the glass. In the morning it will be a knot.

On the couch, Ollie is counting by sevens again. He doesn't turn his head. "It's not looking for me anymore," he says. "It's looking for the one who said no."

Teddy gets his paper at eight. What it costs is for me to say.

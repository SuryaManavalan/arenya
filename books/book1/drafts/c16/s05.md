Nana Itzel opened the screen door before they could knock.

Tongs in one hand. The other already reaching down for Ix's ears. Behind her the kitchen was thick with guajillo smoke, chiles blistering on the comal, a stockpot of hominy soaking on the back burner. The dresser was still shoved crosswise against the garage door at the end of the hall. The freezer hummed its small cold hum.

Nana looked at Kino's jacket. Not at their faces.

The welcome drained out of her.

"*¿Qué traes ahí?*"

"Nana," Kino said, and then, softer, in Spanish, something about having come back with a thing they shouldn't be holding. He took the bundle from inside his jacket, Arenya's scarf and Bell's napkin still wrapped around a coffee sleeve, and set it on the counter as far from the stove as the room allowed. Away from the salt line at the sink.

"No," Nana said. "Don't open it on my table."

She turned off the comal. She fetched a chipped enamel bowl from under the sink and poured white powder into it from a paper sack. Slaked lime, the stuff she soaked the corn in. A pale dust hung in the window light. She added water from the kettle, and it hissed, and clouded, and warmed.

"Salt is for keeping things out," she said, and set the bowl on the stove where the table couldn't see it. "*Cal* is for taking the skin off. Corn. Walls. Bones in a grave, so they don't smell. Ink." Her eyes came up to Arenya, sharp and tired. "Who gave it to you? And did anybody say *thank you* when they took it?"

Arenya put her hands flat on the table, well away from the bundle.

"Nobody gave it to me. Bell left it on the counter at Tartine. Exact change." Her palm was quiet. She didn't trust that. "Kino bagged it off the register tray. I didn't take it. I didn't say thank you. I didn't say anything. I stood there lying to a barista about my shift."

"Nobody said thank you," Kino added. "I watched my own mouth the whole time." He washed his hands at the tap, slowly, then looked at his forearm. "The only thank-you I've said this week was to Hale, at the pier. Before I could stop it. If you're asking who owes, it's me."

Arenya pulled out her notebook and wrote *cal = takes the skin off* without looking down.

"I'm sorry about your kitchen, Nana. We keep bringing the week in here with our shoes on."

Nana didn't answer. She set two plates in front of them: eggs scrambled with yesterday's tortillas, a slash of red salsa, a concha from the bakery bag on the fridge. She tapped Arenya's plate once with a fork.

*Now.*

Only then did she pick up the tongs.

She lifted the bundle by a corner of the scarf and let the coin slide onto a saucer. A Barber dime. Liberty in her headband, worn almost smooth, and *1912* still sharp, as if somebody had kept polishing it. When Nana turned it over with the tongs, a tiny **S** sat under the wreath. San Francisco mint.

The dime was damp. The damp didn't dry.

"Exact change," Nana said, mostly to herself. "*Ay, qué listo.*" She pointed the tongs at Kino. "He didn't give it. You didn't thank him. He *paid* it. A paid coin isn't his anymore. It's the till's. That line is closed. So he's looking through a door he already shut, and that's cheating, *mijo*. A cheater's window can be painted over."

She set the saucer beside the steaming bowl and held up three fingers, the old-woman way, folding each one down as she went.

"One. It goes in the lime. *Cal* takes the face off. By lunch it's a grey slug of silver and he's blind through it. But the face goes for good, and the date, and the little S. Whatever he wrote on it, nobody reads it after that. Not him. Not you.

"Two. A paid coin wants to be spent. I walk it to the *panadería* and put it in a till where he's never sat, and it stops being anybody's. But then some cashier has a cold spot on her hand next week instead of you." She looked at Arenya. "I don't like that one. I'm saying it because it's true.

"Three. You keep it. Wrapped, away from skin. You know where he's looking. But a window goes both ways, if you're stupid enough."

She didn't say who she thought was stupid enough. Her eyes slid to the empty air beside Ix's head, the place Arenya's own kept going.

"*Bueno.* Your coin, your choice. I'll do the hands."

Arenya ate three bites, because the plate had been tapped. Then she put the fork down.

"Not the till. I'm not handing some cashier a cold spot because I got squeamish." She looked at the bead of damp on the saucer. "And I'm not keeping it in a drawer where I can feel it. He's been looking at me through that thing for a day."

She flipped to a clean page, mostly so her hands had a job.

"I wanted to keep it one more day. Find out what he reads in it." She glanced sideways. "I'm not asking him to vote. He already voted. He said throw it in the bay."

Kino had eaten two bites of his own. He wasn't looking at the food.

"Lime, Nana. Today." He kept his voice low. "Not the freezer, Arenya. That's a post office. You'd be mailing him a window with our address on it. He already read what he's going to read. Tartine, the sleeve, BART. He knows we're hunting the Camry. What he doesn't know is what we do when he stops looking."

He picked up the tongs, gently, from Nana's hand, and lifted the dime by its edge. The bead of damp followed it up like it didn't want to let go.

"Say when. After this, he's blind and we're not."

Arenya thought about Bell's cold hands. The exact change laid on the tray like punctuation.

She nodded once.

"When." It came out flatter than she meant. "Face first."

She copied the date and the mint mark into her notebook before it was gone. *1912 / S / Liberty.* If Bell couldn't read it afterward, the page was hers and it was only ink.

Kino turned the dime so Liberty faced down, toward the lime, and let go.

No splash. No show. It slid off the tongs and sank a finger's width into the powder as if it weighed more than a dime should. Kino pressed it under with the flat of the tongs and held it there. The damp let go of his wrist like a hand loosening.

The lime hissed.

A dry little sound, a match dropped in sand. The bead pulled *down* into the cal, didn't evaporate, pulled down, and a ring of grey spread out from the coin, the same dead grey as the freezer salt, until it was the size of a jar lid. The paper under Arenya's pencil went cold for exactly one stroke of the S.

Then it was just paper.

"*Mira,*" Nana said. She leaned over the can, not too close. "Look."

Pressed into the grey, faint as breath on glass, was a print. Not a fingerprint. Knit ribbing. A cuff. As if someone on the far side had leaned an elbow on the window to get a better look, right up to the last second.

Then the lime settled, and it was gone.

"Bell," Arenya said. She wrote *cuff, knit ribbing* and underlined it twice. It was the only thing the man had ever given them for free.

"*Bueno.* He's blind." Nana pressed the lid down on the can with both palms. "And that one's not going in my pot. Kino, you go to Mi Tierra after. Buy me new cal. The good one, the Morelos." She said it like a grocery list, because it was one. The pozole had to be right.

The kitchen went quiet the way a room does when a fridge stops that you hadn't known was humming. Ix let out a long breath against Kino's boot.

And Arenya's palm woke.

The pencil dot. Dead since Glen Park. It didn't burn. It was *warm*, like a cup somebody had just set down. It wasn't pointing at the coffee can. When she tilted her wrist toward the can, nothing. When she tilted it toward the hall, it leaned.

The dime had been a window. It wasn't the only one.

* * *

She stood slowly and held her hand out in front of her like a compass. She stopped in the kitchen doorway. She didn't step into the hall.

Eight feet of linoleum and a runner rug, family photographs up one wall. At the end, the dresser: dark wood, four drawers, a doily, and the plaster Guadalupe facing the kitchen like a guard. Under the dresser's feet, where the door met the jamb, the frost line lay exactly as long as it had yesterday. One thin ruled stroke of white.

It hadn't moved. The warmth in her palm didn't care about it.

When she tilted her hand toward the door, the dot cooled a shade. When she tilted it up, toward the top drawer, it warmed the way a hand does over a burner that's just been switched off. Not hot brass. Not Bell's cold. It felt like a page in her notebook she had already written on.

Ix padded past her ankles, ignoring the doorway rule entirely. He went to the dresser, sat, and put his nose to the seam of the top drawer. Then he looked back down the hall at Kino. His tail moved once, slowly. It was the look he gave a closed car door when someone he liked was on the other side.

Behind her, the scrape of Nana's chair stopped partway.

"That drawer is just my things," Nana said.

Too fast. It was the first thing she'd said all morning to Arenya too fast.

*Abernathy,* Arenya said, inside, to the place by Ix's head. *Is that him? Or something else?*

*Not Bell, love.* Close and low, the cockney sanded down to something plainer. *Straight up. No hot brass, no Contador chill. Bell's cold has an edge on it like a ruler. This ain't got an edge.*

*It's the top drawer. Camphor. Old paper. And under that, guv, I'd swear it's your aunt's hand lotion. The jasmine one. Don't ask me how a drawer smells like a dead woman. I'm a spirit, not a sommelier.*

A pause, like a man picking his words out of a drawer himself.

*Quick ain't guilty. Quick is scared of what you'll do with it. Let her open it herself. And don't read it aloud till we know who's listening.*

*And the wall in my head?* *Still a wall. But Daly City's got a door in it somewhere. I'd put money on that. Wish I didn't.*

Arenya turned around.

Nana was watching the chiles. Not her.

"Nana." She kept it gentle, the way you'd talk to a witness who was about to leave. "That's the fastest you've said anything all morning." She lifted her palm a little, so Nana could see she wasn't pretending. "It's warm. Not Bell-cold. Warm like something somebody already wrote on. And Ix likes it."

She let it sit for a breath.

"I'm not going to open your drawer. It's your house. But Aunty's been dead a week, and she left me papers in a place I didn't know about. If there's something in there that's hers, I'd rather you hand it to me than have me stand in your hall guessing."

She looked at Kino. *Back me up, or tell me I'm out of line. Either's fine.*

Kino hadn't come down the hall. He'd moved to her shoulder instead, between her and the drawer.

"Nana," he said, in Spanish, the way you'd speak to a skittish animal you love. "*¿Qué hay en el cajón de arriba?* I'm not accusing. Ix doesn't sit like that for Bell." Then, in English, to Arenya: "You're not out of line. You asked it right."

He turned back and waited. He didn't fill the quiet.

"You never said *just my things* to me," he said finally. "I've asked you plenty of hard things. If it's Susmithaa's, it's hers to receive. If it's ours, it's ours to carry. If it's yours and it hurts, say so, and we'll shut the drawer and go buy your lime."

Nana stirred the chiles. Once. Twice.

Then she turned off the burner, and that was how they knew.

"*Ella me dijo,*" she said, to the pot, "you would do this. Your aunty. 'She'll stand in your hall and be polite at you, Itzel, and you will feel like a thief in your own house.'" She wiped her hands on the dish towel for longer than they needed. "*Ni modo.* Move, *mijo.*"

Kino moved.

Nana walked down the hall past the photographs. Ix got out of her way without being asked. She lifted the Guadalupe off the doily first and set her on the floor facing the door, like posting a guard. Then she opened the top drawer.

Camphor came out. Old paper. And under that, faint enough to be memory, sweet almond.

Arenya knew it from every hug for nineteen years before her brain caught up.

Nana took out a tea tin. A dented red Brooke Bond, the kind from an Indian grocery.

"Thursdays," she said. "Eleven years she brings her tea. Because she says mine is *agua de calcetín*." Sock water. She almost laughed. She pried off the lid.

Inside, on a bed of dusty leaves, lay one sheet of the same cream paper as the March declaration, folded in thirds. Nana didn't unfold it. She held it out at arm's length, the way you hand someone a pan that's still hot.

Through the paper Arenya could see the shape of the writing. Susmithaa's hand. Three short paragraphs. The same three ways of saying *no*. At the bottom, two lines. One signed, a looping *S. Sowri.*

The other was blank. Printed beneath it, in Aunty's neat capitals:

**WITNESS — ITZEL R. MARTÍNEZ.**

The dot on Arenya's palm went warm as a held cup. It wasn't pointing at the drawer anymore.

It was pointing at the empty line.

"She asked me in March," Nana said. Her voice was very steady, the way people sound when steady costs them. "After the shed. She said the first stroke would hold a little while, but a no needs two. And I said, *déjame pensarlo.* Let me think. Because I know what that book does to a name once it's in it. And my name in it is *his* address."

She didn't look at Kino.

"So I thought. Every Thursday, I thought. And then there were no more Thursdays."

She set the tin on top of the dresser, next to where the Virgin had been.

"That is the part I left out."

Nobody said anything. Down the hall the freezer hummed.

Arenya took the paper in both hands, folded, the way you take a bird. She didn't open it. The lotion smell was so exactly Aunty that for a second she couldn't find her voice, and she let the second pass.

"Thank you," she said. And heard it.

"No. Not *thank you*." She breathed. "I mean, you didn't owe me this."

She looked at Nana, not the paper. "The line is blank and it has your name on it, and I'm not going to ask you to fill it. Not today. Not like this, in your hall, with Kino standing right here. You thought about it for months because you knew what it costs. That's not cowardice. That's the only reason I'd trust you with anything."

She turned the sheet over once, checked the back, and slid it into her notebook between two clean pages.

"It stays folded until Kino and I have a plan that doesn't spend you. She gave me one stroke. I'm not going to go looking for a second in the people who love me."

Quieter: "She wrote *Feed Itzel's boy.* I think this is the other half of that sentence. Someone has to feed *you* back."

Kino put his hand on Nana's shoulder, the one she'd been holding too straight, and she let it stay.

"*Nana.* You don't have to be the one. Not for my sake." A beat. "But if the day comes you want to, I'm standing next to you. Not in front. Next to."

Nana looked at Arenya for a long time. The way she read the Lady's cards: all of it, top to bottom, before she said what she saw.

"*Ay, mija.*" She reached up and patted Arenya's cheek twice, hard enough to count. "She said you'd be polite. She didn't say you'd be *kind*. That one she kept for herself."

She bent and picked the plaster Guadalupe up off the floor and put her back on the doily. This time she turned her around, so she faced the garage door and the thin white rule beneath it.

"There. You watch *that*. Not my business in the kitchen."

The tea tin she left on top of the dresser with its lid off, so the hall would smell of camphor and Susmithaa all morning.

Then she went back to the stove and lit the burner. "Kino. Morelos. The yellow bag, not the one with the rooster. And tortillas. Arenya, you sit. You eat the rest of that egg. A paper doesn't need you hungry to stay folded."

"I'm coming with him," Arenya said. She checked her bag: notebook, Malachi's napkin, the titanium card. She pushed the letter deeper between the pages. "Bell's blind through the dime. I don't think it's the only window he's got." At the door she looked back. "Twenty minutes, Nana. The dresser stays put. If anybody knocks, even somebody nice, even somebody with a casserole, you don't open it."

Then, lower, to Kino, on the step: "After the cal. The Camry. We've got a frame to chase."

Against the cover of the notebook, her palm had gone from a held cup to the warmth of a pocket. It wasn't pulling anymore.

It was keeping her company.

Out on the street a car door shut, somebody's ordinary morning, and at the end of the hall Ix didn't even lift his head.

Sri Ganesh Tiffin took up half a strip-mall unit between a vape shop and a tax preparer.

Six tables. A laminated menu with the prices taped over twice. A TV in the corner playing a Tamil serial on mute, where a woman was crying in a doorway.

It was four-fifteen. Too late for lunch, too early for dinner. The room belonged to them and to one uncle reading the *India West* with his glasses pushed up onto his forehead.

The woman at the register didn't ask their names. She tore a slip off a pad, wrote a **2** at the top, and spiked it on the pass-through. Through the hatch, batter hit the tawa and hissed.

Then the smell came over the counter. Browned ghee. Curry leaf. Mustard seed popping.

It hit Arenya like a kitchen in Daly City at eleven on a Sunday. She almost turned around to see who was standing behind her.

Nobody was.

The dosa arrived a little longer than the plate was wide, still crackling. Steam came off the sambar in a straight line. She watched it for a full three seconds, because she'd started watching steam.

It went straight up. Nothing about it was wrong.

Her palm lay cool and quiet against the steel tumbler.

She tore off the first piece, dragged it through the podi, and ate it before she could think of a reason not to.

It was very good. She hated how good it was.

"Okay," she said, mouth half full. Her notebook sat closed and flat under her left hand like a paperweight. "Rule for the next twenty minutes. We don't say his name. We don't say the number. Nobody writes anything down."

She pushed the ghee podi across the table.

"Twenty minutes of being two people at a dosa place. How's the rectangle? Don't say fine. Give me a number, one to ten, how much it's annoying you." She held up a finger. "I'll go first. The palm is a two. My brain is an eleven, which is a different problem."

Kino didn't answer right away. He wrapped both hands around his tumbler and poured the coffee from it into the dabarah and back, long pours from high up, three times, the way any aunty would.

Something to do with his hands, she thought. Something to do while he listened to his own heart lag.

"Three," he said finally, and took the podi. "Annoying, not hurting. Like a tooth that went numb at the dentist. You keep poking it with your tongue." He tapped two fingers flat under his collarbone, once, without looking down. A small crooked smile. "Okay. Four. It's getting a vote."

"Phone left on a counter overnight?"

"That's the one." He shrugged one shoulder. "Half a beat behind. It'll catch up. Or I tell Nana, and she makes me drink something that tastes like a tire."

"Eleven's fair," he added, sprinkling podi over the ghee. "Mine's a nine. I'm just better at sitting on it."

"And thank you," Arenya said, before the moment could close. "For the phone. I haven't said it right." She turned the tumbler a little on the table. "I held my mouth shut, and you held the phone, and I'm not pretending those weigh the same."

"You don't owe me an even weight." He said it simply. "You held the harder thing."

He lifted his tumbler an inch. "To paper dosa."

"To paper dosa."

Steel clinked on steel. It was the least magical sound she'd heard all week, and she could have wept.

* * *

The counter man came by with a steel bucket and refilled Kino's katori without being asked. He looked at the plate Kino had already cleaned down to the crisp edge, and set a second ladle in its own bowl beside it.

"Eat, eat," he said, to nobody in particular, and went back to his tawa.

A bell jingled. Warm October wind came in with a DoorDash kid who checked his phone, took a paper bag off the register, and left.

For a while nothing in the room was counting anything.

Kino watched the uncle turn a page. He watched the serial, where a man was now walking away down a long corridor while the music swelled for nobody. Something in his face eased. The ranger who watched the trail went off duty.

"When I was nine," he said, "Nana had me sit on the mountain all night. By myself. No flashlight." He turned his tumbler a quarter turn. "She said if I was meant for it, something would come sit with me."

Arenya kept very still. It was a trick she was learning from him.

"Nothing came." The dry laugh was mostly breath. "Fog. A skunk. In the morning I told her a deer had, because I wanted it to be true." He shook his head. "I've never told anybody that. I don't know why I'm telling you in a dosa place in Livermore."

He looked up, straight at her. His voice went quiet.

"You held your mouth shut against your mother's voice today. I've seen grown men with twenty years of practice not do that." A beat. "How are you? Not the palm. You."

She looked at the dosa for a long while, because it was easier than looking at him.

"Me." She tore off a piece she didn't plan to eat. "Okay. Honest version. No bit."

Her mouth did the thing that was almost a joke and then wasn't.

"Two months ago the worst thing in my life was a listicle called *Eleven Hamsters Who Are Living Their Best Life*. I used to lie in bed and think, if something real ever happens, I'll be ready. I'll be the one who notices."

She set the piece down.

"And something real happened. The first thing it did was write my name in a book next to my dead aunt's. Today it called me in my mother's voice. And the worst part, the part I can't say to Ava or to Amma, is that some small, disgusting part of me is *glad*. Like, finally. Finally it's a real story."

Her left hand pressed flat on the notebook.

"I'm sitting here with a ghost's ink on my palm and part of me is taking notes."

Under her hand, the notebook settled.

Not cold. Not writing anything. It was only a little heavier than paper had any right to be, the way a cat settles when it hears its name. It lasted exactly as long as she meant the words.

Then it was a notebook again.

She didn't look down. She did not look at Kino either, which was how she knew he'd seen.

He said nothing. His eyes stayed on her face, and he let the quiet run, the way you do with something skittish.

At the counter, the man scraped the tawa clean with a long flat blade and hummed along with the strings. The uncle folded his paper into quarters, stood, and left exact change by the register. On his way out he said, "Good appetite, children," without turning his head.

The bell jingled. Outside, First Street was going gold.

"So that's how I am," Arenya said. "Scared. And kind of ashamed of what I'm not scared of."

"Glad's not a sin," Kino said.

He dipped a piece of crisp in the sambar. "I've been glad for twenty-six years that something might finally happen to me. Waiting my whole life to be needed. That's the same thing, Ari. And I'm the one who got trained for it."

Ari. Nobody had told him he could. She let it stand.

"Taking notes is how you stay a person in a room that wants you to be a name." He ate the crisp. "Keep doing it. Just don't let it sign anything."

She laughed, once, wetly, and wiped her eye with the back of her wrist, the one with the coin on it.

"Also," she said, "for the record. A deer came."

He went still.

"You were nine. You sat on a mountain all night in the fog with no flashlight, and you *stayed*. That's the deer." She pushed the second bowl of sambar back toward him. "Nana knew it was a lie, and she let you keep it, because it was the true kind. Eat, eat. He's right."

"A deer came," Kino said, almost to the tumbler. "Okay."

The laugh was shorter this time, and it cracked at the end.

"Don't tell Nana you said that. She'll take credit."

He finished the second bowl. He wiped his hands. He slid a ten under the katori, where the counter man would pretend not to see it.

At the door he held it for her. The bell rang over their heads.

"Then we go see your mother," he said. "Why she's been keeping your aunt's mail."

"I ask," Arenya said.

"You ask." He nodded toward the freeway and the long gold road to Daly City. "I'll watch her hands."

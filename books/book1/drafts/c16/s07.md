Lupe set the third mug down on Kino's side of the booth, at the empty place, and turned the handle outward.

The way you would for a regular.

Then she went back behind the counter to a grill hissing over one order of hash browns. The radio was doing the Giants pregame. Everything in the diner was ordinary except that cup.

Its steam didn't rise.

It leaned. Toward the front window, like a candle flame in a draft, though the door was shut and the air in Lupe's was as still as a held breath. The dot on Arenya's palm warmed slowly, the way a stovetop does when you've forgotten you turned it on.

She didn't look at the cup. She'd looked at it enough for one morning.

The bell over the door rang.

Malachi came down the aisle in a Raiders windbreaker two sizes too big, zipped to the throat. There was a fresh scrape along his jaw. He carried a CVS bag with nothing visible in it. He got as far as a step short of the booth and stopped, because he was looking at the mug.

"I didn't order that," he said quietly. "I didn't tell anybody I was coming in. I didn't know I was coming in until I saw your ponytail through the glass from across the street." His eyes followed the steam past his own hip. "Who's sitting there?"

"That one's for your friend, hon," Lupe called, cheerful, not turning. "He already paid." And then, half to herself, flipping the hash browns: "Nice old man. Counted it out in Spanish."

Arenya slid her notebook flat under her palm so her hand would have something to do besides shake.

"Sit," she said. "Not there. Next to me. Kino, scoot." It came out clipped. "You said don't call you. I didn't. You walked in on your own, which I'm choosing to find reassuring."

Kino was already up. He didn't touch the mug. He put himself between the empty seat and Arenya, back to the window, and nodded Malachi into the wall side, as far from the cup as the booth allowed.

"Not the third seat," he said, low. "Don't pick it up. Don't drink it."

Malachi sat. He put the CVS bag on his knees and kept one hand flat on top of it.

"Nothing in the bag," he said. "A prop. For the man I thought was following me. Raiders jacket, because nobody remembers a Raiders jacket. The jaw is a fence I misjudged." He looked at her. "I didn't see a cardigan. I saw a beige car with pollen on the roof, parked at a florist, and I walked past it without turning my head. Then I saw a ponytail in a window. I don't believe in being called, so I'll say I got hungry."

"Old man," Arenya said. "Counted it in Spanish. Grey cardigan, knit cuffs. That's Bell." She tipped her head a fraction toward the leaning steam. "Either he's been in this booth or he's in it now, and neither of us can see him. Either way he knew you were coming before you did."

"Yes."

"I got a plate, Malachi. Seven-K-B-Q-four-R. One character hidden behind a sign. I'd like to trade."

He took the notebook out from under her palm, read the line once, and gave it back.

"Seven-K-B-Q-four-R," he said. "Q fits. That's a real string. It goes to a lawyer or a clerk, not a cop. I won't run it myself. My name's on one query already, and I'm not putting it on a second."

With Kino standing in the aisle, the steam had to choose.

It didn't choose the window anymore. It bent around Kino's hip the way water goes around a post, and crossed the table low, a finger's width above the Formica, and stopped over her notebook.

It didn't touch the page.

It read it.

The dot went from warm to hot. Hot enough that she had to hold her knee absolutely still. On the radio the announcer was running the lineup, and the numbers came through a little too clearly. Batting second. Batting third.

Malachi watched it settle. Up close he smelled of road dust and somebody else's cologne, which presumably came with the jacket.

"Close that book," he said, very quietly. "Then I'll tell you who hit me."

She closed it.

She did not snap it shut, because snapping is a reaction. She lowered the cover like a lid on a pot, slid the whole thing off the table into her bag, and looped the strap around her wrist. Her knee was burning. She let it.

"Closed," she said. "Wherever it's reading now, it's reading the cover. Nice, boring cover. Dog breeds, mostly."

The steam lifted off the empty Formica like breath off a window and hung there, patient.

"Nobody hit me," Malachi said. He said it flat, and then he let it sit, and she watched him decide. "That was the lie I could afford. The true one is worse. I ran that plate under my own name. The log says *never issued*. Anyone with access can follow that back to a badge number." His jaw worked around the scrape. "I walked through Colma in a stranger's jacket to see who was close enough to read it."

"Not DOE," Arenya said.

"I'm not DOE." He said it to the tabletop. "I'm not going to say more in a room where the coffee has an opinion."

Nobody said anything. Kino's thumb had found the birthmark through his sleeve. It was quiet, and she could see he didn't like that.

"Counting in fives, out loud, onto a desk," Malachi added, lower still. "In my work that's a man who wants the sum witnessed. Whoever hears it is a party to it. If Bell counted for the clerk, then Benny and Rodel and Lupe have all been entered without agreeing to anything."

Lupe, at the grill, did not turn around. Which was its own kind of answer.

"Lupe," Malachi said. "Did he say when? Or did he leave the cup and go?"

With the notebook shut, the steam had nothing to read. It drew back toward its own mug, like a man sitting back in his chair.

"Said he'd be back when it's cold, hon," Lupe said, plating the hash browns and sliding them under the heat lamp for nobody. "Said don't clear it. His friend likes it warm." She wiped her hands on her apron. Then she pulled a green guest check off the spindle by the register and walked it down the aisle, stepping around Kino as if he were furniture she'd long since gotten used to.

"And he said give this to the girl. No charge."

She laid it face-down beside Arenya's folded hands and went back to the counter.

The back was blank. Through the thin paper the pencil showed in reverse: **3 COF.** And in the little box marked SERVER, where Lupe would have put her number, a single upright stroke in soft grey pencil.

The dot on Arenya's palm stopped burning.

It *pulled*.

The way Kino's birthmark had pulled toward the Camry. Not toward the cup. Toward that stroke, like a dog finding the one scent in a field. One stroke beside one stroke. *Dos testigos.* An old man in a cardigan had just offered to be her second.

She was so tired. That was the thing. She was so tired, and it was free, and it was already paid.

"Don't pick it up," Kino said. He had crouched at the booth's edge, level with her, away from the window. "Don't put your thumb on that stroke." His eyes stayed on the check. "He's not offering you a witness. He's offering you a second hand in the entry. His hand. Row twelve says a witness of one, waiting for a second. The clerk never asked who the second would be. It would count Bell."

"I know."

"That's what a trap looks like when it's polite. It's free. It pulls the way I'd want to be pulled if I were you and I was tired." He glanced at the dot, then at her face. "It's your row. I'm not taking it from you. But if you want a second stroke, it's Nana, with her own pencil. Or it's the Livermore room. It isn't him."

"Kino."

"Yeah."

"I know." She turned her palm over on the Formica, so the dot was facing the table and not the room. "No," she said, very softly. To the check. To the cup. To whoever was leaning on the other end of it. "I'm not taking an offer from a man who counts me in without asking. You want me to stand as a witness to my own entry. Cute."

Nothing happened.

Then something did. In the third mug, the steam stopped leaning. It went straight up, thin and ordinary, the way steam rises off any coffee left too long.

Lupe looked over from the register, and studied the cup the way you'd look at a regular's empty stool. "Huh," she said. "Guess he's not coming back."

She left both tens where they were, and didn't thank anyone for them.

* * *

They went out one at a time, because Malachi said so and because nobody had a better idea.

Kino first. "Ix is on the street," he said. "I want his nose on the door before anyone walks out." He put a ten under the sugar caddy anyway. "Not for the coffee. For Lupe. She's been pouring for him a while, and she shouldn't be the one who owes." He waited for Arenya's nod before he moved. The bell rang once behind him.

Arenya took a napkin and folded the check inside it, using her pen cap to slide the green paper to the edge of the table so her skin never met it. Evidence isn't agreement. Tell that to the ledger. She zipped it into the pocket with the tea-tin paper, against her ribs, and hitched the bag to the street side.

"Not DOE," she said to Malachi, barely moving her lips. "Noted. You owe me a real name, later. Not here."

"Freezer," he said. "Nana's freezer is the only post office I trust. Don't text my old number."

"Freezer, then."

She left Lupe a second ten under her own mug. "Thank you for the coffee," she said, to the back of the apron. "And for everything you didn't have to know."

Lupe lifted the spatula without turning.

The bell rang again.

Mission Street was bright and smelled of cut stems and bus exhaust. Kino was on the corner with his back against the diner's brick and his hand loose at his side, the way he stood when Ix was near. A patch of shade under the florist's awning, nose toward the cemetery road, tail low. Something low and heavy and very patient. Kino rested two fingers on the air above it, without touching.

The beige Camry was gone.

Where it had stood, the pollen on the curb had settled into a clean rectangle the exact size of a car. Untouched. As though it had only left a moment ago, and the street hadn't noticed yet.

In the florist's bucket every chrysanthemum faced the road again, all but one. That one faced Lupe's door.

Behind them, through the diner glass, a man in a hooded Raiders jacket went out the kitchen side and didn't look back. Uphill. Toward the cemetery road, counting something under his breath. She hoped it was in English.

"North's the way he went," Kino said. "We go the other way. BART. Nobody follows him by following us." He checked the sky, and then the curb, and she watched him not like what he found. "Nana's kitchen. The tin, the check, the dime. Then we decide who holds the second pencil. In daylight. Together."

"Agreed." She fell into step beside him, the zip pocket pressed to her ribs. "Kino. Thank you for saying it in there. I'd already decided. But it helps to hear someone else say the reason out loud." She almost smiled. "A polite trap. That's the worst kind. It's the kind I'd write a listicle about."

"Seven Signs Your Free Coffee Is Haunted."

"Don't. I'll use it."

She glanced back once. Through Lupe's window a cup sat steaming at nobody, straight up, ordinary.

The check didn't pull anymore. But it was warm. A little warmer than her own body, the way the dime had been before it went grey and blind. A window left unlatched.

"We don't pick the second stroke because we're tired," she said. "We pick it because someone chose to stand there. Nana gets to say no. So do I."

"Walk," said Kino.

She walked.

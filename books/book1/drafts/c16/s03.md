Nana Itzel taped a sheet of butcher paper to her fridge on Wednesday night and wrote on it in the square hand she used for church raffles.

*Fri 30 — 7:40 — the box.*

Then a long blank.

Then, circled twice: *10 Nov — día dieciséis.* And under it, smaller: *pozole. chile aparte.*

Thirteen days. She set the marker down like it weighed more than a marker.

Out in the garage, the frost line across the side door had melted into a wet stripe on the concrete. It was perfectly straight.

* * *

Ava wrote an entry in her symptom log at 1:15 a.m. that she hadn't planned to write.

*Same lamp. Same flame. The hand again: old, a woman's, a ring on the middle finger worn thin as wire. It pinched the wick out. Not angry. Like turning off a light in a room someone just left.*

Her kitchen faucet dripped on the beat of a clock she didn't own.

At 3:19, south of Market, Caleb was awake, because he was awake at 3:19 every night now. The canister sat on the motel nightstand beside two untouched blister packs. The burnt Crown at the back of his skull had been quiet for two days.

Tonight it clicked. One dry note, like a pen clicked closed.

He went still, the way he used to when Shepherd's voice was about to come. Nothing followed. No voice, no count. His hands shook. He put them flat on his knees until they stopped, and he did not reach for the left pocket.

"Not yours," he told the empty room. "Whoever's holding that pen. I'm not signing."

He put the canister inside his jacket, against his ribs, and watched the door until it got light.

* * *

Arenya woke Thursday with the lamp burning low and steady, and her right hand already open on the blanket, as if she had fallen asleep holding something out.

Under the frost *12* and its rule, the pencil dot had spread. It wasn't a dot anymore. It was a short, faint line, like a stroke someone had started and lifted the pen from.

She didn't touch it. She photographed it next to the *12*, the way she'd log any piece of evidence, and her hands weren't quite steady doing it.

*It's not waiting for me anymore,* she wrote in the notebook, under *Thursday. Thirteen days.* *Somebody started.*

*Morning, love,* said Abernathy, somewhere near the ceiling, and for once he didn't sound like he was performing. *Look, don't touch. Nobody finished that. Somebody started and lifted the pen. That's not the clerk, he's tidy as a bank. This is someone leaning in, and stopping because they heard something. Or were told to wait.*

"Great," she whispered. "A collaborator."

*And it spread while the lamp stood straight. It didn't lean at a threat. Whoever's writing, the lamp doesn't count 'em as one.* A pause. *Hot brass off your palm, faint. Wet ink under it. Same as Box 112. I'm telling you before you ask.*

She hadn't been going to ask.

*Part of me knows what an unfinished stroke wants,* he said, lower, and the cockney thinned for half a word. *A second hand to agree with it. Don't offer yours. Not even to be polite.*

"Noted."

*Thirteen days. Eat something proper, your Ladyship. Not just the coffee. I saw that.*

She went downstairs in socks. Amma stood at the stove with her back turned, and Arenya told the back of her head: Kino was coming, and nobody answered the door to a grey cardigan. Not for a survey. Not for a flower.

She texted Kino. *Come over. Bring Ix. The line on my hand moved.*

Then she put the phone face-down on the counter and allowed herself one cup of coffee before she decided what to be afraid of.

* * *

Across the Bay, Malachi read a forwarded tip twice at a gas-station counter, over coffee he didn't drink.

*An elderly resident reports a man in a grey cardigan "drawing on her garage." No damage. Responding officer notes a "very straight water stain."*

Three blocks from the Sowri house. An address he had already flagged once.

He didn't call the officer. A second query left a second trail, and his own people read trails. He took the burner out and made the call he had promised her. Six words and a time.

"It's me. Ten o'clock. Somewhere with people. You choose it. Text me a street, not a name."

He took the battery out and drove to Daly City, slowly, and parked at the far end of the hill without stopping at the house. He wanted to see who else was watching it before she saw him.

Nobody was. A recycling truck worked its way down the block. A kid with a backpack ran late. Then a young man in a hood parked two doors down from the Sowris', looked over the hedges for a long time, and knocked on the door. Twice, then once.

Malachi wrote the pattern down.

He didn't notice the rear window of his own car. A line of frost had been drawn across the glass from edge to edge, very thin and perfectly straight. It had started forming while he read the tip. In the morning sun it was already beginning to sweat.

* * *

"It's Kino," said the voice through the wood. "Two and one."

She checked the peephole anyway. She checked the hedges behind him. Then she opened the door just far enough to pull him in by the sleeve.

"Hood down, wolf in the hall, shoes off. Amma will feed you whether you want it or not." She turned her hand over between them. "It didn't move. Somebody touched it."

Kino leaned over her palm with his hands clasped behind his back, like a man at a museum. He looked at the grey sheen on the pencil line, at the *12*, at the new stroke. He didn't breathe on it.

"That's not graphite," he said quietly. "Graphite smudges the wrong way. This sits *on* the line, like it was gone over once, lightly, by someone checking." His birthmark ached, slow and warm. "Someone read it, Ari. Not wrote. Read."

"So we're being proofread."

Her phone buzzed face-down on the counter. One call, unanswered. A number she didn't know. Then a voicemail.

Amma turned from the stove with the spoon in her hand and looked at the phone, at the door, at her daughter. "Which one first, kanna?"

"Nobody listens alone," Arenya said. "Speaker. Low. Nobody answers it. Not yes, not no, not maybe. If it counts, we let it count to itself."

She set the phone in the middle of the counter between the four of them and the pot of rasam. She pressed play and kept her eyes on Kino's face, not the screen.

A car-interior hiss, an engine idling under it. A man's voice, clipped and federal.

*"It's me. Ten o'clock. Somewhere with—"*

The hiss thinned. Very close to the microphone, closer than anyone in a car could be, a pen clicked shut.

A second voice, dry as a page being turned, in no hurry at all:

*"…doce. Leído."*

Then the first voice came back in the middle of a word, as if nothing had cut it. *"—people. Don't bring the hand into a room you didn't pick. Bring the—"*

Nineteen seconds. It stopped.

Nobody said anything. Amma's spoon had stopped above the pot, and a drop of rasam fell from it without a sound. On Arenya's palm, the grey sheen on the pencil line dulled a shade, like a thumbprint wiped away.

In the hall, Ix's ears were turned toward the far end of the block. Its lips had drawn back from its teeth.

"That was Malachi," Kino said, very low. "And that second voice said *leído*. It read it. Not us. *It.*"

Amma didn't ask why. She took the salt down from the shelf, the cylinder with the blue girl on it, and poured a white line along the sill over the sink. Too thick. She did everything too thick when she was frightened.

Arenya turned the phone face-down under the shaker. For a second the salt nearest the phone looked grey. Then it didn't.

* * *

At the top of the hill, Malachi's call log said *0:19*.

He had said six words and a time and hung up. He was sure of it. He checked again anyway, and didn't like how that sounded.

In the mirror, the frost line had finished sweating. Where it had been, the glass was marked with a thin dry stroke. A tally.

He got out with the engine running and didn't touch it. He photographed it from the angle where the sun was cleanest, and looked up and down the hill. A kid on a scooter. A woman with a stroller. No sedan. No cardigan.

Someone had put a pen to his car while he sat in it.

He took the SIM out of that phone and drove to a pay phone in Colma, across El Camino from a monument shop whose window said GRANITE · BRONZE · LETTERING in flaking gold leaf. He had picked the pay phone three days ago because no one else would think of it. The receiver smelled of someone else's cigarette. Nothing on it was cold.

From a second burner, one he hadn't given her, he sent a text with no name in it. *Don't come to ten. The line is read. Pick a public street, say nothing aloud, and send it to this number by 9:30.*

He wanted her alive more than he wanted her answer.

Waiting at the curb, he opened the photo of the back window to check the angle. The stroke was clean and dry and photographed well. But the glass had reflected the street behind him, and at the far left edge, where he had seen a kid on a scooter, there was a sleeve.

Grey. Knit cuffs. Resting on the roof of a parked car as casually as a man waiting for a light.

He hadn't seen it when he turned. He'd have bet his badge.

* * *

The text came to the kitchen counter with no contact name, a 650 number.

She looked at it the way you'd look at a dog that had just learned your name.

Kino slid the phone a hand's width farther from the shaker with two fingers on its edge. "Whoever that is, he's afraid of the same people we are," he said. "That doesn't make him a friend. Pick the street yourself. Type as little as you can. No *I*, no names. I'll be at your elbow. I won't tell you which street. That's yours."

She took out the coffee-stained notebook and wrote in block capitals, tilting the page to him.

*IF I TYPE A STREET, IT'S WRITTEN. WRITTEN = READ?*

Under it, faster, she wrote what she already knew. *TARTINE. GUERRERO + 18TH. 7 AM. AVA OPENS. LINE OUT THE DOOR, STROLLERS, LAPTOPS. NOBODY'S CLERK SITS IN THAT LINE AND STAYS INVISIBLE.*

Then she crossed out *TARTINE* hard enough to tear the paper. Names were how she'd gotten onto row twelve.

What she would actually send was barely a sentence. *Where I got the footage. Opening. Outside, by the bench.* A clerk reading it would get a riddle with no address. A Department of Energy man would get a corner.

She raised her eyebrows at Kino. He read the page sideways, reached the torn word, and gave one small nod, the kind you'd give across a classroom.

She didn't type it in the kitchen. She went out the back door into the cold, past the salt, and typed with the screen tilted down against her chest like a secret from the sky. Wet jasmine. Somebody's dryer vent. She sent it and set the phone face-down on the concrete with her palm flat on top.

For a moment, nothing.

Then the glass under her hand went faintly warm. It wasn't the phone. It was the pencil dot, warming like a coin held too long in a fist.

Nothing ticked. Nothing said *leído*. Either the clerk had read a sentence with no street in it and found nothing to enter, or nobody had read it at all.

"Amma," she said through the screen door, very quietly, "the rasam's fine. Turn it back on. We're not hiding from soup."

Inside, the burner clicked on. Amma did not look up, but she set out a fourth bowl.

* * *

In Colma, the second burner buzzed.

Malachi read it once and didn't write it down. *Where I got the footage.* He knew where she'd gotten the footage. He'd watched that bakery from a corner table after the paramedics left, and so had four men with laptops.

He didn't answer. A reply was another page for someone to read, and what she needed from him was a position. He snapped the SIM and dropped the halves into two different storm drains.

Then he went back to the sleeve. In a motel off Mission, with the laptop's wifi off, he cropped it, enlarged it, and read forty pixels of plate in curved glass shot into low sun. Seven characters he would have sworn to. A dealer frame he could almost read. *SERRA—*

At 2:10 a.m. he called a woman at CHP who owed him, from the motel's landline. He gave her the plate. He heard her type it.

*"No record, Shaw. That plate's never been issued. You sure you're not reading it backwards?"*

He hung up and looked at the crop again, and thought of the curved glass. He hadn't flipped it. Mirrored, the characters made an entirely different plate, and it was too late to call back with that one.

His name, his badge number, and a plate that didn't exist were now sitting in a CHP query log with a time stamp. He had spent a week watching other people leave paper.

Tonight he had left some.

* * *

Thursday, 6:58 a.m. Guerrero and 18th.

Fog lay in the street like it had been poured. The bench was wet, and someone had wiped one end dry with a sleeve. The line had already started: two strollers, a man in cycling shoes, a woman on a call in Cantonese. Inside, at a corner table that faced the door and the bench, a man had been nursing one black coffee since 5:30. There was no phone on his table.

Arenya came up 18th from the J stop with her hood down. Kino walked a step behind her and a step to the street side, so his shoulder was between her and the curb. Ix nosed every tree well on the block.

She stopped six feet from the bench and didn't sit. She looked through the window at the corner table before she looked at anyone else. She gave Malachi exactly one nod, the kind you give a stranger on a train who's read the same page.

At seven the lock turned. Ava propped the door with her hip, apron on, hair tied back, a tray of morning buns on her other hand.

She saw Arenya. She saw the hand Arenya was holding flat against her thigh.

And the oven light behind her had gone the color of a lamp's flame, and was leaning, very slightly, toward the street.

"Morning, Ava." Arenya's voice came out too bright, so she brought it down. "I'm not here to ask for anything. I needed a crowd and a door that opens at seven, and you're the only person I know with both. If anybody asks who's been in here this week, tell them the truth and tell them it was coffee. And if the light does anything strange..." She glanced at Kino, who was watching the oven, and at Ix, who was watching the street. "You don't have to be brave about it. I won't think you're crazy. I'm the last person in this city who gets to."

Ava looked at the oven light for exactly as long as it took to be sure. Then at Arenya, at the hidden hand, at the animal, at the man in the corner. Her left hand went warm under the tray.

"Okay. Clinical summary." Fast and thin; she slowed it. "It's seven a.m., I've slept two hours, and my oven is doing something that isn't in the manual. It's leaning at *you.* I'm holding you to the not-crazy thing, because my mother already holds the record." She set the tray down and wiped her hands on her apron, palms up, because she couldn't hide them. "Also, I dreamed you. A woman made of lamps going out one at a time, a hand with a ring pinching the wick. Your face was in it, or near it. I wrote the text and didn't send it."

She breathed.

"Come in. All of you, the wolf too. But that guy's been here since five-thirty, and I'd like to know if he's with you."

In the corner, Malachi turned his empty cup a quarter turn on its saucer. He did not get up. A man who stands when the girl arrives has told every window on Guerrero who she came for.

When the line had swallowed Ava's attention, he rose, paid for nothing, and passed the bench on his way out. He stopped one arm's length from Arenya, facing the street.

"You picked a good corner," he said, low. "Don't look at me. I'm leaving something and walking on."

He set a folded napkin on the dry end of the bench. Then, quieter, for her only: "The line on your hand is read. Whoever reads it is sitting closer than I'd like. Let someone else hold your pen today."

He walked up 18th toward BART. No phone. He didn't turn around.

Arenya picked the napkin up by its dry corner and slid it into the notebook between two pages of her own handwriting. She took the pen out of her bag. She put it back.

* * *

Kino saw him first, because he'd been watching for a cuff on a roof and found one in the line.

Three people back, behind the stroller and the man in cycling shoes: a grey cardigan with knit cuffs. Mr. Bell stood with his hands folded in front of him and a single white chrysanthemum in its florist's sleeve. He was reading the menu board with the mild interest of a man who had all morning. When the line moved, he stepped forward exactly one pace.

The door chime rang for the cyclist ahead of him.

When Bell crossed the threshold, stepping politely over Ix, the chime made no sound.

Behind the counter, the oven light gutted and, for the first time, leaned *away.*

"Good morning," Bell said to Ava, pleasantly, in a voice like a page being turned. "A plain croissant, please. For here." He set the chrysanthemum beside the tip jar, stem toward her. "And whatever the young lady at the door is having. I'd like it on my account."

Arenya did not take the bun. She was not signing for a pastry. She stopped in the doorway over Ix, close enough that the chime would have rung if the chime did anything, and said it level and small, as if it were any other sentence.

"Ava. Don't take the flower. Don't take his money. Don't write his name on anything, not even a ticket. If he wants a croissant he can pay cash like a person." She put a crumpled ten on the nearest table and slid it toward her with one knuckle. "That's for mine. I'm not on anyone's account."

Kino murmured the count she was asking for, eyes on the stroller. "Eleven steps to the corner. The J is forty more. Between him and the door there's a stroller, a cyclist, a woman on her phone. That's enough people. He won't do anything in front of them. He doesn't have to. He only collects."

He stepped in over Ix, not around, and stood at Ava's elbow so that Bell had to look past him. He turned the tip jar a quarter turn, until its glass sat between Bell's hand and her till.

"Cash, señor. Four-fifty. Buy it, or don't."

Ava had the Sharpie and the cup in her hands before she knew it. Four years of habit; her mouth was already shaping *name for the order?* She heard it. She caught it. She set the Sharpie down on its cap.

"We don't do accounts. Sorry." Her customer-service voice, the one for men who wanted to explain oat milk. "Cash or card. And you can take your flower when you go. No gifts from customers. Health code."

It wasn't true. She couldn't think of a truer way to say *no.*

Then she did what she was actually good at. She looked.

The door was open to fifty-degree fog. The cyclist had left a faint breath-mist going out. Bell left none. The steam off the espresso wand drifted toward the window, toward Kino, toward anything else in the room, and bent a hand's width around Bell, the way smoke avoids a closed jar. His hands hadn't moved since he folded them. She held her own breath for one count, and the flame in the oven glass shrank like a shoulder turning away.

*Case note, 7:04. Subject polite. Chime silent. Light averse.*

She tonged a croissant onto a plate and set it on the counter. Not into his hand.

"Of course," said Bell. He wasn't offended. He sounded like a man who had heard *no* many times and filed every one. He laid the change down a coin at a time: four quarters, four more, two dimes, a nickel, five pennies. Exact. One of the dimes was worn almost smooth, and the date was just legible. *1912.*

"Health code," he repeated mildly, as if he were noting it somewhere. He took the plate. His eyes went to the tag pinned to her apron, with the courtesy of a man reading a name off a lapel.

"Thank you, Ava."

Her left palm went cold under the heat, as if a thumb had rested on it.

He didn't take the chrysanthemum. He didn't push it toward her either. "It isn't a gift," he said, to no one in particular. "It's a reservation. For the sixteenth day. I understand there will be pozole."

He sat at the corner table Malachi had left, the one that faced the door and the bench. His back was very straight. He didn't eat.

Nobody in the line noticed anything. The woman on her phone said something in Cantonese and laughed.

Arenya's stomach dropped through the floor. *How does he know about the pozole?* Then she stopped the thought, because it was the one he wanted her to have.

She went in over Ix to the end of the counter, where Ava could see her and Bell would have to turn his head. She wrote in block capitals and tilted the page.

*HE SAID YOUR NAME. YOU DIDN'T SAY IT BACK. GOOD. DON'T ANSWER TO IT TODAY. PALM COLD?*

Under it, smaller: *SALT IN YOUR APRON. DON'T TOUCH THE DIME OR THE FLOWER. KINO WILL BAG THEM.*

"I'm sorry," she said, low and a little ragged. "I came for a crowd, not to hand you a case file. You get to say *out,* and I'll walk and take him with me. But I think he follows the name now, not me."

Then she turned her head just enough to have the corner table in the edge of her eye, and said to the room, at the volume of someone ordering coffee: "Ava's shift ends at three. If anyone wants to come around then, I'll be here."

A plain lie. She wanted to see which way the cardigan turned.

Kino took a napkin from the dispenser and used it like a glove to lift the dime off the till tray by its edge. He dropped it into a folded coffee sleeve, folded the end over twice, and put it in his inside pocket, away from his skin. The chrysanthemum he left exactly where it was. Moving it would be a way of answering him.

He pressed a pinch of salt from Nana's kitchen into Ava's apron pocket with two fingers, the way you'd slip a kid a coin. "Not for luck. So there's something in there that isn't his." Then, to her left hand: "If the cold comes back, don't rub it. Put the palm flat on something that's been here a long time. Ask it to hold."

Ava pressed her palm to the steel counter. The cold sat in the middle of it like a coin, a thumb's width exactly.

"Left palm," she said, barely moving her mouth. "Localized, about two centimeters. I'd like it noted that I hate that I can measure it." She closed her fingers around the salt. Behind her, the oven glass flickered back up, a flame trusting the room again. "Not out. If I leave, he gets my kitchen, and my kitchen's the only place the light likes me." She wiped the counter in a slow circle around the chrysanthemum without touching it. "And the three o'clock lie is bad. I close at two on Thursdays. If he's listening, I'd rather he think you're careless than that I am."

Bell's head did not turn for *three o'clock.* It did not turn for *two on Thursdays.* But his right forefinger lifted a quarter inch off the table and settled again, like a pen tapping a correction into a margin.

Ava looked straight at the corner table for the first time. At ordinary customer volume: "Sir, your croissant's getting cold."

She watched his hands, not his face.

He looked at her. His expression was courteous, even fond.

"It keeps," he said. "Most things do, if someone looks after them."

He broke off one horn of the croissant. He didn't eat it. He laid it on the edge of the plate, neatly, the way you'd set aside a receipt. Then he folded his hands and looked out at the bench, where the napkin wasn't any more.

"Go out the front, both of you," Ava said. "Take the wolf. Text me one word when you're a block away. Make it a word that isn't a name. I'll text back a pastry."

* * *

They went out together with Ix between them. The fog was lifting off the top of Guerrero, and the J's bell clanged two blocks down. Nobody followed.

Arenya counted her steps without meaning to. Under the rule on her palm, the pencil dot throbbed once for every one of them, all the way to the corner. Then it stopped, as though it had been told where she was and was satisfied.

Beside her, Kino touched the coffee sleeve through his jacket lining. The dime inside was exactly as cold as a dime should be, and no colder.

That was somehow worse.

At 7:14 Ava's phone lit up on the shelf under the register with one word. *fog.*

She texted back: *kouign-amann.*

In the oven glass, the flame stood up straight. Behind her at the corner table, Bell still hadn't eaten a bite, and he didn't look like he was waiting for anyone.

He looked like a man who had already been told when they would be back.

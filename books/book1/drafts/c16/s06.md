Mi Tierra smelled of masa and floor cleaner.

The cal sat on the bottom shelf by the dried chiles. Morelos, two bags, a picture of a smiling corn cob on the front. Arenya stood by the produce bins with her notebook shut against her ribs, the folded declaration inside it, and watched Kino count out his change.

"Cover story," she said, low. "One more time, because I'm a bad liar when I haven't rehearsed. You're Joe. I'm your cousin who's done this before and hates dealerships. Grey or beige Camry, a few years old, we're not picky. Nobody says Bell. Nobody says Sowri. Nobody gives a last name."

"And if a salesman wants your license?"

"I get a phone call." She tucked a strand behind her ear. "And if the dot on my hand wakes up, I tell you before I tell the salesman. Deal?"

He nodded. The cashier counted his change back into his palm the ordinary way, *diez, quince, veinte,* out loud, and nothing in the room leaned toward the counting.

Kino closed his hand a beat fast anyway.

They took the bags and the warm tortillas back to Daly City. Nana accepted them at the door without opening it wider than a forearm. The dresser hadn't moved. The Guadalupe still faced the garage. A moment later the Corolla keys came out through the gap.

"Gas is half," said Nana's voice. "Don't park where you can't leave."

* * *

Serramonte Boulevard ran downhill toward the Colma line: mall, In-N-Out, the cemetery hills a dull green behind the fog.

SERRAMONTE AUTO turned out to be a narrow lot squeezed between a tire shop and a shuttered Sizzler. Thirty-odd cars under limp pennant string. Prices in yellow grease pen. A stucco office with a bell on the door.

A heavyset man in a Warriors windbreaker was hosing down a Civic. He lifted the nozzle as the Corolla pulled in and smiled with his whole face.

"Morning, morning! Benny. You folks looking, or you folks *looking?*"

No beige Camry on the lot.

But through the office window, a corkboard covered the back wall. Polaroids, dozens of them, each a smiling buyer holding keys beside a car, each dated in Sharpie. The dot in Arenya's palm, quietly warm since Nana's kitchen, gave one small tug toward that window. A sleeping cat lifting its head.

*Brass again,* Abernathy said, on the bond. *Faint. Not off the cars. Off that corkboard. Every one of them Polaroids is a little receipt, ain't it?* The accent slipped half a word, went old and flat, and he caught it. *Don't point with the hand, love. Look at the dates, not the faces. And Bell's blind through the dime, not through brass. If it goes hot, you say "bathroom" and we walk. Union rules.*

She climbed out and put on the face she used for hold music. Patient. A little bored. Nobody worth remembering.

"Looking," she told Benny. "My cousin Joe needs something reliable. Camry, maybe. Something that's been around the block but not through a flood." She tipped her head at the office. "Do you do those Polaroids for everybody? Very small-town."

Her left hand stayed in her jacket pocket. It did not point.

Benny killed the hose and wiped his palm on the windbreaker before he offered it. "The wall? Everybody. Thirty-one years. My dad started it. I still buy the film off eBay, it's a crime what they charge." He laughed at something private. "You drive off my lot, you're on the wall. Even the ones who don't smile. *Especially* those."

Kino got out last and stayed a half-step behind her shoulder, hands visible. "Joe," he said, and nodded once.

"Just Joe? Like Cher." Benny didn't mind. Cash people came without last names all the time. "Cash buyers are my favorite. Come in, come in, I got coffee, I got a binder."

Arenya didn't look at the board. She looked at the glass, where the board looked back.

Low on the right, half under a newer photo, was the curve of a beige roof and the edge of a man's arm holding up keys. The face was hidden by the overlap.

The sleeve wasn't. Grey knit. The cuff turned once.

*That one,* said Abernathy quietly. *The picture's the brass, A. It's still being looked at. From both sides.*

* * *

The bell jangled. Burnt coffee, a space heater ticking, a desk buried in auction sheets.

Kino stepped over the strip of concrete in front of the door. Not on it. Arenya noticed because he did it so smoothly, and because the strip was wrong: the hose water had run all the way across the lot to the storm drain, the way water should, and then reached that one stretch of concrete and *beaded*. Small, even drops, spaced like tally marks. It sat there and refused to soak in. As if the strip had already been written on and wouldn't take anything more.

She stepped over it too.

Benny dropped into his chair, which faced away from the wall. "Camrys, Camrys. I got an '18 coming Tuesday, grey, one owner, a dentist."

Kino took the customer chair, square to the desk, so that Benny's back stayed to the corkboard. "Who's the dentist? Why's he selling? Dentists don't sell a car that's never died." He tapped the binder, dull and stubborn. A man who didn't like paperwork and was going to make you read all of it. "Show me the service records. Not the auction sheet. Mileage on the sheet and mileage on the dash are two different people."

"Service records!" Benny said, delighted. "A man who reads!"

He swiveled all the way around and started digging in a filing drawer that screamed on its rails. His back was a wall of Warriors gold. The drawer was loud enough to cover a parade.

"Joe's easy, he doesn't like paperwork," Arenya said, to nobody. "Make him prove it, Joe." She wandered to the board like a woman admiring a nun. (There was a nun.) "Thirty-one years. That's a lot of keys."

She said it warmly. Her right hand lifted the corner of the top Polaroid with one fingernail, the way you peel a sticker you mean to put back.

She kept her eyes off the arm. She kept them on the white strip at the bottom, where a hand that had written ten thousand of these had written:

**WALT — 3/19 — 4:51 PM — CASH ✓**

March nineteenth.

The same afternoon Hale had rented Box 112 with new twenties, fifty-nine minutes after Susmithaa signed her declaration. Two men, one afternoon, one mile apart.

Then she saw the border.

The Polaroid's white margin was frosted. A fine rime ran along the bottom edge, under the Sharpie, straight as a ruled line. The space heater ticked four feet away and the frost did not care.

In her pocket the dot went from warm to hot, sudden as a held breath. It didn't pull toward the photo.

It pushed away. A hand jerking back from a stove.

*Window's lit, love,* Abernathy said, very quiet and very fast. *Not the dime. That. He's lookin' out of his own picture at the* room. *Not at you. Yet. Put the top one back and it shuts like an eyelid. Take it, and you're carryin' a window in your bag.*

Behind her Benny slapped a folder on the desk. "Ha! Oil changes every five thousand. She's a saint."

She read the strip once, slowly, the way you read a license plate you'll have to swear to. Then she let the Polaroid fall flat over its twin. It settled. The heat in her pocket dropped back to warm.

She didn't take it. She was not carrying his window past the beaded strip and into Kino's car.

She flipped her notebook open against her thigh, as if doing sums on a payment plan, and wrote: *Walt. 3/19. 4:51. Cash.* Under that, smaller: *59 min after S.'s declaration. Hale's twenties, same day.*

"Dealer frame on a sedan this clean usually means it came off your own lot," she said, bored, pen moving. "Is this a trade-in, Benny? Or a private sale you're brokering?"

"Own lot, beautiful. Auction car. Came in February, sold in March. No consignment. I'm not a charity."

Kino leaned an elbow on the counter so his shoulder blocked Benny's view past him. "The Camry in the Polaroids. The beige one. Walt, the board says. He the type who haggles, or the type who pays cash and leaves? Last time I bought off a cash guy the title was a mess. You remember how he signed?"

Benny leaned back until the chair groaned and regarded Kino with fond suspicion.

"Walt," he said. "Oh, *Walt.* You don't forget Walt."

He told it the way a man tells a story he's told at the tire shop. Old guy. Cardigan, in March. Nice manners, like a librarian. Didn't haggle. Didn't test drive. Paid cash, old bills, soft, every one folded once down the middle, and counted them onto the desk out loud.

"In *Spanish.* 'Diez, quince, veinte.' Doesn't speak a word of it otherwise, I tried. Then he signs everything, beautiful hand, fountain pen. And reads every page twice." He snorted. "*Twice.* Nobody reads my contracts. Title's clean. Walter Bell. Address was a mailbox, Colma Notary over on Mission. Number twelve."

Arenya's pen went through the paper.

"Wouldn't take a picture," Benny went on, thumb jerking at the board without looking at it. "'I don't photograph, Mr. Benasaraf.' Very polite. I said, Walt, everybody goes on the wall. Took it anyway. Came out funny, see. Face washed right out. Forty bucks a pack and it washes out the one guy."

Arenya's pen was still moving. Kino's elbow was still on the desk. Benny's eyes went from one of them to the other, friendly, and stayed a beat too long.

"Funny thing. He called me. Last week, Tuesday maybe. Wanted to know had anybody come asking about his car. I said, Walt, who'd ask about a Camry? He says, 'If they do, Mr. Benasaraf, take their names. I'd like to send a thank-you.'"

He pulled a sticky pad toward him and uncapped the Sharpie, all good cheer.

"So. Joe. And the lady is…?"

* * *

Arenya closed the notebook on her pen with a soft, bored snap.

"Oh, don't bother with the pad, Benny. We're not buying today. We're window-shopping with a spreadsheet." She stood and hitched her bag onto her shoulder, keeping it between her and the corkboard. "Joe's the one who can't commit. I'm the one who says, let's go look at the Honda place and come back. Nobody gets a name until there's a number."

She tapped the desk twice over the sticky pad, friendly, so that its corner lifted away from the Sharpie.

"But that's a great story. Counting in Spanish. I'd have remembered that. If he calls again, tell him the Camry's got a fan. Doesn't need a thank-you note." She turned toward the door. "Joe, babe? Shall we?"

Kino slid the pad back across the desk with two fingers. Not rude. Final.

"Joe's fine," he said. "Joe Ramos. But you don't need a name for a thank-you, Benny. You need one for a sale." He nodded at the board. "Man pays in folded bills, won't be photographed, and calls to ask who's been asking? That's not a customer. That's a guy checking his fences. I'd want to know why, if it were my lot."

"Joe—"

"Hold the dentist's car till Saturday. We'll call." He put out his hand, and Benny shook it, and Kino did not say which number.

Then he fell in half a step behind her, between her and the corkboard, and walked her over the beaded strip without looking back.

Out in the sun, Arenya's whole body let go. She kept walking fast.

"Box twelve," she said, flat. "Bell's the one who rents it. Not Hale. Hale's is 112. Two boxes, same shop, same week. Rodel told me *he* wouldn't take anything out of the box. Rodel knew whose twelve it was."

"Either Bell's following the same paper trail," Kino said, low, "or he laid it." He thumbed the cold dime through the scarf in his pocket. "Honda place first, so the lot sees us go. Then Mission, on foot."

"And I want the plate before I want anything else. If it's parked within a block of that shop, I'm not going in. I'm just going to look."

"Look, don't enter." He steered them across the boulevard, taking the traffic side. "If the plate's there, we don't touch the car, don't stand near it, don't read it out loud. If your palm goes hot, you say 'Civic' and we turn around. No arguing. No one being brave."

She held up her left hand, palm out, the cold *12* and its warm pencil dot facing him like a pledge. "Hot's a stop sign. I know what it costs when I ignore it."

He almost smiled. "Nice work in there. The hold-music smile. Benny never knew he was being read."

"Hold music is ninety percent of my professional skill set. The other ten is clicking 'Next Slide.'"

At the edge of the Honda lot she paused by a row of price-stickered Fits like a woman comparing trim levels, and turned to a fresh page. *Plate: 7K, B/8, gap, 4, R. Check for frame. Check for frost.*

Then, softer, to the empty air at her shoulder: "Abernathy. If the window opens, you tell me *before* I look."

*Deal, love,* he said on the bond. *Before, not after. Cross me heart.* A beat. *Spirits don't have one, but it's the thought.*

* * *

The Honda place was four hundred feet of glare. A kid in a lanyard delivered the Civic's safety rating in one breath. They nodded at the trunk space and took a brochure. Across the boulevard, Benny's office window showed a shape in a chair with a phone at its ear and the cord looping. He wasn't looking their way.

He was looking at the corkboard.

They left on foot by the back of the lot. Past the car wash, down the hill where Serramonte turned into cemetery fence, onto Mission.

Colma did what Colma did. Wrought iron. Cypress. A monument shop with blank granite hearts lined up in the yard. A florist with white chrysanthemums in buckets out front. The living were outnumbered about a thousand to one here, and the sidewalk knew it. It was quiet in a way traffic didn't touch.

Two blocks ahead: a green awning, the OPEN neon half lit. COLMA NOTARY & MAIL.

Her left palm warmed as she walked. It wasn't frost. It was the pencil dot, waking the way it had near Tartine. A little more with every storefront.

*Dot's warmin' faster than the walkin' explains,* Abernathy said. *Somethin' on this street is lookin' back. Not the shop yet. Wider. The whole town's got a ledger-hush on it, and I don't like that I know the word for it.*

She swept the curbs one by one, brochure in hand, doing her best impression of a woman reading about airbags. Past the florist's buckets. Past the blank hearts. Hunting beige. But the dot kept dragging her eyes toward itself, and she kept making them go back out to the cars, and the cars all came up the same: parked, nothing.

*You rolled nothin' on the curbs, so don't trust the empty,* Abernathy added. *If you see a bucket with one chrysanthemum turned the wrong way, that's his "reservation" sayin' hello. Say "Civic" and I'll back Kino's play. Eat somethin' after. I mean it.*

Kino took her hand like a man tired of window-shopping. Slowed them to a stroll. Talked about the Civic's trunk, whether her sister's baby seat would fit. His forearm was prickling all the way to the elbow and she could feel it in his grip. He let his shoulders sag, a man who'd already lost the argument about the payment plan.

Half a step before the awning he tilted his head toward the florist's buckets and murmured: "Those aren't for sale, I think. Don't look at them long."

She didn't.

She also didn't see it. It had been parked so long it had stopped looking like a car. Nose to the curb, half under the cypress drip line, pollen dusted over the beige roof till it matched the sidewalk. They walked right past its rear bumper. Kino's brochure brushed the side mirror.

Neither of them turned a head.

Then they were under the green awning, and the notary's front window gave them everything back in reverse.

Behind the taped-up USPS RATES sign, the street was mirrored. Cypress. Buckets of white chrysanthemums. A beige Camry with a dealer frame she could read backward: SERRAMONTE AUTO, DALY CITY.

Someone was in the driver's seat.

A grey sleeve. A knit cuff resting on top of the steering wheel, both hands folded there the way a man folds his hands on a church pew. He wasn't looking at them. In the reflection the head was turned toward the shop door, patient as a clock face. Where the face should be, the glass gave back a soft wash of light.

Like Benny's Polaroid.

The dot went from warm to hot all at once, like a mug someone had just filled.

Beside her, Kino's fingers closed on hers. His forearm had stopped prickling. Now it *pulled*, faint and steady, toward the curb behind them. A compass needle near iron.

And inside, behind the counter, Rodel looked up from his ledger and saw them through his own window. His face did something careful. Very slightly, he shook his head.

*Window's open, love,* said Abernathy, and for once there was no accent in it at all.

"Civic," Arenya said.

She said it bright and a little too loud, a woman who'd just remembered the Civic had the better trunk. Her palm was a coal in Kino's hand. She didn't turn around. The plate was right there in the glass, backwards and in front of her, so she took one steady half-second off the reflection and put it in her head like a stitch.

Then she pulled them left along the shop front, away from the Camry's grill, toward the cross street. She laughed at nothing. Shoulder to his shoulder.

"I want to go back and ask about the Civic's warranty," she said. "Right now. Don't look at the flowers."

Under her breath, to the street: "Don't look at me, Walt. I'm a woman who likes hatchbacks."

"Warranty. Right." Kino turned with her, no hurry. The pull dragged at his back like a leash and he didn't fight it, and he didn't look at it. He pitched his voice for the sidewalk, bored and annoyed. "Five-year, bumper to bumper, or I'm not signing." Then, nearly without moving his lips: "Cross at the corner. Past the monument yard, not back up the hill. If the engine turns over, we split. You go left, first open door. I go right and make noise."

He let Ix go ahead of them. She felt more than saw it: a low, heavy darkness in the cypress shade, something that put itself between her and the curb.

The plate had turned itself right in her head, a stitch pulled tight. **7 K B Q 4 R.** There was one more character after it. It fell behind the taped corner of the USPS sign, and the glass wouldn't give it up.

And as she took the plate, the reflection took something back.

The washed-out head in the driver's seat didn't turn toward her. It turned toward *where she had been standing in the glass.* A beat late. The way a sleeper turns toward a sound in the next room.

Then the window held only awning and street.

Behind them a small bell rang: the shop door. Rodel Panganiban stepped out onto the sidewalk in his shirtsleeves with a push broom and began sweeping cypress needles off his stretch of concrete. Slowly. Squarely. His back to them. He stood between the shop door and the curb.

He didn't call after them. He just swept.

* * *

They crossed at the corner.

The monument yard ran along the cross street, rows of polished blanks: hearts, open books, plain upright slabs. None of them had a name yet. Their reflections slid along the black granite beside them, two shopping-day strangers and a smudge of shade at their feet.

At the far curb, Kino's forearm let go.

He felt it as a snap. The pull didn't fade. It was cut, a line snipped clean, and he stumbled half a step. Arenya's hand tightened.

They turned, with no glass in the way now, and looked straight back down Mission at the florist.

The beige Camry was there. Pollen on the roof.

The driver's seat was empty.

No door had opened. Nobody walked the sidewalk but Rodel and his broom.

In the front bucket of white chrysanthemums, one bloom had turned on its stem. It faced the corner where they stood.

Her palm dropped from coal to warm, and stayed there. It wasn't cooling off like it had on BART. It was simply *in range*, of something that was no longer where she'd left it.

She didn't stop walking. She put the notebook against her hip and wrote by feel, without looking down: *7KBQ4R + ? (last char hidden, USPS sign). Camry front of florist. Pollen.* Her handwriting was awful. It would be legible.

"The empty seat is worse," she said, flat, not turning her head. "He's not in the car. He's *somewhere that isn't the car*, and my palm still thinks he's close." She flexed her fingers. Warm. Not hot. "And one flower's looking at us. I am not looking back at it."

"He doesn't need the car," Kino said. He took the curb side again, walking her away from the granite edges. "It's a post. Like the freezer. Somewhere to sit and be counted." The cut rope of the pull lay quiet in his arm. "That scares me more than him sitting there. A man who can leave his seat empty and still watch the door."

Ahead, the monument yard was a row of blank black mirrors. She kept to the middle of the sidewalk and her eyes on the traffic light.

"Next open door," she said. "Diner, funeral home, dry cleaner. Anything with a light on. We go in, sit down, I order coffee I don't drink. Then we call Malachi with a plate and a mailbox number and finally get to be annoyed at somebody who isn't Bell."

Half a block on, there was a yellow-lit diner with a handwritten PIE sign.

Kino held the door. He checked the room first. Back to the wall for him; window and exit in her eyeline.

"Coffee. Then Malachi," he said. "And Rodel swept for us. We owe him a visit, in daylight, when that Camry's gone."

* * *

LUPE'S, said the door, and nothing on the menu said otherwise.

Six booths, a counter, a pie case with one lemon meringue left and a slice already gone from it. Through the glass, the street was a yellow-tinted strip. The light changed. Nobody in a cardigan crossed.

The waitress was sixty, in orthopedic shoes, a pencil behind her ear. She didn't ask their names. She turned two mugs over and poured.

Arenya wrapped both hands around hers and let the heat do what Nana's thumb had done on her elbow. She did not intend to drink it. She opened the notebook flat and turned it so Kino could read.

*Walt Bell. Box 12, Colma Notary. 3/19, 4:51, cash, folded once. Plate 7KBQ4R+?*

Tucked flat between the pages was Malachi's napkin. SERRA—, 7K?4R, and under it, in his cramped pencil: *don't call me, I'll find you.* His phone and its SIMs were in a storm drain somewhere. There was no number to call. Only a plate, a mailbox, and a man who'd said he'd come.

"Post, not a car," she said. "You're right. So it's a post that reports. Benny got a phone call. Rodel got a window. Everything Bell touches becomes a place he can look out from." She tapped the *12*. "Hale pays in new twenties. Bell pays in old soft bills he counts in Spanish. *Whose* Spanish? Nana's? Somebody who sat at her table?"

Kino turned his mug a quarter turn and didn't drink.

"It bothers me too. *Diez, quince, veinte.* Counting by fives, out loud, onto a desk. That's not a tourist's Spanish. That's how my grandmother counts out the offering money, so the spirits can hear the sum." He said it plainly. "Or it's how something that keeps ledgers counts. The clerk spoke Spanish to me in the shed. Bell might be borrowing its voice. Or it might be lending him its books."

"Text Malachi the plate," she said. "Not a call. Just the string and 'Box 12 is Bell.' If he's alive, he'll answer. If he doesn't, that's an answer too."

"From the booth. Then power it off and put it in the sugar caddy. Nothing in your pocket." Kino looked at the window, then back at her. "And the plate has a missing character. Don't fill it in on a guess. A wrong plate to a cop friend is worse than no plate."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were thinking about it."

She was. She let it go. "Fine."

"Then after pie, I want to go back to Daly City and tell Nana about the counting. She'll know whose Spanish it is."

The waitress came back with the pot. She stopped at the table and frowned at it as if she'd miscounted. Then she took a third mug from the tray, set it right-side up at the empty place beside Arenya, and filled it.

"For your friend," she said. "He said he'd be right in."

She was already walking away.

Steam rose off the third cup, straight up. Then, very gently, it bent.

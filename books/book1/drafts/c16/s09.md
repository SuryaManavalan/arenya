Kino's car sat at the bottom of Nana's hill with the engine running and the heater blowing dust.

He had both hands on the wheel. He wasn't going anywhere yet.

Arenya buckled in, set her phone on her knee, and pulled up Ava's name. Her thumb hovered.

"Ava first," she said. "If I do it after Livermore, I'll have a better excuse to be a coward."

She pressed it. Speaker, low.

Four rings. Then the hiss of a steam wand, a grinder, and somebody calling a name that wasn't Ava's.

"Hey. I'm on. Two minutes." A pause, and the phone moved closer to her mouth. "My hand's still cold, by the way. The spot. I keep holding a cup on it and it doesn't take the heat. That's normal, right? That's a normal thing that happens." She laughed, and it was a bad laugh. "Tell me it's a normal thing."

"Hold on," Arenya said. "Can you step somewhere?"

A door thumped. The café went muffled. Under Ava's breathing, a compressor hummed. The walk-in, then.

"It's not normal," Arenya said.

She didn't dress it up. Ava would have heard that too.

"It's not fatal either, as far as I know. It's a mark. A man read your name out loud on purpose, and I'm the reason he was in the room. I used you, Ava. I needed to see what he'd do, and you were the person at the counter. I'm sorry. That's on me, not you."

She flipped the notebook open on her knee and kept the pen capped. Her hands needed something to hold.

"Here's what I need you to do. Don't hold hot cups on it. Wrap it in a clean cloth and keep it dry. If a grey cardigan, a white chrysanthemum, or a beige Camry shows up near Tartine, you don't talk to it. You call me. And tonight, when you get home, put a line of table salt across your doorway. I know how that sounds. Do it anyway."

Silence on the line. The compressor cycled.

"I'll tell you everything when I'm back," Arenya said. "Not on a phone with your steamer going. You deserve the whole thing, with the bad parts in. And if you want out of any of it, I'll help you get out."

She waited.

"Yeah," Ava said. "I figured out the first part around the time my hand went numb."

Beside her, Kino's eyes came off the road for half a second.

"Can I tell you something weird?" Ava went on. "Last night, around nine, it stopped. The cold spot. I was wiping down the bar and it just went warm, like someone let go of my wrist." Quieter: "Was that you?"

Nine o'clock. Nana's pencil.

Arenya almost said yes. It would have been so easy, and so cheap.

"No," she said. "I wish I could take that one. It was somebody else, doing something brave in a kitchen."

"Okay." A breath. "Okay. I'm keeping the salt. I'm keeping the code too. Croissant means fine, kouign-amann means call me. I'm not taking the spare bed, but text me the address. Don't say it out loud on the phone, right? That's a rule. You've got the voice of somebody with rules now."

"I do," Arenya said. "That's one."

"Good. Because the guy, the cardigan, does he—"

* * *

It was an hour east. Over the San Mateo Bridge, the long grey flat of the bay at their elbows, then 92 to 880 and the sprawl of 238 and finally 580, where the land lifted its skirts and went to gold.

Kino drove. The fog bank sat on the coast behind them like a held breath, and it did not follow.

The hills out here were brown and bare, creased with cattle trails. Nothing grew taller than a fence post. Along the ridgelines, rows of white turbines turned so slowly they looked like they were thinking about it.

The signal bar on her knee dropped to one as the road climbed.

Ava was still talking at the top of the pass. "—and Arenya, the cardigan, does he—"

Then the line clicked into silence.

**Call Failed.**

Arenya stared at it until the screen dimmed. She set the phone face-down.

"Dead zone," Kino said. "It always is, right at the summit." He didn't sound sure that was all it was.

"Nine o'clock," she said. "Nana told me to stop making it bigger." She huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh. "Ava asked if I'd let go of her wrist. I almost said yes. I don't get credit for that one."

She opened the notebook and wrote in the jolting handwriting of a moving car: *Ava: croissant = fine, kouign-amann = call. Text address. Don't say it aloud.*

Then she looked up, out Kino's window, at the ridge.

"Third from the left," she said. "It's out of step."

He didn't answer right away.

She watched it. The blades went round, all of them together, a slow white chorus. The third turned a hair behind the rest. It lagged. Then it caught up, a little too quick, the way a person turns back to look after they've already said they're leaving.

Like something glancing back.

"Tell me that's the light," Arenya said.

"It's not the light." His voice was very even. "But it's not close, either. It's a window, not a door. Bell likes windows." He eased off the gas a notch. "Don't look at it straight. Look at the road. Look at your notebook. Whatever's in that blade gets nothing from being stared at."

She looked at the notebook. The ink swam a little. She made herself read her own line about croissants until it held still.

The summit slid behind them.

"Say it," she said, not looking up. "I used a barista as bait."

He kept his eyes on the road a few seconds longer than he needed to. The turbines turned. His thumb tapped the wheel once.

"No. I won't say it. You didn't use her. You used a door, and she was standing in it." He flicked the signal and eased into the slow lane. "That's different, but not by much, and you know it. That's why you called."

A mile of road.

"Nana would've told you the same. Bait that's told the truth afterward is a lookout. Ava's a lookout now, if she wants to be. Her choice." He glanced over, brief. "You did the part most people skip. Don't make it bigger than it is, and don't make it smaller."

Arenya looked at the dark screen on her knee. She didn't say thank you, because she'd have cried, and she wasn't going to cry on the Altamont Pass.

"Lookout, not bait," she said instead. "I'm going to try to deserve that distinction."

"Nine o'clock was Nana's pencil," Kino said. "Ava's wrist let go because the entry stopped counting her. Just don't let him write her in again."

His left hand flexed on the wheel. The birthmark on the forearm above it was quiet. Neither pulling nor warm. He held onto that. She could tell by how still the rest of him was.

* * *

On the downslope, past the last of the turbines, the phone buzzed once against her knee.

The Tartine number. Sent, the stamp said, eleven minutes ago, and stuck somewhere up on the ridge until now.

**okay. salt tonight. kouign-amann if anything.**

She read it twice. She thumbed back *ok. salt tonight.* and then, underneath, the street address, and under that, *salt on the sill.*

Send.

Her palm lay open on her thigh. The 12 and the two graphite strokes sat where Nana had left them, cool and quiet. She hated how grateful she was for that.

"Two stops," Kino said. "Gas. And somewhere I can wash this road off my face." He tipped his chin at the windshield. "Daylight's ours till about six."

They stopped at a Chevron at the bottom of the grade. She bought two bottles of water and a bag of almonds she didn't want, and stood by the ice machine while a man in a Raiders cap pumped diesel into a truck with a tarp over its load. He didn't look at her. Nothing looked at her. She checked the sky like a woman checking her mirrors. Nothing glanced back.

When she got in again, Kino's face was damp and his hair was wet at the front.

"Better?"

"No," he said. "But cleaner."

Then the valley opened.

Livermore lay below in a long flat of haze, vineyards ruled in green lines, a wind sock hanging limp at the little airport. Kino took the Vasco exit and followed the hills south. The road narrowed and turned to oak and dry grass.

The temple came up on its rise above the vines.

White gopuram tiers, stacked and carved with a thousand small gods, caught the sun. The car park was half full of Hondas and minivans. A sign at the foot of the steps read *FOOTWEAR HERE* over rows of wooden cubbies, and in the cubbies, sandals and school shoes and one pair of very serious running shoes.

From inside, a bell rang once. Someone's morning puja. The sound came clean across the gravel.

Arenya knew that bell. Not this one, but the family of it. Amma's puja room in Daly City had a brass one the size of a fist, and every morning of her childhood it had gone off at six like a very small, very pious alarm.

For a moment her eyes stung.

Kino killed the engine. In the quiet, the dashboard clock ticked over to 9:20.

"Nothing's listening," he said. He was watching the temple the way he watched trails. "Not yet."

"Nana said Colma's no doesn't reach this far."

"Nana's usually right about reach."

To the left of the main steps, a smaller door stood propped open with a brick. A paper sign was taped to it, laminated against the sprinklers:

**OFFICE — ARCHANA / DONATIONS / RECEIPTS. 9–12.**

Through the gap she could see a desk, a ceiling fan turning, and a shelf of fat ledger binders labeled by year in black marker. At the desk sat an older woman with reading glasses on a chain and a cardigan.

Blue.

"Blue cardigan," Arenya murmured. "Good sign. I'm counting it."

She slipped her shoes off at the cubbies, because she was not going to start this by being rude to a god. She checked that the salt was still in both pockets. She fixed her ponytail. Then she un-fixed it, because she wanted to look like a niece, not a reporter.

"I'll talk," she said. "You hang back by the door and watch the room. Tell me if anything turns to face us."

Kino nodded once. "Ask for the receipt book by name. Priests lie out of habit. Ledgers just add."

"I'm going to be so polite to this office," she said, "they'll want to adopt me."

At the office door she knocked on the frame with two knuckles. The woman looked up over her glasses. A fan turned. Somewhere deep in the building, the bell rang again.

Arenya put on her most Daly City voice. It came up from somewhere below her ribs, the one she used on aunties at weddings and on the phone with relatives in Chennai.

"Namaste, Aunty. Sorry to bother you."

She had her hands folded. Her palm was cool against her knuckles.

"I'm looking for a receipt from March eighth. An archana, a hundred and one dollars, number 2207, in my name. Ari. Arenya Sowri." She let her voice drop where it needed to. "My aunt paid for it, and she's passed away. The family needs the paperwork. Could I please see the receipt book for that day?"

Nana drove slow and boring, like she'd been asked to.

Mission Street. A bus. A man selling mangoes off a tailgate. Three cemeteries, and then, because this was Colma's idea of a joke, four.

At the red light on the Daly City line, the dashboard clock rolled over to 12:00.

The phone in Arenya's pocket buzzed once against her hip. It didn't ring. It ticked, the way a clock ticks.

Nobody said anything about it.

* * *

The kitchen smelled the way it had yesterday: hominy, oregano, the chili Aunty had said not to be rude about. Nana shot the deadbolt, set the chain, and then, without explaining, knelt with a grunt and poured a line of salt under the back door from the blue Morton can.

She put the pot on to warm. She pulled out a chair for each of them. She sat down herself, hands folded, and did not touch the pot.

Arenya laid the phone face up on the oilcloth, between the salt shaker and a jar of Tajín. *Missed call. Unknown. 12:00 PM.* Under that, the last thing she'd opened: the photo.

"Kino," she said. "It's yours. Like I asked."

He pulled it toward him with one finger.

She watched his hands, because his face told her nothing. It never did. He read once with his thumb held off the glass. Then he read again with it on, and something went through him so quietly she might have missed it if she hadn't been counting his breaths. He took his hand back and laid it flat beside the salt. His left sleeve sat very still over his forearm.

"First time, it's what you said," he said. His voice was low and even, the voice for somebody standing too close to a drop. "Cream envelope. Typewriter caps. Your name, all of it, middle name too." A half-second glance at her. "I didn't know you had one. I'm not going to say it in here. Neither should you. *To the beneficiary. Collected in person. ID required.* Same as you told us."

He turned the phone face down with one finger, like a card he didn't want to play.

"Second time, there's another line. It wasn't there before. It's not blurry like the rest. It sits on top of the picture like frost." He looked at Nana, who would know what it meant, and then at Arenya, because it was hers. "It isn't written to you. It's written to me. To whoever reads it to you."

Nobody breathed.

"I'm not going to say it. I'm not hiding it. I'll write it down for Nana, or you can look yourself if you choose." His jaw worked. "But 'the one who reads it to her' is a job. If I say it out loud, in a room, with you listening, I think I take the job." He tipped his head toward nothing. "The clerk asked me *¿Y tú?* and I didn't answer. This is them asking again, in different handwriting."

Arenya noticed, with a small cold drop, that he kept glancing at his own knee. At the place where something heavy and quiet should have been resting.

"*Abuela*," he said. "Tell me I'm wrong."

Nana didn't tell him he was wrong.

* * *

She reached into the sugar bowl for the pencil stub she kept there for grocery lists, tore the back flap off a PG&E envelope, and slid both across.

"*Escríbelo.*" She tapped the paper. "Your Susmithaa said it right. Writing is not keeping. *Saying* is how they get you. You say it to her, in a room, and now you're her mail." She saw Arenya's face and softened, but didn't take a word of it back. "In Colma the old people know. When the mail comes for the dead, somebody has to sign for it. Whoever signs gets the next one too. And the next. That is what *c/o* means to them. Care of. Care is a job."

Kino wrote. Nana read it without moving her lips, folded it twice, and got up. The freezer opened with its rubber gasp. She put the paper in the same Ziploc as the grey man's card, between the frozen tortillas and a bag of peas, and shut it.

"Two things in my freezer now with their handwriting." She sat back down heavily. "I don't like it."

Then she turned the phone face up again and slid it halfway to Arenya, and stopped, her fingers still on it.

"It's your name on that envelope. Kino won't take the job. Good. Smart. But it is still your mail." She didn't let go. "You can look yourself, and then nobody read it to you. Or you leave it, and we eat, and we think. I won't say which. *Ella* would have looked." A small crooked smile. "*Ella* also told the insurance man to go to hell in three languages, so."

The pozole ticked on the stove.

Arenya put two fingers on the phone, beside Nana's. She did not pull it closer.

Every instinct she owned was pointing at it. Eleven years of listicles, and before that a whole life of wanting to be the girl who looked. The cracks in the world where truth leaked out. Here was one, lying on an oilcloth with a rooster print.

*Don't verify,* Aunty had written. In her own hand, in her own kitchen, in her last week.

"Looking is verifying," Arenya said. Her voice came out clipped. She made it slower. "I think looking is the first stroke. And I think whatever's on that screen knows it." She slid the phone back to the middle, face down, and set the salt shaker on top of it like a paperweight. It wasn't a plan. It was what she had. "So no. Not at this table. Not with the pozole on, and not with Kino's arm that cold."

"But I'm not leaving it," she added. "Mail that gets ignored just keeps coming." To Kino: "Thank you for not reading it out. I know what that cost you." To Nana: "Eat first. Then we write down everything we *can* say out loud, in order, and see what shape the hole is. I'm good at holes. It's what I do."

Under the table her left palm was cold. She closed her fist on the 12 and kept it there.

*Good call, love,* Abernathy said, close in her ear, from the chair nobody else could see. *Salt shaker on it. Daft, and right.*

*When Kino turned that phone over I got brass again. Not hot this time. Cooling, like a pan taken off the ring. And wet ink under it, same ink as the notary's box. Whoever wrote that second line wrote it to be* read. *It wants a reader more than it wants you.*

A pause, with a little of the old register in it.

*So don't look today. Not because it's a trap, though it is. Because you've got a clerk's pencil dot in your palm already. Don't hand him a second mark to line up with it.*

*And since I owe you. That voice, the one that said* mark it so it can be found again. *It sounded like me. I can't tell you more, and I'll not dress it up. Someone built a wall round it, and I don't know if it was them or me. It's not a nice thing to not know.*

She didn't answer. She opened her fist a quarter inch, to let him know she'd heard, and closed it again.

* * *

They ate.

Nana ladled the pozole into the good bowls and set down radish, cabbage, lime, the dried chili in its little dish. She watched Arenya's spoon until the first mouthful went in, and only then picked up her own. It was very good, and very hot. Kino ate like a man who'd hiked since dawn, with his cold arm in his lap.

For six minutes nobody talked.

It was the best six minutes of the week.

Then the bowls were pushed back, and Nana brought out a yellow legal pad from the drawer with the batteries and the rubber bands, and two pens, because she didn't trust one.

At 12:41 the phone under the shaker buzzed once.

The screen didn't light. The shaker didn't rattle. But the spilled grains around its base had gone dull and grey, like salt on a winter windshield, in a perfect circle the size of the phone.

Nana saw it. She put a fresh pinch on top of the grey and said, "Write, mija. In order."

Arenya wrote the way she always did: numbered, block capitals, no adjectives. Adjectives are where people hide.

**1. MARCH 9.** MERIDIAN LETTER. AUNTY = 11 (INSURED). ME = 12 (BENEFICIARY). SHE NEVER BOUGHT A POLICY.
**2. MARCH 19, MORNING.** AUNTY'S DECLARATION AT RODEL'S. NO CONTRACT, NO AGREEMENT, NO UNDERSTANDING. THREE WAYS. APPA WITNESSED.
**3. MARCH 19, ONE HOUR LATER.** HALE RENTS BOX 112. CASH. A YEAR.
**4. TODAY.** BOX LEFT OPEN. ENVELOPE INSIDE, ADDRESSED TO ME. BELL FILED FOR THE 03/19 JOURNAL AND WALKED IN AFTER US.
**5. YARD.** ROW 11, AUNTY'S NO IN THE MARGIN. ROW 12, ONE STROKE, WAITING. CLERK SAID *FALTA UNA.*

She stopped, tapped the pen on the pad, and drew a box under line 5. She left it empty.

"Here's the hole. Someone made Aunty row eleven *before* she died. March 9, she's already in the book. Nobody counts a person that early unless they plan to be the cause, or plan to be right." She glanced at Kino. "What did I miss? You heard the voice at the shed. I only heard you describe it."

He read her list upside down, then turned the pad a quarter toward him and took the second pen.

"You didn't miss anything. You found the thing that matters." He tapped the box. "The clerk said *doce* and *falta una* like it was reading a bill. It wasn't surprised. A clerk doesn't enter what hasn't happened. So either somebody told it ahead of time, or somebody who *reads* its books wrote the plan into them." Quiet. "*Los que cuentan.* They don't hunt, they collect. March 9 is when they put the debt on the page."

Under line 3 he wrote, in a plain trail-log hand: **HALE = same dry count as the voice on the voicemails. Older than Hale. He's the mouthpiece.** Under line 4: **BELL watches. Box left open = bait, not a mistake.**

"It buzzed at 12:41, not 12:00," he said, looking at the grey ring. "They aren't just waiting for you to sign. They're keeping time on us. Which means they're listening, or reading something in this house." His eyes went toward the freezer once, and away. "Nana's two things with their handwriting. I'd want to know if cold paper counts as a post office."

He added a last line without being asked, and boxed it beside hers.

**WHO TOLD THEM MARCH 9?**

"That's the hole," he said. "Not what they want from you. Who gave them the date."

* * *

Nana was out of her chair before he finished the word *office*.

The freezer light was yellow and ordinary. The peas were fine. The tortillas were fine. The Ziploc was not. Its inside had grown a fur of white frost so thick you couldn't read the card through it, and when she lifted it out it was heavier than paper had any right to be, the zipper frozen shut.

She didn't open it. She set it in a saucer on the counter and poured salt over it until it was a small white hill. Then she stood with the blue can in her hand and looked at the two of them like she was waiting to be scolded.

"I put it in there because cold keeps things," she said. "My mother did that with letters she didn't want to answer." She shook her head once. "*Pendeja.* Okay. Where does it go, then? Not back in the house's cold."

She turned, not to the freezer, but to the wall by the phone jack, where a free parish calendar from Our Lady of Mercy hung on a nail, a saint for every month and the Sundays in red. She lifted October. September. Back and back with one finger until she reached MARZO, and held the pages flat against the wall with her palm.

She said nothing. She just held it there so Arenya could see the numbers.

Monday the 9th was in black. The Sunday before it, the second Sunday in March, was in red.

**8.**

The pink temple receipt was folded against Amma's chest by now. But Arenya had read it herself, in the bookroom, with the dust. *Shiva-Vishnu Temple, Livermore. Archana, 1. Second Sunday in March.* Name line: *S. Sowri.* And under *Nakshatra,* Arenya's own birth star.

A priest reads those lines aloud. That's what an archana is. A name and a star, said into a room.

Arenya looked at the red 8 for a long time.

Then she wrote it in the empty box, slowly, so her hand wouldn't shake.

"March eighth." Her voice had gone flat. "Aunty went to Livermore the day before the letter. She had them say my name and my star out loud, in a room, for her. An archana." She put the pen down. "She wasn't signing anything. She was *praying* for me. And somebody heard it."

She made herself keep going. "A priest says a name and a star into a temple, and next morning a man in a suit has both. Not from the Lattice. From the receipt, or from the room." Her throat tightened. "Her kindness was the leak. It's not a hole. It's a door she held open for me."

Nobody said *I'm sorry.* Nana's hand came across the table and covered hers, the closed one, and stayed.

After a bit Arenya said, "Don't put it back in the cold. Salt's working, so keep it buried, but it has to leave the house. Amma's temple. Somewhere that's been saying names for a hundred years and knows how to *finish* one." She looked at Kino. "Or is that me wanting a clean answer? Tell me if I'm reaching."

He took his time. He looked at the salt darkening from the bottom on the saucer, then at her fist.

"You're not reaching about the door. That's the best read on the table." He said it plain, the way he'd confirm a track. "She didn't leak it, Ari. She *gave* it, to a god, for you. Somebody was just standing at the back of the room with a pencil."

Ari. Nobody called her that but her aunts, and he knew it.

"Where it goes, though. I'd slow down. A temple isn't neutral ground. It's full of names, and now we know somebody reads them. Carry a frozen letter in there, to people who don't know what it is, and you could put it in front of a hundred more. And it's Amma's place. She's already carrying you. I wouldn't hand her this without asking." He looked at Nana. "I'd do it the mountain's way. Running water and open air, away from anybody's name. The Pacifica pier, tide going out. Whatever it is, it goes where it can't be anybody's mail. I'm not sure it works. Tell me if the old people did it different." He rubbed his cold forearm, finally. "And I'd go. In daylight, with Ix."

Quieter, to her: "Clean answers aren't wrong because you want them. They're wrong if you stop checking after you get one. You're still checking. Keep going."

* * *

The calendar fell shut. March, April, all the saints slapped back down to October.

"*Agua que corre,*" Nana said. "Yes. My mother did that. Letters from a man in Jalisco she should not have been writing to. Into the river at Guadalajara, every one, one Holy Saturday." She almost smiled. "But she had a rule. You only carry *your own* letter to the water. You carry somebody else's, now you're the mailman. Like the boy said. The job."

She pointed the salt can at the white hill on the counter. "Two things in there. One is the boy's own writing. He takes that to the ocean himself. The other is the grey man's card. That is not ours to drown. That's *his.*"

She sat. She put a finger on the legal pad, on Kino's line. *BELL watches.*

"Mija. The grey man wrote his own address at the notary, yes? A post office box. Here in Daly City." Her eyes went hard and bright, the look of a woman who had fought utility companies for forty years. "My mother's other way, for men she didn't want: you don't throw it away. You write *REHUSADO* across the front. Refused. You send it back to him in the mail, with a stamp. Then it's on his paper, not yours. A no he has to *receive.*"

The brightness dimmed. She was honest. "But then he knows we know his box. And he knows somebody in this house can write. That's not hiding anymore. That's answering."

On the saucer, the salt had gone grey a third of the way up. Out on the street a car went slowly *down* the hill, the same slow car as before, and this time Kino, standing at the edge of the window, saw it.

"Beige sedan," he said. "Doesn't stop."

The brake lights tapped once at the corner. The way you'd tap a pen.

Arenya didn't go near the glass. "Kino takes his own page to the water," she said, fast, before she could argue herself out of it. "Pacifica, daylight, Ix. Yes. Nobody carries mine. Mine's the envelope, and I'm not touching that."

She looked at the saucer. "REHUSADO is a good idea, Nana, and I want to do it. But the card is a tracker. I'm not putting it in a mailbox with our handwriting and the Daly City post office stamped on it. That isn't answering. That's handing him a receipt."

She wrote in the margin: **BEIGE CAMRY, 12:5x, DOWN THE HILL, BRAKE-TAP.**

"Not mail. In person, and not by me." She breathed out. "Malachi calls tomorrow, once. He says he's government, and he knows what Bell is. I don't trust him. But he's a man who wants to be useful. Let him carry the card back. It's his problem to deliver. We're not the ones signing." Then: "Right now the car's the problem. Nana, is there a door that isn't the salted one? I'd like his plate before he sees us leave."

She noticed she'd stopped hiding her hand. She was gripping Nana's pen with the other one like it could bite.

* * *

Kino was already off his chair. He didn't go to the glass. He stood to the side of the frame with the curtain edge between him and the street, the way you stand at a ranger-station window when a vehicle has worked the lot too long.

"Don't run for the back," he said. "That's what he wants. Somebody bolting where he can see it. He's tapping the corner, not blocking it. He's telling us he's there." He looked at the pad. "You already wrote the time. Good. Let me get the plate. I look like a guy who lives on this block. Nana, give me the trash. Any trash."

Nana handed him a grey bag, tied, heavy with pozole bones. He pushed his hood down and pocketed his keys. "I take it out like it's Wednesday. I look at the car like it's rude, then at the curb like I'm annoyed at the curb. I read the plate in the second I'm looking at nothing." At the door he paused. "Ari, stay off the glass. Nana, if I'm not back in two minutes, honk twice from the window, like at Colma." Almost a smile. "And your idea about Malachi is a good one. The card's his to carry. I won't touch hers."

The chain slid. The deadbolt turned. He stepped out with the garbage and nothing on his mind, and Arenya, at the end of the hallway, felt the missing weight at his knee like a cold spot in a room.

Nana, behind her, was whispering. "The garage. Through the laundry, the side door to the yard. No salt there." A breath. "I didn't think to." Then the blue can, and the hiss of salt on concrete.

Arenya stood in the hall and counted. She could see only a slice of the street: bins at the curb because it was Wednesday, the fog pulled back to the ridge, wind. A man in a black hoodie, walking a bag to the bin like a man who hated Wednesdays.

The lid dropped harder than it needed to. His boot straightened somebody's tipped recycling. He looked downhill, at nothing, at the corner, at the sky. His eyes crossed the back bumper of a beige car and kept moving.

He shook out his left arm like it had fallen asleep and came back up the walk at the pace of a man who wanted his lunch.

* * *

"Seven K," he said, when the chain was back on. "Then a letter, B or an 8, the glare ate it. A gap. A four. And the R." He tapped Arenya's wrist, the left, where she'd already written *7K_R* in ballpoint two nights ago. "Same R. Dealer's frame, chrome. SERRAMONTE AUTO."

"Half a plate and a dealership is a sentence I can finish," Arenya said. "I can call Serramonte Auto. Or Saiesh could find out who sold a beige Camry in that frame." She stopped. "Not Saiesh yet. Later."

Kino opened his mouth, and the side yard spoke.

It wasn't a sound. It was the lack of one: the wind dropping off, all at once, in the narrow concrete strip between Nana's house and the neighbor's. In the laundry the salt stopped pouring.

Then, in a voice Arenya had never heard her use: "*Ay, no.* Mija. Come see. From the door. Don't step."

They came to the laundry doorway and looked.

Across the side path, eight inches off the ground, a line had been ruled from the fence post to the gas meter. It was thinner than fishing line and white as hoarfrost, perfectly level, ruled the way the blanks in the stone yard had been ruled. It ran straight across the doorstep of the one door nobody had salted. The kind of thing you'd walk through in a hurry and never feel until your ankle went cold.

Outside, the beige car's engine note changed. It pulled away from the curb without hurry, signaled left at the corner like a man taking a driving test, and was gone toward Mission. Its brakes didn't tap this time.

Nana stood in the doorway with the blue can in both hands. "In my house," she said quietly. "He drew on my house."

Arenya stopped a full step back from the sill, toes behind the salt, and crouched to look along the line at eye level without touching the air near it. Level. Not a hair off. She hated how much she respected that.

"Don't step. Don't cut it. Don't pour on it." Quietly. "Cutting is a mark too. A ruled line is a ledger line. Somebody is counting the doors in this house."

She photographed it from where she was, no flash, three angles, and a fourth with the gas meter in frame so the position was on record. Then she went back to the pad and wrote: **7K ? ? 4 R. SERRAMONTE AUTO. FROST LINE, SIDE DOOR, FENCE TO METER. 12:5x.**

Kino had come in behind her. He looked at the line once, the way he'd look at a snake on a trail, noted where it was, and looked away. "Ix described one just like this at Serramonte. It isn't a trap that bites. It's a count. Walk through it and it enters you." He put a hand on Nana's shoulder, lightly, and she let him. "Nana. You didn't miss a door. You salted every one you could think of. That's what they were counting on. It's the one you didn't think of."

"Agreed on all of it," Arenya said. "Nobody alone. Nobody out the side. Not the front either, until that thing is back at your knee."

"I'll go get it. It's in Nana's car." He flexed his forearm. "I'm not sending it through first. I'm asking it to look."

He took the pencil stub from the sugar bowl and added a line under hers, small: **SIDE DOOR LINE DRAWN BETWEEN 12:5x AND 1:00. NOBODY SALTED THE GARAGE. NANA DIDN'T FAIL. THEY KNEW.**

Nana read it. Her mouth worked. She didn't say thank you. She patted his hand twice, hard, like she was tenderizing it.

* * *

"My mother said a door that is not a door can't be counted," she said at last. "You don't argue with the line. You make it a wall."

It took ten minutes, and Kino's objections about her back, and the old dresser from the garage, the one with the stuck drawer and forty years of Christmas lights in it, walked across the inside of the side door a foot at a time. Its back sat flush to the frame. On top, Nana set a Virgen de Guadalupe candle. She didn't light it.

"There. Now it's furniture. Count furniture, *cabrón.*"

Ix came in through the front, a long low shape most people's eyes slid off of. It went straight to the laundry. It didn't go to the dresser. It went to the window over the dryer, where it could see the side path, and looked at the frost for a long time with its ears flat, nowhere near it.

Then it turned its head and looked, very deliberately, at the *other* end of the line. The gas meter. The grey PG&E box with its little dial. It stayed looking until Kino saw it too.

Arenya saw him see it. Neither of them said what it meant. They didn't know what it meant. That was the point of looking.

Ix lay down across Kino's left boot and put its chin on his instep. His forearm stayed cold. It stopped getting colder.

The afternoon went the way afternoons go when you're waiting. Nana washed the bowls and dried them and put them away in the wrong order. Arenya filled four pages of the legal pad, and drew a box on the fifth. Kino stood at the side of the window frame, watching a street that did nothing. The beige car didn't come back.

At 2:15 the salt on the saucer went grey to the top and stopped, as if it were full.

At 3:30 the garage door rolled up on the Corolla, and the four of them went out the front of a house with a dresser against one of its doors, and Arenya kept her left hand shut the whole way down the walk.

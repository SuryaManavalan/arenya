The door of Tartine swung shut behind them without a sound.

No chime. Arenya noticed it the way you notice a missing tooth. For six years that bell had announced every person who ever walked in or out, and now it hung there on its little spring, polite and mute, like it had decided to stop speaking to anyone.

Guerrero Street was wet with fog. Morning traffic hissed over it.

She looked back once. She'd told herself she wouldn't.

Through the front window the corner table was lit like a display case. Mr. Bell sat with his cardigan cuffs folded exactly once and his hands flat on either side of a coffee he hadn't touched. He wasn't watching them leave. He was watching the white chrysanthemum on the counter, the way a man watches a coat he's left on a seat to save it.

*Placeholder,* she thought. *He's holding our place.*

The pencil dot on her palm throbbed. Not with her pulse. Slower, like a pen tapping on a desk while someone waited for you to finish your sentence. She walked half a block with it, past the 18th Street corner, and it faded to a faint warmth, and by the laundromat it was gone.

The frost twelve above it didn't fade at all.

Her phone buzzed in her fist. Ava.

**he's still here. not eating. it's 7:22. am i ok to stay**

* * *

She stopped under the laundromat awning. The place breathed warm lint through its propped door, and somewhere in the back a dryer was drumming somebody's sneakers. *Thunk. Thunk.* Uneven. It was the only thing on the block with no rhythm to it, and she found she was grateful.

She thumbed out a reply with her eyes on her palm.

*Stay if you can stand it. Palm on something old. If he speaks to you again, say "I'm working," and nothing else. I'm sorry about three o'clock. I'll explain it properly. Not by text.*

Send. Then she turned to Kino.

He'd gone on a few steps and stopped with his back to the wall, the way he did, watching the street both ways before he let himself look at anything else. Something low and heavy and spotted-dark pressed itself against his shin. She didn't look at it directly. You didn't, in the mornings.

"Okay," she said. "Rules of the morning. He knows when we'll be back. So we're not coming back on his schedule."

She flipped the notebook open to Malachi's napkin and tapped it. "Serramonte Auto. A dealership. Which means a service bay, a paperwork trail, and a salesman who will talk to a woman who says she's *thinking* about a Camry." She tapped again. "Bell's plate is the only thing we have that isn't a name. Somebody's name is on that car. I want it. I'm tired of everything in this story being unsigned."

A beat.

"You tell me if that's stupid. Thing about names is, he reads them. So I'll leave mine off everything. I'd rather you didn't give yours either."

*Abernathy,* she added, down the bond, *if you've got an opinion about the wet-ink smell, now's the time.*

Nothing yet. Just the dryer.

Kino took the street in one more time before he answered. "Tell Ava yes, stay. Two o'clock, public, salt in her pocket. Nobody does anything to a woman in her own kitchen with forty people watching. Tell her to text a pastry every half hour. If one's late, we come back through the front door."

"A pastry."

"Whatever's in the case. Different one each time, so we know it's her."

That was Kino. He heard *scared* and built a fence.

He took the coffee sleeve out of his inside pocket with two fingers and held it a little away from his body. "This goes to Nana. Not the freezer. She'll know what a 1912 dime wants. Then we go to the house, and you tell me what you're deciding about the second stroke, Arenya. Out loud. Before Friday. I'm not choosing for you. I just don't want you choosing alone at 7:39."

"Not stupid, by the way," he added. "The plate." He checked the street again. "A plate nobody issued means somebody made it up. Made-up things still need a place to get made. A dealer frame is a place. Good."

"Wow. Bronze medal."

"Two rules." He ignored that. "Daylight, and nobody. I'm a ranger looking at a used truck. You're thinking about a Camry. No card, no number, no name. If the salesman asks, I'm Joe. You're whoever you want, as long as it isn't real."

"Joe."

"It's a good name. Nobody remembers a Joe."

* * *

The dryer thudded. Her phone buzzed. **working.** A minute later: **croissant.** No punctuation. Ava had taken to the code like it was a shift.

Down the block the corner table was a grey shape that hadn't moved.

The dot on her palm was still warm. Not throbbing now. A low steady pulse sitting under the frost, like a second heartbeat that had all day.

*He's in range,* she thought. *And he isn't in a hurry.*

Kino's sleeve had gone damp at the bottom.

She saw it because he'd lifted it to check. A ring of condensation had soaked through the cardboard, perfectly round, exactly the size of a dime. The morning wasn't cold enough for that. Not even close. The low dark thing at his knee sniffed it once, then turned its face away like a patient refusing a spoon.

"That's enough for me," she said.

She pulled her scarf off her neck and held it out, not touching the sleeve. "Don't put it back in your pocket. Wrap it in this, then the napkin. Carry it by the cloth. Cardboard's already losing."

He took the scarf by one end. Three turns around the sleeve, then the napkin over that, then the bundle went inside his jacket on the side away from his forearm. His birthmark gave something like a slow warm pulse. She saw it in his face, a flicker, like a hand had settled on the back of his neck.

"Wet, not cold," he said quietly. "Cold would be a threat. Wet is a signature. Something is writing itself on the sleeve."

She almost laughed. She wrote it down: *Dime: leaking. Not cold, wet. Ink?* And underlined it twice.

"Now put the notebook away." Kino's voice was mild. "We're not reading anything he can see over our shoulder."

She put it in her bag.

Then the bond opened, and he was there. She felt him before she heard him, the hot-brass smell, a shabby coat that didn't move with the wind.

*Oi. Wet ink, you asked, so here's me being useful for once, love.*

*It ain't leakin', it's* answerin'*. That dime's got a ring of damp the exact size of itself, in a morning that don't call for it. Somethin' old that's been* kept *sweats when it's been looked at. Cold things in a warm pocket don't do that. Things that've been told where they are do. Ix knows it, and Ix is smarter than the two of us put together. Scarf's right. Cloth, not card. Carry it by the corner and don't let it sit against skin or your phone.*

Ix. She let herself have the name, just in her head. It was less frightening with a name.

*Now the bit I don't like, guv. 1912's a long time to hang on to one dime. He didn't* pay *with it, he* spent a marker. *Somebody wanted it in Kino's pocket. Don't chuck it in the bay, that'd be a post, same as his note. Nana'll know. Let her.*

*And the dot on your hand. It stopped when we cleared the laundromat, and it come back warm and patient when the dime started sweatin'. It ain't the dime lookin' at you. It's him, usin' the dime like a window.*

A pause. For a half second the voice was lower and older and had no cockney in it at all.

*Last thing. Daly City. I don't love it, and I can't say why, which I* also *don't love. Eat somethin' that ain't a bun, yeah? Union rules.*

She stood very still under the awning.

*A window,* she sent back. *Great. I've been looked through by a man who doesn't breathe, and the dime is his binoculars. Thank you, that's very comforting.*

*Okay. Nana, not the bay. Agreed. And I'll eat something that isn't a bun, but if you say "union rules" again I'm filing a grievance. Is your not-loving Daly City a feeling, or a memory? Because yours is the one memory somebody walled off, and I'd like to know if the wall has a door on that side of the county.*

He didn't answer. She hadn't really thought he would.

* * *

Kino was already looking at her, patient, the way he waited for deer.

"He can reach a pocket from a table," she said. "Fine. Then we stop carrying things he can reach." It came out drier than she felt. "Add that to the list of sentences I never thought I'd say in a listicle."

"Out loud," he said. "You said you would."

"Right." She breathed in lint and fog and the faint sweet scorch of the dryer. "Okay. Two options for row twelve."

She held up one finger. "A second witness. But I don't let anyone volunteer. I'm not putting a name on that row because somebody loved me too fast. So that's out, unless it's somebody who already stands in it."

A second finger. "Or I un-say the archana. In that temple room, with Hale's *other listeners* sitting there." She stopped. Started again. "I don't trust the man. But I trust that he wasn't lying about the listeners. That's the one that scares me."

"Which way are you leaning?"

"Livermore." It came out smaller than she meant it to. "Not today. After we know who owns that Camry, because if Bell's car has a name, so does whoever sat in that room. Nothing's decided until I tell you at 7:39. Deal? You hear it first. You're allowed to say I'm wrong."

"Deal." Kino looked at the street, at nothing, at Ix. "You hear it from me first if I think you're wrong. And you hear it from me if I think you're right."

Then, after a moment: "On the temple room. If you go, you don't go as the only one who can hear. Nana comes, or I do, or both. Listeners in a room works both ways. A room full of their people can also be full of ours."

She nodded too fast.

"Both of you, then. Nana and you, if she'll climb into a car for a temple." She swallowed. "Nobody sits in that room alone with my name in it again."

He took that without comment, which was the kindest thing he could have done.

She sent Ava one more text, *kouign-amann, thirty minutes,* because her thumbs wanted something to do. Nothing with her name. Nothing with his.

"And Daly City is my family's backyard," she added. "If a salesman says my aunt's name, we leave."

"Agreed." The corner of his mouth moved. "You'll hear it from me."

* * *

They took the 24th Street stairs down into the station's warm breath. Ix got two looks and one smile, from a man in a hospital lanyard who clearly believed what he'd seen was a very large, very well-behaved shadow of a dog and had decided not to push it. The train south was half-empty, commuters going the wrong way, everyone holding their phones like small hot stones.

Somewhere past Glen Park, where the tracks climbed out of the tunnel into flat white daylight, the dot on Arenya's palm went cool.

It didn't fade. It cooled all at once, like a hand lifted off a table.

Across from her Kino's shoulders dropped half an inch. The bundle in his jacket had done the same thing. The damp was suddenly just damp.

Neither of them said anything. She kept her palm turned up in her lap, waiting for the warmth to come back.

It didn't.

At Balboa Park her phone buzzed. **kouign-amann.** Then, a minute behind schedule, in a cluster:

**he left**

**didn't eat. took nothing**

**left a tip**

She read it twice. A man who'd read Ava's name on a tag and put a cold circle in her hand had left a *tip*. Of course he had. Mr. Bell was the kind of person who tipped.

The fog over Daly City was burning off in strips, the hills coming up green and white like a bad photocopy. By eight they were climbing Nana's front steps under the lemon tree. The porch light was still on from the night before, a small yellow bulb against the daylight. And through the screen came the smell of dried chiles blistering on a comal, smoky and sweet and so entirely, aggressively *alive* that Arenya stopped on the top step with her hand on the rail.

"Real breakfast," Kino said behind her. "Nana's going to feed you whether you asked or not."

"That's a rule?"

"No." He reached past her and opened the screen. "That's just Nana."

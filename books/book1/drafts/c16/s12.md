The fog beat them to Daly City.

It came over the ridge in one slow grey sheet, and the row houses on the hill had their porch lights on at four-thirty. Appa's Corolla wasn't in the driveway. Behind the house, Aunty's cottage sat dark, curtains drawn the way she'd left them.

Amma opened the door before Arenya could knock. House sari, cardigan, reading glasses she pretended not to need pushed up into her hair.

"You drove to *Livermore*," she said, which was how she said hello. To Kino: "Sit. There's rice."

There was rice. There was sambar and two kinds of curd, and a steel plate already set at the place Kino had sat last time, as if she'd heard them coming over the pass.

In the puja alcove the brass lamp was lit. Its wick was trimmed short. The flame stood dead upright, though the kitchen window was open a crack.

On the table lay a manila folder. **SUSI — ACCOUNTS**, in Amma's capitals. Her hand rested flat on top of it. With the other, she wiped a counter that was already clean.

"Appa went for washers," she said. "For the tap. He'll be back." She didn't look at the clock. "So. Eat first. Then you tell me why my sister's temple called this house today asking about a check."

Arenya sat. She ate one bite of rice, because refusing Amma's rice was a declaration of war and she wasn't ready for war yet. The notebook stayed shut in her bag. She put both hands flat on the table where her mother could see them.

"Amma. Eat first, fine. But I'm not going to lie to you while I chew." She nodded at the folder. "Check 2207. A hundred and one dollars, cashed by a man who isn't the Livermore priest. The image says someone endorsed it. I'm not accusing you. I want to know whose name is on the back, and whether you knew it when you hid that folder."

Quieter: "You said *my sister's temple*. Not *our* temple. Why?"

Across from her, Kino watched Amma's hands. The one pinned on the folder. The one wiping.

At *2207*, the wiping hand stopped.

It didn't slow. It stopped, mid-circle, on a patch of counter that had been clean an hour ago. The rag's corner twitched once. Then Amma folded it into a neat square, which was what she did when she had decided something.

"Because she chose it," Amma said to the rag. "Ours is four streets away. Everybody's aunty is at ours. Your aunty drove one hour to Livermore so nobody from Daly City would watch her ask God for something." She looked up. Her eyes were dry and furious. "She was my sister. Twenty-nine years. You don't get to ask me why with that face."

She slid the folder to the middle of the table, beside the rice, and opened it.

The March statement. The check images printed small at the bottom. The front of 2207, in Aunty's round, careful hand: *for Ari.* And the back.

**R. Sowri.** Ruler-straight strokes, every letter built from right angles, the last *i* closing on a small bracket-shaped hook. Beneath it, same hand: *Pay to the order of H. Dalal.*

Arenya's notebook held half of that hook. She would have known it anywhere.

"That is not your Appa's signature," Amma said. "Your Appa signs like an engineer. Small, leaning left, lifts his pen like it costs money. I knew in one second." Her thumb pressed the corner flat. "So I asked him. The night after the funeral. And your father, who cannot lie to the gas man about the meter, said, *Leave it, Lakshmi. It's handled.*"

Her voice caught. She made it uncatch.

"Thirty-four years. First time I didn't believe him. So I hid it. From him, not from you."

Arenya didn't apologize for the face. She turned the statement a quarter turn so the back of the check faced them both, and put one finger beside the hook without touching it.

"I'm not asking with that face because I think you did it. I'm asking because you're the only one in this family who saw it and said something." It came out rougher than she meant. "I'm sorry about Aunty. I'm sorry I made it sound like a case."

She slid the notebook half out of the bag and showed her the single hooked line she'd copied in Livermore. Same bracket. Same right angles.

"That's the visitor's hand, from the temple register. The same man signed Appa's name on Aunty's check and made it payable to H. Dalal." A breath. "Amma, Appa's work shoes are by the garage door, toes out. Where is he really?"

Kino hadn't looked at the garage door. He'd looked at the shoes. You set boots toes-out when you meant to leave in a hurry, or wanted someone to think you already had.

"Those weren't there when we came in the front," he said quietly. "Did you see him go, Amma, or only hear the door?"

"Washers was true." Amma's eyes went down the hall and stayed there. "Hardware store on Westlake. He left at twelve-thirty in his good shoes. *Those* were on the rack in the garage this morning. I didn't put them there. I didn't hear the door." Her mouth flattened. "And *handled*, he said, like the man from the insurance was a leaking pipe. I said, handled by who, Raghavan? He said, *by someone who owed her.* Then he went and watched cricket with the sound off."

The rice cooker clicked over to *warm*.

Through the wall, where the side yard ran along the house, Kino heard a small mechanical ticking. Too even for a clock. A dial turning with nobody watching it.

He shut his eyes and turned his head to the plaster. Each time he aimed at the sound, it slid away: in the wall, down the hall, somewhere much closer. Then he understood why. The cold rectangle over his sternum was ticking too, in time with the dial, and his own heartbeat sat half a beat behind both. For a second the kitchen tilted, as if he were the meter.

"Does anything in the side yard turn by itself?" he murmured. "A timer, a sprinkler?"

"The gas meter," Amma said, very quietly. "By the garage door. Your Appa reads it every Sunday and writes it in a little book. Thirty years at PG&E, he doesn't trust their numbers." She was watching Kino's face now. "It shouldn't be turning. The stove is off. The heater is off. The rice is electric."

The moment the word *meter* was spoken in the kitchen, the ticking stopped. In the wall. In Kino's chest.

The silence after was worse. It was the silence of something that had already counted what it came to count.

In the alcove, the lamp leaned, very slightly, toward the hall.

Arenya was on her feet before she'd decided to be. She put a hand on Kino's shoulder to check he was still the one in the chair.

"Heartbeat. Yours or the room's?"

"Mine." He pressed two fingers to his wrist. "Half a beat late. But mine. The room's stopped."

"Amma, stay at the table. Don't touch the folder. Don't say his name again. Please."

She took a pinch of Nana's salt from her pocket and laid a short line across the kitchen doorway where it met the hall, the way Nana had on the sill. Kino laid his behind it, a finger's width back. His grains lay a little crooked. His hand had shaken.

Amma sat down on the far side of the table without being asked twice. She folded her hands over the rag. Her lips moved, and it wasn't English.

"Three keys," she said between verses. "Mine. His. And your aunty's, on the red string. After the funeral I looked for it in her purse. It wasn't there."

Arenya went to the window over the sink. She didn't lean into the gap. She looked.

The side yard in a strip. The grey gas meter on its pipe by the garage door. Its glass face was white with frost, from the inside, the four little dials hidden behind it. In October sun. And from its base a hairline of the same white ran across the concrete and up to the garage door handle. Thinner than a pencil line. Straight as a ruler.

The same ruler that ran to Nana's side door.

Wedged between the meter and the pipe was Appa's meter book, a small green ledger with a pen clipped to it. The wind had opened it. She could read the last entry from here, because her father wrote small and very neatly, leaning left: today's date, *3:12 p.m.*, a reading. And under the reading, three words pressed so hard into the paper that they'd torn it.

*Ari does not sign.*

"Reading it out loud," she said, and her voice only wobbled on the last word. "Appa's meter book. Three-twelve p.m., his handwriting, pressed so hard it tore. *Ari does not sign.*" She swallowed. "He wasn't writing about a meter."

"Look at the pressure," Kino said. "That isn't a threat. Your father is on our side, and somebody made him write it where only the meter would hold it." He moved with them, not ahead: Amma's chair between her and the hall, himself at the salt with his back to the window, Arenya on his left, away from the glass. "Keep praying, Amma. The lamp's been telling us where to look."

Half a minute later, the frost on the meter began to clear from the top down, as if someone on the other side had started to wipe it.

It cleared one dial at a time. She read each as it came.

"Two." Dead still. "Two." "Zero." "Seven."

*2207.*

In the green book, Appa's reading was *4471*, the ordinary number he'd written every Sunday for thirty years. The dials didn't say that now. They said the number of Aunty's check, and no needle moved.

Then the smallest needle, on the right, gave one tiny tick. As if someone had drawn a single pencil stroke.

Under her hand, on the bag, the notebook ticked back. She felt it go heavier. Just slightly. Just enough.

Behind her, the lamp leaned toward the hall. Kino's heartbeat slipped another quarter-beat.

"It's not your name," Kino said, counting. "It's the check. Somebody's reading us the receipt. It wants us to flinch, or answer." A pause. "I'm a quarter further behind. Four now. Still mine."

Arenya took her hand off the bag like it was a stove burner, and folded both arms across her chest to keep them out of trouble.

"The small needle ticked once. One stroke. Nothing since." She didn't turn from the glass. "A receipt needs someone to take it. Nobody takes it."

They waited it out. Amma's verses. The faint hum of the rice cooker. Kino counting under his breath.

The needle didn't tick again. The frost finished clearing, and there was nothing behind the glass. No face. Only dials. Then, one at a time and without a sound, the needles slid backward through the numbers to *4471*. On the concrete, the ruler-straight line broke in the middle like a snapped thread, and the sun took both halves in under a minute.

Nobody had accepted the receipt, so the meter took it back.

The notebook didn't take its weight back. It stayed one stroke heavier.

The lamp straightened. The flame stood tall.

Then the front door: a key in the lock, the rattle of the chain Amma never used, a plastic hardware-store bag rustling.

"Lakshmi? The washers were the wrong size, so I got both—"

Appa stopped in the kitchen doorway, on the far side of two lines of salt, in his good shoes. A slight grey man in a cardigan who had always looked faintly apologetic. He looked at the salt. At Kino standing guard over it. At the open folder with the endorsement facing up.

Something left his shoulders, and it wasn't relief.

Wound twice around the first finger of his right hand, as if he'd been worrying it the whole drive, hung a brass key on a faded red string.

"So," Raghavan Sowri said quietly to his daughter. He did not step over the salt. "She showed you."

"Don't." It came out sharper than she meant, and she watched it land. "Don't step over it, Appa. Not yet. Just stand there."

He looked small in the doorway with the hardware bag hanging off his wrist. He'd always looked like that. She had just never been scared of it before.

"Nobody showed me anything. I found it. That's my job, apparently." She nodded at the red string. "That's Aunty's key. Amma looked for it in her purse and it wasn't there. And there's a page in your meter book, in your handwriting, at three-twelve this afternoon, when you were supposedly at the hardware store. You pressed hard enough to tear it. *Ari does not sign.*"

Her voice went very flat, so it wouldn't do anything else.

"Who's H. Dalal? Who owed her? And who did you write that note for? It wasn't Amma and it wasn't me." Quieter, because it was true and she couldn't keep it in: "I'm not asking as the person who writes about poodles. I'm asking as your daughter, the one it's counting. Please don't say *it's handled.*"

Kino stood to one side of the line with his hands open, where Appa could see he wasn't a wall.

"Mr. Sowri. Thank you for stopping where you did. That line is Nana Itzel's. It isn't for you. It's for whatever walked in behind your good shoes today." One breath. "Tell us the truth in front of Amma."

Appa set the hardware bag down on his side, carefully, as if it might hold something fragile. It held washers.

"In order," he said. It was how he'd always answered her, engineer's habits even when frightened. "One. I don't know who H. Dalal is. In March a man telephoned. He said he was from the Livermore temple. Very polite. American. He said *Sowri-garu* correctly, which nobody does. He said, *Your sister has offered dakshina for the visiting pandit. Does the family approve?* And I was at my desk with the cricket on, and I said—"

His mouth worked.

"*Whatever my sister wants is fine.* That was all. Then the check went to him, *per family.* I was the family. I said yes for her without knowing what I was saying yes to. She never blamed me. Your Amma doesn't know that part."

He didn't look at Amma. Amma looked at him.

"Two. Who owed her: Rodel. The notary, from the flower shop. Twenty years ago his mother was dying at Seton, and Susmithaa sat with her nine nights when he could not get off his shift. After the funeral he telephoned me himself. He said, *Mr. Sowri, they used my number, I am sorry, I will handle the check. But you must hold her key. The side door is the one they'll come to.* So I took it from her purse at the hospital. I lied to your mother. *It's handled.*" He held up the red string. "This is me handling."

His voice thinned.

"Three. Rodel said, if they come, the meter will show it first, because it counts. Write the family's no where the counting thing reads, in your own hand, and press hard. So at three o'clock I came in by the side with her key. The needles were already wrong. Two, two, zero. I wrote it. I put my shoes toes-out by the door, the way my mother did, so whatever came would walk away. Then I went and bought the wrong washers, because I did not know what else a man does."

He held the key out over the salt at arm's length and didn't let his fingers cross.

"It was for the meter, Ari. And for whoever was reading it. I'm not good at this. Your aunty was." A small, helpless breath. "Take it, or tell me where to put it."

Arenya looked at the key for a long moment. Then at Amma, who hadn't taken her eyes off him.

"Amma first," she said. "It's her sister's key." And to Appa, softer: "You didn't sign. You said yes to a phone call. That's the kind of mistake it's built to collect. It's mine to carry, not yours."

Kino waited until Amma had decided. Then, gently, the way he'd talk to a man who'd walked into the wrong canyon: "The shoes toes-out were right. And the page you tore is why that meter gave its number back." He let that land. "Did the man say his name? And on *Sowri-garu*, did his voice lean, or was it flat?"

Amma got up.

She walked to the salt and reached across it, the way you'd take a hot dish from someone, and took the key off his finger, string and all. She didn't look at it. She carried it to the alcove and laid it at the foot of the lamp, on the little brass plate where the ghee drips, so the red string coiled in the light.

The flame dipped once. In greeting, or acknowledgment. Then it stood tall again.

"There," Amma said. "Now the lamp has it. Not you, not her." She turned. "Raghavan. Step over. Wash your hands. Eat."

He stepped over both lines carefully, the way he stepped over garden hoses. Nothing happened, because nothing should. He went to the sink.

"No name," he said, under the running water, to Kino. "Perfect, but flat. Like a man reading it off a card. Every syllable the same weight. Nobody's mouth does that with our names. I noticed and did nothing." He shut off the tap. "And Rodel. I asked him how he knew. He said, *The front door of a Hindu house has a lamp. The side door has a meter. Things that count come in where things are counted.* Then he said something I didn't understand."

He dried his hands on Amma's rag, which nobody was ever allowed to do. Amma let him.

"*He asked me for one-twelve before it was empty. I should have known then.*"

Arenya stopped with her spoon in the air.

She'd stepped over the salt like a garden hose and sat where Amma pointed, and she'd been eating because her hands needed a job. Now she said it to the plate, then to the room.

"Hale rented Box 112 on March nineteenth. Somebody asked Rodel for that box before Hale ever walked in." She shook her head before anyone could answer. "Which means Hale was only the name on the form. Appa, did he say who asked?"

"Only *he*." Appa looked at the hand she'd taken away from his, as if he'd like to keep it. "Rodel said *he* the way you say it about the weather."

"Daylight, then," Arenya said. "Before the seven-forty cutoff. You, me, Rodel's own register on the counter. I'm not going alone, and not at night."

"Friday's the cutoff, not tomorrow," Kino said. "We have a day. Use it. I'll call Nana tonight and ask whose Spanish Bell counts in." He swept his own salt line into a folded napkin, crooked grains and all, and left the doorway line for Arenya. "Appa. I'd put those work shoes toes-out by the front door, too."

"I'll drive you," Appa said. "I won't go in. I'll sit in the car and read the meter on the flower shop."

It might have been a joke. In thirty years, nobody had been sure when Appa was joking.

Amma put rice on a third plate and pointed at it, at the two of them, and at the folder, which she closed.

"Eat," she said. "All of you."

They ate. Amma refilled the plates without asking. The cricket stayed off. The light through the window over the sink turned orange, then grey, and the meter in the side yard ticked along at an ordinary pace, burning gas for an ordinary stove as Amma heated rasam nobody had asked for.

Arenya's notebook stayed shut in the bag at her feet, one stroke heavier than it had been that morning. Kino's heart ran a quarter-beat behind where it should, and he didn't mention it again.

In the alcove, the red string lay coiled on the brass plate at the foot of the lamp. The flame didn't lean toward anyone.

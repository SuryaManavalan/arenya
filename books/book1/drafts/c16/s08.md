The Camry didn't show up behind her.

Nothing did. That almost bothered Arenya more.

Colma BART was concrete and wind. A man in a hi-vis vest was power-washing gum off the platform, and the spray hung in the cold without bending around anyone. The train came in on time. Kino took the seat facing the doors. Ix folded himself into the shadow under the bench, a low heavy darkness that nobody else's feet found.

Through her jacket, the napkin-wrapped check sat against her ribs like a hand warmer that was starting to run down.

Between Colma and Daly City the warmth thinned.

It didn't go out. One stop wasn't far enough for that.

* * *

Nana's porch light was on, in daylight.

She opened the door in an apron and her reading glasses, and the house behind her smelled of hominy and dried chile. The pozole was already going. She looked at Kino. She looked at Arenya. She looked at the zip pocket.

She didn't ask.

The kitchen table had been cleared to bare wood. Three things sat on it in a row, squared to the edge like a place setting: the red Brooke Bond tin, the saucer of grey cal where the dead dime slept, and a yellow No. 2 pencil, sharpened so recently that the shavings still lay in the saucer beside it. The dresser hadn't moved from the garage door. A thin rime of frost still traced the jamb behind it.

"Siéntense," Nana said, and didn't sit herself. "I talked to your tía all night, mija. In my head. She won." She tapped the pencil once without picking it up. "I haven't said yes. I want to hear what you came to ask me, in your words, before I decide anything. And I want to know what's warm in your pocket."

Arenya sat, because her knee wanted her to. She took the napkin out and set it on the wood, still folded, well away from the pencil.

"It's a guest check from Lupe's. Bell left it. One pencil stroke in the SERVER box." She kept her hands flat, away from it. "He was offering to be my second witness. I said no out loud. I didn't touch it. It's still warm, and I don't like that I want to keep it, and I didn't want to leave it in a diner for someone else to pick up."

She breathed.

"What I came to ask isn't *will you sign.* It's smaller and worse. What does signing cost *you*? Not me. I'm already in the count, and I'm tired of being the only one who's read the whole page. If you say no, I'll eat your pozole and thank you, and we'll go to Livermore and try to un-say it. If you say yes, I want you to say it knowing what Bell does with a hand that's been counted."

She looked at Nana's glasses instead of the pencil. "Aunty wrote *Don't sign.* I think she meant me. I don't think she got to decide for you."

Behind her, Kino wiped his boots twice on the mat, like he was nine. Ix stayed on the porch, a darker patch of shade by the rail.

"Let her say it first, Nana," he said. "It's hers to ask." He pulled out the chair beside Arenya's and didn't sit. "I'm not here to push. Whatever you pick up or leave on that table, I'm standing next to you. Same as I said yesterday." He nodded at the pencil. "That's a good point on it."

Then, quieter, in Spanish, something about the pozole smelling like an answer too, and who was in charge of the order of things. *Tú mandas.*

Nana's mouth twitched.

"Eat first," Arenya said. Her voice came out smaller than she meant. "Kino's right. Nobody should decide this on an empty stomach. And I'd like to watch you cook for ten minutes without thinking about ink."

Kino sat then. He slid the saucer with the dime a hand's width farther from the napkin, so the two cold-and-warm things couldn't talk to each other. Then he got up again, took the ladle off its hook, and held the lid back so the steam wouldn't hide the pot from her.

"Tell me where the bowls are. I'll set three. Four, if Malachi's coming up through the freezer."

He glanced at Arenya. One small nod. *That was a good ask.*

"Comemos," said Nana, like a vote had been called.

On her way to the stove she took the salt box off the counter and poured a small white hill onto the folded napkin without touching it. "So it stops listening while we eat."

The warmth under Arenya's ribs went out like a pilot light.

She was surprised how much she missed it.

* * *

The pozole was red and deep, and the hominy floated in it like moons. There was shredded cabbage, radish coins, a saucer of oregano rubbed between Nana's palms, lime wedges, and a little clay bowl of chile de árbol ground almost to dust. Nana set the chile beside Arenya's bowl and looked at her over her glasses.

She didn't say it. She didn't have to.

Arenya added the chile.

Nana talked with her back half turned, wiping a counter that was already clean.

"You asked what it costs. Bueno. A witness isn't a signature, mija. A signature is *I agree.* A witness is *I was there, and I'll say so again.* The book doesn't keep the one who signs. It keeps the one who'll say it again. Your tía knew that. That's why she asked me and not your papá."

The rag stopped.

"When my name goes on that line, it's written with where I live. Not this address. *Where I live.* Who eats at my table. Who sleeps here. The grey men read the book. Then the mail starts. Never a threat. Offers. Every month, a little paper. *Sign here and we close the account. Sign here and the boy is left alone.* Each one a little worse than the last. Your tía got eleven years of envelopes, mija. Meridian was only the last ones. That's what Bell does with a counted hand."

She turned around.

"He writes to it."

She looked at Kino, not at Arenya. "And you gave your note to the water. You're out. I felt it Tuesday, the cold went off you like a fever breaking." Her voice didn't shake, and Arenya could see what that cost. "If I sign, mijo, you go back in the book. As *who sleeps here.* That's the part I left out. That's what I sat on every Thursday while Susmithaa drank her tea and didn't push me."

The rag went down beside the pencil.

"So it isn't my decision alone. And it was never only hers. You tell me, Kino. Not *I'm standing next to you.* Tell me if you'll go back in."

Arenya put her spoon down.

It was the best thing she'd eaten all week and she'd stopped tasting it.

"Before he answers." She kept her eyes on the bowl. "Eleven years of envelopes. Nobody told me that. I'd have asked differently." She made herself look up. "I can't be the reason you go back in. If the answer's no, it's still an answer. It doesn't make you less. Take as long as you need. I'll be here eating Nana's food."

Kino set his spoon in the bowl. The lime wedge he'd meant to squeeze stayed where it was. For a while he watched the hominy instead of anyone, because that was easier.

"Then let me tell you something first. So you're not deciding on a lie."

He said it in Spanish, low, and he didn't look at Arenya. She caught the shape of it anyway, the way you catch a song through a wall. *Semanas.* *La Señora.* *La espalda.* Weeks, and the Lady, and her back.

Then, in English, so Arenya would hear it too:

"For weeks she's had her back to me. In the dreams. I climb up, I sit where I always sit, and she's turned the other way. I didn't tell you, Nana. I thought if I trained harder she'd turn around." He breathed out through his nose. "She didn't. I know where she's looking now."

He didn't nod at the chair beside him. He didn't have to.

Arenya's face went hot. She kept it still.

"So, yes. I'll go back in. Not because the mountain asked. It didn't. Not to be counted for something. I spent my whole life waiting to be written down somewhere that mattered, and this isn't that, and I know it isn't." His thumb found the birthmark on his forearm and stayed there. "*Who sleeps here.* That's true. I've slept in that back room since I was six. If the envelopes come, they come to both of us. I'll read every one out loud at this table, in daylight, and we'll burn them in the sink. Tía got eleven years of them alone. You won't."

He picked the spoon back up. It was getting cold, and she'd made it.

"That's my yes, Nana. It's mine. It isn't hers. The pencil's still yours."

Nana didn't answer.

She took off her reading glasses, folded them, and set them by the tin. Without them her eyes were wet and very plain.

"La Señora," she said, mostly to herself. "Ay, mijo. Six weeks you carried that and ate my food and said nothing."

She put her hand on the back of his head for one second, the way you'd check a child for fever.

Then she sat down at her own table for the first time since they'd come in.

* * *

She opened the Brooke Bond tin.

Camphor and almond-jasmine came up out of it like someone had opened a door in another house. Arenya's throat did something. She let it.

Nana smoothed the declaration flat with the side of her hand. *S. Sowri*, the loops leaning right. Under it, the empty line, her own name typed beneath.

She picked up the pencil.

She didn't hurry. *Itzel Rosario Martínez.* The lead scratched, the only sound in the kitchen. Then, beside the signature, two small strokes, side by side, the way she'd seen them in the salt in March.

"Dos testigos," she said. "Ella y yo. Ya está."

The kitchen did three things at once.

Behind the dresser, the frost on the garage jamb ticked like a cooling engine and went from white to clear, and ran down to the floor in one thin line.

Under the salt on the napkin, something gave. A soft *tck*. A pencil lead, snapping.

And the dot on Arenya's palm stopped being a dot.

She turned her hand over on the table. Under the frost-scar *12*, beside the rule, there were two short marks now. Grey as graphite. Side by side. Her palm was cool. For the first time in days it was just a hand.

She didn't say anything. The first thing in her throat was a joke, and it was the wrong one.

Nana set the pencil down, point away from all of them, and picked up her spoon.

"Now it's cold," she said of the pozole, and her voice cracked right down the middle. "Eat anyway. Both of you. Then somebody tell me what we do about Livermore, because I'm not finished being afraid, mija. I've only stopped being alone."

Arenya's hand wasn't steady. She ate the cold pozole anyway, with the chile in it, because Nana was watching.

"Thank you," she said. It came out rough. "Both of you."

Across the table, Kino finished his bowl. When the spoon scraped the bottom he reached over and put his hand on Nana's, the one that wasn't holding hers. Nana's fingers shook a little. His didn't. Out on the porch, Ix shifted against the rail, and Arenya felt it through the floorboards: a low, settled weight, like a cat that had stopped watching the door.

* * *

"I'll tell you about Livermore," Arenya said. "But first I want to read every word on that declaration out loud. Once. Here, in daylight. Anyone who stands for me should be able to say it again."

"Read it," said Kino. He pushed his bowl aside and squared his hands on the wood, like a man settling in for a long watch. "Slow. I'll say it back after you. Nana too. That's what a witness does." He looked at Nana. "Abuela, if the room goes cold, or the salt moves, nobody gets up. We finish the line."

Nana slid the page across the bare wood with two fingers, the way she'd pass a recipe card, and kept her other hand under Kino's.

One sheet of cheap bond, typed on a machine with a sticky *e*, corrected in blue ballpoint where the *e* hadn't struck. Afternoon light lay across it. Arenya kept her thumbs out of the margin where the two strokes sat, and held one finger an inch above the paper, and read the first line at the pace of someone crossing a rope.

*I, Susmithaa Sowri, of 41 Hollister Court, South San Francisco, being of sound mind, declare the following before a witness.*

"She asked for it," Kino said back, when she'd gone on. "Not you."

Nobody got up.

*One. On the eighth of March I had an archana performed at the Livermore temple in the name of my niece, Arenya, with her star and her gotra. I asked for it. She did not. She was not present and did not know.*

*Two. The priest who read it was not our priest. I noticed too late. If anything was promised in that room in her name, it was promised by my mouth and not hers, and I take it with me.*

"*Así es,*" Nana murmured, after the fourth line. Twice the pilot light on the stove flickered blue. That was all.

*Three. Neither she nor any of my blood is party to any contract, policy, or account opened beneath Colma or elsewhere in my name or hers.*

*Four. Any envelope addressed to the beneficiary is addressed to no one.*

Arenya said it. Kino said it. Nana said it, last, the vowels leaning.

The Brooke Bond tin gave a small tinny *pank* as its lid settled, the sound a can makes in a cooling car. On Arenya's palm, the two grey strokes darkened. Pencil grey became the grey of a pencil pressed hard.

Then nothing.

The last part was addressed to her.

*Ari — I'm sorry I asked for you without asking you. I wanted the god to know your name before anyone else learned it. Someone else was listening. Don't go back into that room to fix my mistake unless you go with people who will say this again.*

She read it alone. Her voice held until the second word.

*Ari.*

Nobody called her that. She hadn't known her aunt had a name for her she never used.

Nobody repeated that part. It wasn't for the book.

When she finished, the kitchen was quiet enough to hear the pozole tick on the stove.

"*Not our priest,*" Nana said, very softly. "She never told me that either."

Arenya set the page down square to the table's edge. Then she took her coffee-stained notebook out of her bag, opened to a clean page, and wrote, because writing was the only way she stayed upright. *Mar 8. Livermore. Priest not hers. Who?*

She underlined *Who?* twice, hard enough to dent the page beneath.

"So that's who was listening," she said. "Not Bell. Somebody who stood in that room and let her say my name."

"Underline it again," said Kino. "That's what the clerk counts. It isn't the room. It's whoever stood in it."

* * *

"What do two strokes do?" Arenya asked. "Does it close it? Hold it open? Does Bell still collect on the sixteenth day, or did we just make it harder for him to say it's his?"

Nana put her glasses back on, and became somebody who answered questions again.

"Only what Susmithaa told me, and what I've seen. One stroke is a *no* that can be argued. Two is a *no* that stands. On the sixteenth day, when they come to count, your account reads *disputada, dos testigos.* A clerk who never errs doesn't pay out on a dispute." She lifted one shoulder. "Bell can't say you're his. He can wait. They're good at waiting."

She tapped the declaration, just under *not our priest.*

"But this is a different book, mija. Whatever that man heard in Livermore, he heard it in a temple, not in Colma. Our no doesn't reach there. That's why she wrote *don't go alone.*"

Arenya nodded slowly. "Then we don't ask the god anything. We ask the office. Who read the archana, who booked it, who was in the room." She looked up. "Nana, did she pay by check? Cash leaves nothing. A check leaves a name."

"Check," Nana said, as if Arenya had asked whether Susmithaa wore a seatbelt. "Always. She said cash was for people who wanted to forget."

She reached into the open tin, lifted the corner of the tea-paper lining, and drew out a thin stack of yellow duplicate carbons, the kind that tear from the back of a checkbook. She had kept them here with her Thursday things. She thumbed through. PG&E. *Itzel — groceries (she'd argue).* Ravi's birthday.

She stopped.

*No. 2207. Mar 8. $101.00. Pay to: LIVERMORE TEMPLE — archana.* And on the memo line, in that leaning hand: *for Ari.*

"A carbon can't tell you who cashed it," Nana said. "The bank has the back of the original. The endorsement. Whose hand stamped it."

She laid the carbon on top of the notebook, under the twice-underlined *Who?*, and for once she said nothing clever at all.

Kino took it by the edges and read it twice. He photographed it flat on the table with his own phone, front only, then slid it back where Nana had put it. "A hundred and one is a temple number," he said, low, in case the porch was listening. "An offering, not a fee. So the office logs it. We ask for the receipt book, not the priest. Nobody gets cagey about a receipt."

* * *

Things to take. Things to leave.

Arenya left the declaration in the tin, in the cold, to share a shelf with Malachi's mail. She'd keep the photo she'd already taken. The check stayed under the salt; nobody opens it. She tipped a spoonful of salt into a clean sandwich bag from her backpack, zipped it, and pushed it deep in the pocket where the check had ridden.

Then, because she didn't know what else to do with her hands, she washed the three bowls.

"If an envelope comes for the beneficiary, don't open it," she told Nana. "Freezer. Call Kino. He reads it aloud at the table."

Kino dried the last bowl and hung the towel on the oven handle. He took a pinch of salt from the box, twisted it into a scrap of paper towel, and slipped it into Arenya's jacket pocket without asking. He folded the dead dime into its sleeve, away from skin, and left it under the salt line over the sink. Then he laid his palm on the dresser at the garage door.

Wood. Dry. Just wood.

"Keep the tin open on the table till we're back, Nana. Let the house hear the declaration sit there." He kissed the top of her head. "Leave a note in the freezer for Malachi. Thursday, Livermore, daylight, no names. Ix stays with you tonight."

"I need to call Ava before we go," Arenya said. "She doesn't get to find out from Bell that she was my bait."

"Use my phone," said Kino. "Yours is too near the dime. Take the porch."

She took it. Ix didn't move when she sat down beside him, a dark patch against the rail, heavy and warm as a stove. She found Tartine's number in her notebook and dialed, and listened to it ring.

When Ava picked up, Arenya didn't open with a joke.

"Ava, it's Arenya. From yesterday. Is this a bad time? Okay. Then I'm going to say three things and you can hang up after any of them. I used you as bait, and I'm sorry. The man who read your name is real, and he isn't coming back for a while, but I can't promise *a while* is long. And there's a spare bed at a woman's house in Daly City, with soup, for as long as you want it."

She looked at the dark shape beside her.

"You don't owe me an answer today."

Inside, Nana hugged Kino at the door. She hugged Arenya longer. "Call your friend," she said into her hair. "Then go before the fog."

Kino held out Arenya's jacket as she came in, Nana's hug still in his shoulders.

The afternoon had gone to the gold it gets before fog. Behind them, on the bare table, the tin sat open in the light, the declaration inside it, the salt hill on its napkin, and a yellow pencil pointed at nobody.

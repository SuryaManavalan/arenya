The shoes went in the cubbies. Her sneakers, Kino's boots, side by side like a couple at a very small, very strange wedding.

Then cool tile under her socks, and a smell she knew from every Sunday of her childhood: camphor, ghee, floor cleaner. Somewhere deeper in the building a nadaswaram played on a loop, turned down low, a reed instrument sounding politely unbothered.

The office was narrow. One desk. One ceiling fan pushing warm air from one side of the room to the other. A calendar from a Fremont jeweler. A laminated price list: *Name + Nakshatra + Gotra: $21. Special (with Sahasranama): $51. Family Sankalpam, full: $101.* Beneath it, a strip of tape that said **RECEIPTS ISSUED ONLY FROM OFFICE BOOK.**

Behind the desk, binders. 2022, 2023, 2024, 2025. And a fat one marked **2026 — JAN–JUN**, its colored tabs bristling like feathers.

Her palm stayed cool.

Kino took the door. He didn't say he was taking it. He just put his shoulder to the frame, where he could see the desk, the fan, the shelf, the steps behind them, and Arenya understood she'd been handed the room's back.

"Aquí nadie cuenta todavía," he said, to no one. She didn't need the translation. *Nobody's counting here yet.*

*Yet.*

The woman behind the desk looked up over half-moon glasses. Seventy, maybe. Gold nose stud, a pen on a beaded chain, a blue cardigan. The nameplate said **Mrs. K. Ramaswamy, Volunteer Office Secretary.**

"Good morning, ma. Archana? Next one is ten-thirty." Her eyes went to Kino, to the work jacket and the road on him, and came back. "Or donation?"

Arenya crossed to the desk with her notebook closed against her hip and her palm turned in. She made her voice small and polite, the voice of somebody's daughter.

"Namaste, Aunty. Sorry to bother you. I'm looking for a receipt. An archana from March eighth, in the name of Ari. My aunt paid, and she passed away, and the family is sorting her papers." She set the carbon on the desk, one finger on the corner. "Number 2207. A hundred and one dollars. I don't need to take anything. I just want to know who wrote it, and who read it."

She didn't say her full name. Under her breath, to nobody: *Don't sign. Don't verify.*

Mrs. Ramaswamy rolled her chair to the shelf without standing, a move she'd made ten thousand times, and pulled down the binder. Pink carbon stubs flipped under her thumb, rubber-banded by week.

"Sorry about your aunty, ma. Om shanti." She slid off a band and turned the book around.

Stub No. 2207.

*S. Sowri*, on the donor line, in blue ballpoint. Arenya knew that hand from the tea tin. On the *Archana for* line, Susmithaa had written the whole name, every syllable, with the gotra and nakshatra beneath. Everything a priest needed to say a person out loud to a god.

Arenya read it without touching it. Her throat did something. She let it.

In the corner, a different hand had initialed it: *K.R. 3/19.*

"That's me," Mrs. Ramaswamy said. "Funny. Now I remember. A gentleman came for this same one. Nice-spoken, grey suit. Insurance. Said the policy needed proof of the family's religious *expenses*, can you imagine." She shrugged. "I photocopied it for him. He paid ten cents, in a coin. Old coin. I put it in the hundi." The aunty-gaze came back, kind and immovable. "But this one I can't just show, ma. Privacy. You are the Arenya on here? Show me any ID, I'll make you a copy also."

A copy. Another place her name would sit.

"Aunty, you've been so kind, and I'm not going to lie to you." Arenya kept her hands flat on her own side of the desk. "I'd rather not put my ID on your copier. Somebody has been collecting my family's papers. Can I ask you something instead? The gentleman from the insurance. Did he sign anything?"

At the door, Kino said, low, in the voice he used for animals, "Don't look at the clock."

She didn't. She'd noticed it anyway, the way you notice a person staring at you on a train. A plain wall clock above the binders. Its second hand wasn't stuck. It ticked, and on every tick it landed a hair short of the mark, then corrected, as if something had to count it before it was allowed to move.

"Every tick it waits," Kino said. "Somebody in here is being counted, and I don't think it's the clock." Then, to Mrs. Ramaswamy, easy as a man at a trailhead: "Aunty, that clock. Has it always run like that? Or only since March?"

Mrs. Ramaswamy took her glasses off and let them hang. For a moment she wasn't a volunteer at all. She was somebody's grandmother, hearing something she'd half known.

"Your aunty said the same," she said quietly. "'Cash, Aunty, and no copies.' I thought she was being careful about the government." She opened the drawer and took out a spiral register. **COPY REQUESTS.** Three entries all year. The March 19 line read *Stub 2207 — 1 copy — $0.10*, and beside it, in fountain-pen ink, upright and perfectly even: ***D. Hale.***

A cream business card was paper-clipped to the page. *Meridian Mutual · Beneficiary Services.* No phone number.

"He didn't ask for Sastri garu. He asked *who reads them*, and how often." Her mouth tightened. "I told him. The full sankalpam is read out every Friday at the abhishekam. For one year from payment. Your aunty paid for the year."

Every Friday until next March, Arenya's whole name went up to a god in a room that somebody had photocopied.

Mrs. Ramaswamy counted on her fingers. "The clock. Sastri garu keeps saying it's slow. We changed the battery. Twice. Since... March. End of March."

On the next tick the second hand stopped a full breath short. It waited longer than before, as though whatever was counting had to look twice. Arenya's palm-dot pressed down, light and exact, like a pen held just above paper.

She closed her fingers over it.

"Thank you, Aunty. Truly. I'm family, and I have the original carbon, in her hand. I'd like the sankalpam stopped before tomorrow. Not read, not changed. Stopped. And without my name on a new page. Could you strike it with your own pen and write that it was cancelled by family, carbon shown? I won't sign anything." A beat. "And may I keep Mr. Hale's card? It's the only thing in this room I'd be glad to take."

Kino stepped half a pace closer. When the second hand stalled, he opened his fist and let a few grains of salt fall along the baseboard under the clock, quiet as Nana laying down the tea tin. "Say it out loud while you write, ma'am. So it's you writing it. Not us. And if the clock slows while you write, don't stop. Finish the line."

Mrs. Ramaswamy looked at the carbon. At Arenya. She was plainly deciding whether this was a rule she was allowed to bend.

She slid Hale's card across the desk without a word, the way you hand a child something hot.

Then she uncapped the pen on her chain and said, clearly, "Cancelling. By family." One firm line through every syllable Susmithaa had written. Underneath, in print: ***CANCELLED — BY FAMILY, ORIG. CARBON SHOWN. K.R.*** She added the date.

The fan kept turning. The second hand came up to the mark and stopped. It didn't correct. The room went so quiet Arenya heard the nadaswaram's loop catch and start again.

Her dot pressed once, hard, like a nib set down.

Then the pressure lifted. Cool skin. Nothing.

"There." Mrs. Ramaswamy capped the pen, pleased and a little shaken. "Now the office won't read it. But ma, I must tell you properly. A sankalpam is a promise made *to* Him, not to me. My pen stops the list. It doesn't close the promise. For that Sastri garu does a small visarjana. He says the name one last time and tells Him it's finished. Five minutes. After twelve."

The name. Out loud. In a room Hale had a photocopy of.

Arenya tucked Hale's card into her notebook, face down. "Can it be done without my name being said in the hall? Whispered at the sanctum, door shut? Earlier than twelve?"

Kino crouched by the baseboard. The salt had been counted into a row, evenly spaced. He didn't touch it. "It's waiting for the name, not the closing," he said. "Aunty, can the closing use the receipt? The stub number and *family*, the way you struck it?"

Mrs. Ramaswamy opened her mouth to say *that's not how it's done*.

"Who told you it needs the name?"

The man in the doorway was small and old, bare-chested under a folded angavastram, his feet wet, a steel tumbler of water in his hand. He looked at the struck stub, at the salt, and for a long moment at the clock.

"The name is for the people in the hall, so they know whose function it is. *He* already knows who she is." He set the tumbler down. "Star, gotra, her own hand on the plate. That is enough for a closing. Inside, door pulled, nobody at the rail. Now, before twelve. Twelve is when I'm hungry, not when God is."

"Sastri garu," Mrs. Ramaswamy said faintly, "on March eighth you were—"

"In Fremont. My knee." His mouth went thin. "The visiting man signed my register. Very neat hand." He didn't say more. He didn't like it. Then he turned to Arenya. "One thing, child. If I don't say it, *you* don't say it either. Not to Him, not to yourself, not even in your head. You stand there as the one the stub was for, and you keep your mouth shut until I pour the water. Can you do that?"

A reporter's question rose in her, *who was the visiting man, what did the register say*. She swallowed it. After.

"Yes, Sastri garu. It's the thing I'm worst at. But I can do it."

She told Mrs. Ramaswamy not to let anyone touch the March page, and told Kino, "Hold the door. Watch the salt. If anything counts, cough."

"I'll hold it." He gave Sastri the small nod he'd give an elder on a trail. "Gracias, señor."

* * *

The sanctum was small and warm and smelled of old ghee. Sastri pulled the wooden door to, leaving a hand's width, and set a brass plate under her fingers. Then he began, low and fast, the way men chant who have done it for fifty years.

Her star was in it. Her gotra was in it. Where the name should go he said *ayam*. *This one.* And tapped the plate once.

The lamp flame lay flat, as if a door had opened somewhere it shouldn't.

In the hall, Kino heard a phone buzz on the shoe bench.

It was a cheap flip phone that hadn't been there when they came in. Face up, already connected, a call timer running. 0:12. Under the clock, one grain of salt rolled out of the counted row and sat alone to the right, like a tally carried over.

A voice came out of it, small and tinny, and unmistakable.

*"Ari? Kanna, are you there? Say something, just say it's you—"*

Amma.

Through the gap, Arenya heard it. Everything in her lunged toward it. Her mouth opened.

She bit the inside of her cheek, hard. Copper. She kept her fingers flat on the brass.

*Poodle. Curly. Stupid. Groomed into a ridiculous pom-pom.*

Sastri didn't stop chanting. His eyes flicked to her once. The kalasam was in his hand and the water was not yet poured.

*Amma doesn't call at nine at night. Amma texts. Amma says "kanna" in the other order.*

She held that thought like a rail. Her lips went white. She made no sound.

* * *

Kino said nothing. Not her name, not *Amma*, nothing the phone could keep.

Two steps and he was at the bench. He didn't touch the loose grain. He lifted the phone by its edges, the way he'd lift a dead bird, and folded it shut over the voice, then pressed it flat against his chest with his other palm over it. He planted himself in the doorway with his back to the sanctum, so the sound would have to go through him to reach her.

Then he coughed, once, hard and clear. Not a warning. A hand held out through a wall. *I'm here. That's not your mother.*

* * *

Sastri lifted the kalasam and poured. Water ran over the brass and over her fingers, and he said *samāptam*, flat as a man closing a shop. *It is finished.*

In the office the second hand ticked past the mark. An ordinary tick. The salt on the baseboard slumped into a loose, careless heap, the way salt is supposed to lie.

Against Kino's chest the phone went dead. It didn't hang up. The battery was simply flat, cold as a stone from a creek. When he took it away the cold stayed, a rectangle of numb skin over his sternum the exact size of the phone. Nothing pulled. Something had been said into him that was meant for her, and his body had kept it.

The last screen was burned faintly into the cheap LCD. *CALL ENDED 0:41.* And a number: **650-755-xxxx.**

Sastri dried his hands. He gave Arenya a flower and a pinch of kumkum and finally let her talk. "Done. Whatever was promised in your name here is closed." Then, gruffly, past her shoulder: "Your friend took something for you. Feed him."

She pressed the kumkum to her forehead with a thumb that wasn't steady. Her palm was cool and quiet. She looked at it a beat longer than she meant to.

Then she was out the door.

"Show me." She didn't ask twice. She held two fingers above the numb patch, not touching, the way you'd check a stove. "Does it hurt? Can you feel your heart under it?"

"Cold, not pain." Kino rubbed two knuckles over his shirt, so she could see it didn't bother him. "I can feel it. Slow. Same cold as that freezer."

She took the phone by its edges and read the number off the screen. She knew it. It was on Rodel's card. *Colma Notary & Mail.* She didn't say it aloud.

"You took that for me." Her voice came out rougher than she wanted. "We're eating, right after this. That's an order from a priest."

"Colma," Kino said, wrapping the phone in his bandana. "Somebody wanted that call to land. And wanted us to know where it came from. Or wanted us to think we did."

* * *

Mrs. Ramaswamy already had the register open to March 8, her finger beneath the visiting priest's signature.

The page was mostly her own round hand. *Sahasranamam, Gupta family. Abhishekam, $51.* Halfway down, the handwriting changed, and nobody would have missed it.

**No. 2207 · $101 · Archana · "Ari."** The star, the gotra, both correct, both Susmithaa's. Under PRIEST: *Visiting — Pt. H. Dalal, Fremont.*

The signature was a single straight line with a small hook at each end, like a bracket lying on its side.

Under CONTACT he'd written **650-755-** and the rest of Rodel's number. Then an address. **c/o Colma Notary & Mail, Box 112.**

Arenya knew that box. Hale had rented it on March 19.

Eleven days *after* this page was written.

She took out her notebook and started to copy the signature. The pen dragged, as if the paper had a grain. The line wanted to come out perfect. Her palm prickled once. No pull. A reminder. *A line drawn in that hand is a line drawn in that hand.*

She was one hook in, the long stroke beginning to run on, smooth and eager.

She lifted the pen.

"No. I'm not drawing it." She set it down and took out her phone instead. Three frames: the full page, flat and square, with the clock's face in the corner of one so the time was on record. The signature. The contact line and the Box 112 address.

"Checks," she said. "Does anyone cash donation checks here? I need to know whose hand wrote the endorsement on 2207."

"Checks we never cash. Bank of America, every Monday. I keep the slips." Mrs. Ramaswamy flipped to a clipped deposit book and ran a finger down the column. She frowned. "2207 is not here. Ah. Yes. He asked for it himself. As his *dakshina*. He said the family wanted it so, and I gave it." She looked embarrassed. "So the back side, the signing, that is in the family's bank. Her statement will have the picture."

Kino stood with one shoulder to the wall, where he could see the register and the front door at once. "Who has keys, ma'am? And the visiting man, did anybody see how he came or left?"

"Me. Sastri garu. The board president, Mr. Iyer. Nobody else." She hesitated. "That man, I never saw a car. I came back from the kitchen and he was standing in the hall. Very polite. Very nice teeth." Her eyes went to the shoe bench, then to the floor by the sanctum door. "He never took his shoes off. All afternoon he walked in shoes, and nobody told him. Why did nobody tell him?"

Arenya photographed the deposit column, the line that read *2207 — given to priest as dakshina, per family. —K.R.*

"You did nothing wrong," she said gently. "He didn't take his shoes off, and nobody told him, because he made you forget to. That's what he does." She wasn't sure it comforted. It was true.

Then, to Kino, quietly: "Box 112 was rented on the nineteenth. Hale wrote an address that didn't exist yet. Either he knew he'd rent it, or somebody wrote this after the fact."

"Eleven days." Kino was looking at the hooked line. "Either he sees forward or he writes backward. Bell's kind of ledger doesn't care which way time runs. Only that the column adds up." He shook his head. "And no shoes off in a temple. That's not manners. He doesn't think he's a guest, so none of the house rules bind him."

"The bank has the back of the check. Amma has Aunty's accounts." Arenya closed the notebook. "I'll ask her tonight. Not on the phone. In person, or through Appa."

She squeezed Mrs. Ramaswamy's hand once. "Keep that page covered? And if a man in nice shoes comes asking, you haven't seen me."

Mrs. Ramaswamy laid a folded dhoti cloth over the March page like a sheet over a sleeper. Kino tore a corner from his ranger notepad, wrote the station line and his cell, and slid it under the brass paperweight. "Salt on the sill, ma'am. A window cracked. If he comes back, don't say anything to him. Don't hand him anything. Call this."

"Nice shoes, I will not see," Mrs. Ramaswamy said. Her voice was firmer than her hands.

* * *

By the sanctum door, Kino found no print. He found a line.

Down the hall, in every third grout seam, ran a faint grey hairline. The same dead grey as the ring in Nana's cal. Evenly spaced, each the length of one even stride. They stopped at the sanctum threshold.

He'd come this far and no farther. Whatever Sastri's lamp was, he hadn't walked past it.

In Arenya's notebook, the half-drawn bracket sat alone on the page. It was only ink. It didn't move. It didn't finish itself. But when she closed the cover, the notebook felt a fraction heavier than it should, like a book with a pressed flower in it.

She put it in her bag anyway. *Great,* she thought. *Carrying a grudge, literally.*

Outside, the hills were bright and dry. On the far ridge the turbines all turned in step. Sastri stood in the temple doorway with his arms folded until their car was out of the lot.

Twenty minutes down the road, a dosa place sat in a strip mall off First Street. Ordinary. Loud. It smelled of fermented batter and coffee, and nobody in it was counting anything at all.

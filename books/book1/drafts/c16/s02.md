Highway 1 ran south with the fog sitting offshore like a wall nobody had built.

Nana drove slow and boring. Nobody followed them that Arenya could see. Twice she caught herself checking the side mirror for beige anyway.

The Pacifica pier went a quarter mile straight out into the grey Pacific, concrete and railings, and smelled of bait and diesel and kelp. At four-thirty on a Wednesday it belonged to the regulars. Crabbers with their rings and orange buckets. A woman in a puffy coat feeding the gulls something she shouldn't. An old Filipino man with three rods and a radio playing the Giants pregame.

The wind came straight off the water and went through every layer she owned.

Nana stopped at the foot of the pier and sat down on a bench with her purse in her lap.

"*Your* letter," she told Kino. "I watch. I don't carry."

So he went. Ix went with him, a low dark shape padding along the railing, and the crabbers stepped around it without knowing why they did.

Arenya followed a half step behind. She stayed upwind of the radio and kept her eyes on the planks.

Crabbers. Puffy coat. A kid with a bucket.

No grey cardigans.

She counted them anyway. She hated that she did.

Halfway out, the old man's radio fizzed and lost the game for a second. Then it came back.

Kino didn't look up.

* * *

He stopped at the end, where the railing was lowest and the swell was running hardest out.

He took the page from inside his jacket. It was the one he'd written in Nana's kitchen, pulled out of the salted bag with salad tongs, frozen stiff and now gone soft and damp again. He didn't unfold it. He didn't read it aloud.

That was the whole point.

"Nana didn't fail," he said to the water. Spanish first, then English. "They knew. That's the only thing I'm sending."

He folded it once more, small, and held it flat on his palm over the rail so the wind couldn't have it before the tide did. Then he turned his hand over.

Not thrown. Given.

The page didn't float the way paper should. It lay flat on the green water for one heartbeat, as if it were being read. Then the swell folded over it and took it under the pilings, no fuss, no frost, no gull.

Gone. The way a letter goes when it's posted.

Arenya didn't say anything profound. She put two fingers on his sleeve, over the cold forearm, for about a second, and took them away.

"Okay," she said, dry and quiet. "That's one of us out of the count. Bronze medal for the rest."

Kino looked at his arm.

She saw it on his face before he said anything. He pushed the sleeve back and turned his wrist to the grey light. The birthmark was still there, the Lady's thumbprint. Still cool.

But where her fingers had been, he was warm. Plain skin-warm. And it wasn't fading back.

"It's just a birthmark," he said. He sounded like he didn't trust it. "Nothing's pulling."

"Good," she said, and her throat did something stupid. She tucked her right palm into her sleeve so the 12 stayed out of the wind. "Thank you for not asking me to say anything. I don't have anything yet. But I'm not signing, and I'm not looking at that phone until Malachi calls. Let's walk back slow."

Behind them the crabbers' lines ticked against the rail. The kid's bucket held one furious red rock crab. On the old man's radio, a man read the tide table in a flat, pleasant voice.

*Low water four-fifty, minus point three, Sharp Park.*

It was the most normal sentence she'd heard all week.

* * *

Halfway back she saw Nana's bench.

Nana wasn't alone on it.

A man sat at the far end, a respectful foot of slats between them. Good navy overcoat over a suit, no tie. A paper coffee cup from the bait shop. Fortyish, clean-shaven, with a forgettable, pleasant face. He was angled toward the water instead of toward Nana, like a stranger making small talk about the weather.

Nana's purse was in her lap with both hands on the clasp. She wasn't looking at him. She was looking straight down the pier at the two of them.

She lifted two fingers off the clasp. Barely. Then put them back.

Arenya's stomach dropped through the planks.

The man saw where Nana was looking. He turned, found Arenya's face, and smiled. An apologetic smile. *Sorry I missed you at noon.* He didn't stand and he didn't wave. He set his coffee down on the slats and waited, as if he had all the time in the world and had already budgeted it.

Kino didn't speed up. Ix was ahead of them already, low along the rail. He closed the gap until he was at her shoulder, a half step forward on the side nearest the bench, hands out of his pockets and loose.

"That's Hale," he said, barely moving his mouth. "Nana's two fingers. Same as the honks. Don't say your name. Don't take anything he hands you. Not a cup, not a card, not a pen."

Then, lower: "Your call. I can walk up first and be the boring cousin. Or we turn around and he chases us down a quarter mile of pier in front of the crabbers. I'd rather he talk where there are witnesses."

*Guv.*

It arrived the way it always did, close and slightly too loud, a voice laid against the inside of her ear.

*Brass. Same as Box 112, only fresher, like a stamp still wet. That's him, that's the voicemails, I'd bet my coat on it.*

She kept walking. Same pace.

*Two more things, love. He smells of nothing. Sat by the sea an hour and it never touched him. And his watch is ticking slow on purpose, few seconds behind. Man who's never late because he's never* on *the time everyone else is on. Don't let him set the pace.*

A beat.

*He said he don't lie, it's bad for business. Funny. Susmithaa said* he's lying*, and I still don't know which "he."*

*Ask narrow questions. Whole sentences. On the record, in front of the crabbers. And don't say your name, don't take the envelope, don't thank him. Thanking's agreeing.*

*I'm right here. Thinner than I'd like, but here.*

"Witnesses," she murmured. "Yes."

She kept her hands in her pockets, palm closed. "We go to Nana. Together. No one ahead, no one behind. I'm not hiding from a man with a gas-station coffee." And, under her breath: "If he uses my name, I don't answer to it. And nobody writes anything down."

They stopped two steps short of the bench. Close enough for Nana to touch. Far enough that Arenya wasn't inside his foot of slats.

She gave him the flat, polite face she used on telemarketers.

"You're sitting with my grandmother's friend," she said. "That's the first thing I don't like. Who are you, and why are you on this pier?"

* * *

He didn't get up. He nodded, a little rueful, as if she'd made a fair point at a meeting.

"Daniel Hale. Meridian Mutual." No card. No hand. Both of his stayed on his knees, where Kino could see them. "And you're right, I shouldn't have sat here. Mrs. Lucero was kind enough not to tell me to leave."

Nana's knuckles went white on the clasp.

She hadn't told him her name.

"I'm here because you didn't come at noon, and I understood why. A library is my room. This isn't anyone's." He tipped his head at the water, the buckets, the kid. "Witnesses. Your friend's idea, I think. It's a good one."

His eyes went to Kino. No hostility in them at all. "And congratulations. That was well done, out at the end. Posted, not thrown. Your matter's closed, as far as anyone I work with is concerned. I mean that."

Then back to Arenya, and his voice dropped into the soft register of someone delivering bad news at a kitchen table.

"Your aunt had an arrangement she disputed, Ms. Sowri. She was entitled to. She did it very properly, in Colma, in writing. But a disputed entry doesn't stay open forever, and it isn't my decision how it closes." He let that sit for one swell. "There's a gentleman in a grey cardigan who filed paperwork about you this morning. He's less patient than I am, and he doesn't sit on benches. I'd like very much to settle this with you before he settles it *for* you."

He didn't reach into his coat. But there was a flat rectangle in the inside pocket, the size of a cream envelope, and the coat hung a little heavy on that side.

"I'm not going to hand you anything. I'm not allowed to. Whenever you want it, it's yours to take." He picked up the coffee. "Ask me whatever you like. I don't lie. It's bad for business."

Kino stepped between the bench and the rail, so Nana had a wall of him on one side and Ix pressing the other.

"You know the word *posted*," he said. Flat. "You know my grandmother's name, and nobody gave it to you. That's two things you shouldn't know, said politely. Polite isn't honest."

He tilted his head at the water. "You say you don't lie. Then answer one thing straight. Whose hand does that envelope go into if she doesn't take it? Who's the gentleman, and what does he do with a closed entry?"

Behind him, Ix breathed out slow through its nose. Kino let it be heard.

Arenya waited one swell. Then she added her own, evenly, the way she'd read a quote back to a source.

"Narrow, then. Whole sentences. You said 'a disputed entry.' Who entered it, and by whose authority? Name the person or the office. Second: the March nineteenth box. Did Meridian Mutual Services rent Box 112 one hour after my aunt's declaration was notarized, yes or no? Third: you said it isn't your decision how it closes. Whose is it?"

She didn't look at the pocket. She looked at the watch, then at his face.

"And before you answer. I'm not taking anything, I'm not agreeing to anything, and nothing you say gets thanked. I'm just listening. These people are, too."

* * *

Hale listened to all of it without interrupting.

When she finished he gave a small nod of real professional approval. The look of a man who rarely got asked good ones.

"In order, then." He set the coffee down. "Who entered it: your aunt did. Sunday, March eighth, the temple in Livermore. She had a priest say your name and your birth star aloud and ask for you to be *kept*."

*Kept.*

"She meant protected," Hale said, gently. "In the book it's the same word."

Arenya felt that one go in and stay.

"Nobody makes the entries, Ms. Sowri. The clerk you met in Colma writes down what's true. I only read it. That's my authority. I'm a reader with a briefcase.

"The box: yes. Fifty-nine minutes after her declaration. I wanted to be close to the record. I'm not ashamed of that.

"Whose decision: no one's, which is the problem. Two strokes keep a dispute open. One stroke runs out. Hers runs out on her sixteenth day, when your family finishes mourning her and lets her go. Then the entry closes as written, and whoever has standing collects."

His eyes slid to Kino. "Which answers you. The gentleman is Mr. Bell. He filed for *verification of beneficiary* this morning. If the notary gives him that journal, he doesn't need her to say her name. He can verify her himself."

He glanced down the pier at the kid with the crab bucket and lowered his voice. "What he does with a closed entry, I won't describe with a child twenty feet away. I watched him do it in March."

Nobody breathed.

"If nobody takes the envelope, it waits in the box. I close it Friday, as I always do. Envelopes are patient." He touched the heavy side of his coat, lightly, once. "The one I'm carrying isn't the one in the box. That one's a copy, so you'd know I was serious. This one closes the entry *in your favor*. You receive. Mr. Bell has nothing to collect."

He looked at her closed hand. Then her face.

"I've answered everything straight. I'd like to ask one thing back, and you're free not to answer. Do you know what your aunt was carrying? Because I don't think you do. And I think it matters to you more than it matters to me."

The tide voice on the radio said *four-forty-one*.

Hale's watch, a few seconds behind, ticked over after it.

Kino spoke before she could. Low, to Hale, not turning his head.

"Sixteenth day. You just told us the date and what Bell does with it. Thank you for the straight answer."

Arenya's whole body went still.

He kept going, colder. "'In your favor' is still a letter with her name on it, and you already said you can't hand it over. Nobody here is taking a gift from the man who rented the box."

Then, to her, in Spanish, almost nothing: "No contestes lo de su tía. Es cebo."

*Don't answer about your aunt. It's bait.*

She didn't need the translation. She'd already decided.

She took out the coffee-stained notebook and uncapped a pen. Not to write his name. To let him watch her not write it.

"You asked what my aunt was carrying. No. I'm not answering that and I'm not trading. You asked, I declined, that's the whole exchange." She clicked the pen. "Here's what I did hear, in front of witnesses. Sixteen days. A book that writes down what's true. A man who reads it, a man who collects from it, and a closed entry that 'receives' me whether I agree or not. I don't agree. I'd like that on your record, since you're so fond of records."

She looked at the watch, then up.

"One more, narrow. If I never take your envelope, never sign, and Bell never gets that journal. Does the entry still close on day sixteen, yes or no? And is there any way it stays open that doesn't involve somebody standing for me?"

She put one hand lightly on Nana's shoulder. She did not wait for the thanks he was fishing for.

"You're welcome," Hale said, pleasantly, to Kino.

As if it were nothing.

On the old man's radio the tide voice skipped one word, like a needle, and went on.

Kino's jaw flexed. Just once.

Hale had watched her pen not write. Something like regret crossed his face. It was either real or very well rehearsed.

"Noted," he said. "You don't agree. I'll carry that." He didn't write it down. He didn't need to.

"Yes. If nobody takes anything, signs anything or stands for you, it closes on the sixteenth day. Not taking the envelope keeps it from closing in your favor. That's all it does." He looked into the coffee and didn't drink. "And yes, there's one other way, though I've never seen it done. What your aunt did, she did out loud, in a particular room, through a particular man. Something said in a room can sometimes be *unsaid* in the same room. I wouldn't know how. I don't go into temples."

His eyes found hers.

"I'd be careful who you ask there. Rooms where names get said aloud are popular with more than one kind of listener."

He stood. Buttoned the navy coat over the heavy pocket. Left the paper cup on the slats by Nana's hip.

"Mrs. Lucero. Ms. Sowri. Sir." The same small nod, the same depth, for all three. "The box is open until Friday, seven-forty. After that I'm only a phone number, and you know which one."

He walked up the pier toward the lot at an unhurried pace, a little out of step with the surf.

Nobody turned to watch him go but the four of them and the low dark shape at Kino's knee.

Nana let out a breath she'd been holding since before they got there. She looked at the abandoned cup like it was a snake.

"I didn't tell him my name," she said, very quietly. "Mija. I never told him my name."

* * *

Kino picked the cup up by its rim with two fingers, like something dead, and set it in the nearest trash can, far from Nana. He didn't smell it. He didn't read the sleeve.

Then he crouched in front of the bench so his eyes were level with hers and took her purse hand in his.

"No le diste tu nombre, Nana. Él ya lo tenía. Eso es otra cosa." *You didn't give it. He already had it. That's different.* "Nobody here signed anything. Walk with me to the car."

Arenya crouched on Nana's other side and took her free hand in both of hers. The warm one. Not the one with the numbers on it.

"Nana. Look at me. He's a man who reads books. He read it in one." She kept her voice level and low. "It's not your fault, and it's not a door you missed. We're going to the car, we're going slow, and we're not stopping for anything on the way."

Kino spoke over Nana's head, quieter, still not letting go.

"I said thank you to him. Reflex." His eyes were on the planks. "I felt it get written down. Right on my arm. It was small, like a receipt coming off a pad. I'm telling you so it's on the table. Next time I say nothing at all."

She looked at the arm. At him.

"Thank you for telling me," she said. "It's on the table. Don't carry it alone, and don't beat yourself up. It was small. We'll check it in the car."

Then she flipped the notebook open and, under the wind, wrote one line in her own handwriting.

*Temple. Livermore. Said aloud in a room → unsaid in the same room? Who else listens there?*

She closed it before the ink had finished drying.

"Livermore's next," she said. "But not tonight. Tonight we get Nana home, and nobody goes out any door that isn't the front."

Nana let herself be lifted by both hands. On the long walk up the pier she held Kino's arm, the warm one, and said nothing until the planks turned to asphalt.

Then, mostly to herself: "Sixteen days. I'll make the pozole for the sixteenth. She told me to."

In the lot, Ix circled once and came back unsatisfied. Hale's footprints were there on the damp asphalt, faint and perfect, each one exactly the same distance from the last, like marks on a ruler.

They stopped at an empty parking space. No oil stain. No tire marks.

As if no car had ever been parked in it.

Arenya looked at it for a long moment. She did not take out the notebook.

The Corolla started on the first try. Behind them, out past the end of the pier, the tide bottomed out at four-fifty, minus point three, and turned.
